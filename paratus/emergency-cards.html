Circuit words used on every card
Stop ultrafiltration (UF)
Fluid removal stops. Blood keeps flowing through the dialyzer.
Stop the blood pump
Blood stops moving. The patient is still connected.
Clamp
Close the line clamps (and needle or catheter clamps) so nothing moves in or out.
Return the blood
Rinse the blood in the circuit back to the patient with saline. Each card says whether to.
Disconnect
Separate the lines from the needles or catheter, which stay in place as access.
End the treatment
The session is over for today. A restart needs a physician’s order.
Emergency quick card 1

Validate against your own unit protocols

Code during hemodialysis

Unresponsive, not breathing normally (gasping counts), no pulse in 10 seconds.

  1. 1
    Call the code. Crash cart, defibrillator, oxygen. Note the time.
  2. 2
    Chair flat, compress now. 100–120 a minute, 5–6 cm, full recoil; 30:2 with a bag-valve-mask.
  3. 3
    Pads on; shock a shockable rhythm. Hands off the patient, lines and machine, then compress again at once.
  4. 4
    Stop fluid removal. Air, hemolysis or a dialyzer reaction? Clamp, do not return the blood. Otherwise return it quickly, or clamp and disconnect.
  5. 5
    Keep the needles or catheter in for drugs. Epinephrine 1 mg every 3–5 minutes.
  6. 6
    Think potassium. Calcium gluconate, then insulin with dextrose, per protocol.
  7. 7
    Roles by name, plus a watcher for the other patients. Call the attending.
Drugs, other causes, roles and after a pulse returns
  • Compressions never wait for the machine. Pauses under 10 seconds; swap compressors every 2 minutes. One dialysis nurse manages the circuit while compressions continue: stop ultrafiltration (UF), then return the blood with saline or clamp and disconnect, leaving the access in place for intravenous (IV) drugs. A saline bolus through the circuit treats hypovolemia from UF. Use a defibrillator or automated external defibrillator (AED); pads go clear of the access and any wet area.
  • Drugs (American Heart Association (AHA) adult advanced life support, as in the site’s dialysis code guide): epinephrine 1 mg IV every 3–5 minutes, as soon as possible in asystole or pulseless electrical activity (PEA), after the 2nd shock in ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VF/pVT). Amiodarone 300 mg, then 150 mg, for VF/pVT that persists after shocks and epinephrine.
  • Suspected hyperkalemia (missed session, after the long weekend gap, a short or poor session): give intravenous calcium, then insulin with glucose, as the unit’s order set directs, and check glucose afterward. Never give calcium and bicarbonate in the same line. Guidelines differ on these drugs during arrest: the ERC recommends calcium and bicarbonate for hyperkalemic arrest, while the AHA 2025 guideline calls their effectiveness in arrest uncertain; your unit protocol decides. Emergency dialysis is the only real potassium removal: plan it early with the attending (Card 10).
  • Other causes common on dialysis: hypovolemia from UF (saline bolus through the circuit), hypoglycemia, bleeding from a dislodged needle (lift the blanket; Card 4), air embolism (Card 5), a dialyzer reaction (Card 7).
  • Roles: leader · compressions (swap every 2 minutes) · airway · defibrillator and drugs · circuit nurse · recorder and timer · watcher of the other patients · runner to call the attending nephrologist and the ambulance or code team.
  • After a pulse returns: do not restart dialysis unless the attending says so. 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), potassium, glucose, blood gas; arrange transfer. Hand off time on the machine, fluid removed, last potassium, the access, and whether the blood was returned. Tell the family, document, debrief.

Emergency quick card 2

Validate against your own unit protocols

Blood leak alarm

The machine reports blood in the used dialysate. Treat it as real until it is proven false.

  1. 1
    Patient first. Awake? Pressure, pulse, chills, chest or back pain?
  2. !
    Blood in the lines translucent or “port-wine”? Hemolysis, not a leak: stop, clamp, do not return it, call now (Card 6).
  3. 2
    Check the dialysate outflow line for a pink tinge.
  4. 3
    Test the used dialysate with a blood test strip, per protocol.
  5. 4
    Positive: stop on this dialyzer. Pump off, clamp. No rinse-back by habit: only if protocol and the attending say so.
  6. 5
    Negative: reset once. Alarms again: call the technician. Never bypass the detector.
  7. 6
    Call the attending for any confirmed leak. Keep the dialyzer, record the lot, watch for fever.
Why, the machine, false alarms and reporting
  • The machine: most stop ultrafiltration (UF) and send the dialysate to bypass on a blood leak alarm; some also stop the blood pump. Know your models.
  • Why no automatic rinse-back: a torn membrane lets blood out into the dialysate, and non-sterile dialysate may reach the blood. Practice on returning the blood differs with the size of the leak and rests on custom, not trial evidence; if unsure, keep it clamped until you have asked.
  • Hemolysis is a different emergency: blood in the lines looks translucent, cherry-red or “port-wine”, often with chest, back or belly pain, and the circuit blood carries released potassium. Call the physician at once. Check every patient on the same water and concentrate supply, and keep the circuit and dialysate for investigation.
  • Where to look and test: the dialysate outflow line runs from the dialyzer to the machine; take the sample from that side. False alarms come from air bubbles in the dialysate or a dirty or fogged detector. A detector that cannot be made to pass is a machine out of service, not a sensor to work around.
  • After a confirmed leak: estimate and record the blood lost in the circuit; record the dialyzer brand, lot and, if reprocessed, the reuse number. A new dialyzer and lines, and any restart, only on the attending nephrologist’s order. Check temperature and blood pressure before the patient goes home.
  • Report: incident report; tell the reprocessing staff and the biomedical technician. Repeated failures from one dialyzer lot are reported to the supplier, per unit policy.

Emergency quick card 3

Validate against your own unit protocols

Severe low blood pressure or collapse on dialysis

Recognize: Pressure falls during dialysis with yawning, cramps, nausea, dizziness or sweating, or the patient becomes faint. Unresponsive and not breathing normally: Card 1.

  1. 1
    Chair nurse Lay the chair flat, legs raised, as far as breathing allows; turn to the side if vomiting.
  2. 2
    Chair nurse Stop ultrafiltration (UF). Keep the blood pump running unless you suspect air, hemolysis, a severe reaction or blood loss.
  3. 3
    At the same time · Second nurse Check pulse, pressure, oxygen saturation and alertness. Lift the blanket: look at the access and lines.
  4. 4
    Chair nurse Give saline through the circuit per protocol if the pressure does not recover.
  5. 5
    Second nurse Call the physician on duty (POD) and the attending nephrologist if the pressure stays low, falls again or needs more fluid; at once for chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, fever, bleeding or an abnormal pulse.
  6. 6
    At the same time · Charge nurse Assign a watcher for the other patients while two staff are at this chair.
⚠ Critical Collapse is not always the fluid removal. Chest pain, a new irregular pulse, fever, blood under the blanket, or early itching and wheeze point to another card: check before giving more fluid.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

Bleeding from a dislodged needle or the gut (Card 4), arrhythmia (Card 9), acute coronary syndrome (Card 8), sepsis (Card 14), dialyzer reaction or anaphylaxis (Card 7), air (Card 5), hemolysis (Card 6), low blood sugar (Card 11).

Reassess and monitor

Repeat blood pressure, pulse and symptoms after each step, at the interval your protocol sets, until the patient is stable and the POD agrees. The legs-up position rests on expert opinion only, and a breathless patient may not tolerate lying flat.

Circuit and access

Stopping UF stops fluid removal; blood keeps flowing through the dialyzer. The blood pump is stopped only for a circuit cause (air, hemolysis, severe reaction, blood loss). Changes to the UF goal, session time or dialysis fluid are the physician’s decision.

Return the blood?

If the session is ended because the pressure will not recover, return the blood per protocol when the circuit is sound. Do not return it when air, hemolysis or a severe reaction is suspected, or through a clotted circuit.

Escalation and transfer

Emergency transfer when the pressure does not recover after the protocol’s steps, when consciousness stays altered, or when a dangerous cause is likely. The attending nephrologist decides whether to end the session.

Restart

UF resumes only when symptoms settle and the pressure recovers, at the goal and rate the physician sets. Repeated episodes call for a review of the dry weight and prescription, not a habit of giving saline.

Document and follow up

Times, blood pressure series, fluid removed and when UF stopped, fluid given, symptoms, who was called and when, and the outcome. File an incident report for a collapse or a transfer.

Emergency quick card 4

Validate against your own unit protocols

Venous needle dislodgement or access bleeding

Recognize: Blood on the bed, chair or floor; a wet dressing or blanket over the access; a needle out or a line apart; or a patient who turns pale, sweaty or drowsy for no clear reason.

  1. 1
    Any staff Lift the blanket. Look at both needle sites and both line connections.
  2. 2
    Chair nurse Stop the blood pump. Clamp the line on both sides of the break.
  3. 3
    Chair nurse Press firmly on the site with gloved hands and gauze, and keep pressing.
  4. 4
    At the same time · Second nurse Lay the patient flat. Check consciousness, pulse and blood pressure. Give oxygen per protocol.
  5. 5
    Second nurse Call for help and the emergency cart if pale, sweaty, drowsy or hypotensive; call the physician on duty (POD) and the attending nephrologist.
  6. 6
    Charge nurse Estimate the loss (bed, chair, blanket, floor) and assign a watcher for the other patients.
⚠ Critical The venous pressure alarm can stay silent when the needle comes out: a quiet machine proves nothing. Keep the access and connections in view all session. Never reinsert a dislodged needle or reconnect a line end that has been open.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

Arterial needle out (air may be drawn in: see Card 5), infiltration or hematoma, gastrointestinal bleeding, low blood pressure from other causes (Card 3).

Reassess and monitor

Serial blood pressure, pulse and consciousness. Watch the site after pressure for rebleeding or swelling. Blood tests and fluids are the physician’s decision.

Circuit and access

Blood pump stopped and the line clamped on both sides of the break, so the circuit stays closed. A new needle at a new site is placed only on the physician’s decision, with a sterile needle.

Return the blood?

Blood on the floor is lost. Published reviews give no rule for the blood still in the circuit after a dislodgement, so the unit protocol and the attending nephrologist decide. Blood is never returned through a line end that has been open.

Escalation and transfer

The attending nephrologist for every event. Emergency transfer for shock, ongoing bleeding or a large loss. Fluids and transfusion are decided by the physician.

Restart

Only after bleeding is controlled, the patient is stable and a new needle is placed, on the attending nephrologist’s order. The heparin plan is reviewed before any restart.

Document and follow up

Time found, estimated loss, vital signs over time, actions, who was called. Incident report. Review taping, access visibility and alarm limits; European renal nurses published 12 consensus practice points.

Emergency quick card 5

Validate against your own unit protocols

Suspected air embolism

Recognize: Air or foam in the venous line or below the venous chamber, or an air detector alarm, with or without a sudden cough, chest tightness, breathlessness, confusion or collapse.

  1. 1
    Chair nurse Stop the blood pump and clamp the venous line. Never open the clamp or override the air detector.
  2. 2
    Chair nurse Do not return the blood. Rinse-back would push more air into the patient.
  3. 3
    At the same time · Second nurse Position the patient per the unit’s protocol: lying on the left side is the dialysis teaching. Never sit the patient up.
  4. 4
    Second nurse Give oxygen per protocol. Check consciousness, breathing, pulse and oxygen saturation.
  5. 5
    Charge nurse Call for help and the emergency cart; call the physician on duty (POD) and the attending nephrologist; arrange emergency transfer.
  6. 6
    Any staff Start CPR flat on the back if there is no pulse and no normal breathing: go to Card 1.
⚠ Critical Air on the patient side of the detector is an emergency even if the patient feels well at first. Keep the clamp closed until the source is found and a physician decides.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

A false air alarm in a well patient (line not seated in the detector, tiny bubbles): the nurse finds the cause before anything is restarted. Also pulmonary embolism, heart attack, anaphylaxis.

Reassess and monitor

Consciousness and focal signs (air can reach the brain in a seated patient), oxygen saturation, blood pressure, heart rhythm.

Circuit and access

Blood pump stopped and venous line clamped. Look for the entry point: a loose connection before the pump, an empty saline bag, an open catheter limb, a cracked line. Keep the circuit for inspection; the machine is held until the biomedical technician checks the air detector and clamp.

Return the blood?

No. The blood in the circuit is not returned.

Escalation and transfer

Emergency transfer for any symptomatic patient. The receiving hospital decides further treatment. Clinical review required: sources differ on position. Dialysis texts and reviews teach lying on the left side, some with the head down, and a 2021 review cautions that head-down may worsen brain swelling when air has reached the brain. The AHA 2025 special-circumstances guideline considers lying flat on the back or the recovery position reasonable for gas embolism. No trial compares positions; the unit should choose one.

Restart

No restart on that machine until the cause is found and the detector passes its test. Any further dialysis is the attending nephrologist’s decision.

Document and follow up

Time, what was seen, alarm history, estimated amount of air, entry point, machine number, line and dialyzer lot numbers, actions and calls. Incident report and biomedical check.

Emergency quick card 6

Validate against your own unit protocols

Suspected hemolysis

Recognize: Blood in the venous line looks translucent, cherry-red or “port-wine,” or a sample shows pink plasma, often with chest, back or belly pain, nausea and a rising blood pressure. Several patients may be affected.

  1. 1
    Chair nurse Stop the blood pump and clamp the lines.
  2. 2
    Chair nurse Do not return the blood. It carries potassium released from broken red cells.
  3. 3
    At the same time · Second nurse Check airway, breathing and circulation; give oxygen per protocol; attach the monitor.
  4. 4
    POD Order urgent potassium and hemoglobin and the unit’s hemolysis tests; call the attending nephrologist. Treat high potassium with Card 10.
  5. 5
    At the same time · Charge nurse Check every patient on the same water and concentrate supply. Two or more affected: declare a possible cluster, hold new starts, call the Medical Director.
  6. 6
    Technician Keep the lines, dialyzer, packaging and lot numbers, and take samples of water and dialysis fluid.
⚠ Critical Hemolysis is not a blood leak. A blood leak alarm finds blood in the used dialysis fluid (Card 2); hemolysis shows abnormal blood in the blood lines. With hemolysis, the blood is never returned.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

Blood leak (Card 2), very dark blood of a clotting circuit, acute coronary syndrome or aortic dissection when chest or back pain is the main sign.

Reassess and monitor

Continuous ECG (electrocardiogram) for potassium effects, repeat potassium and hemoglobin as the physician orders, and belly pain.

Circuit and access

Blood pump stopped, lines clamped, treatment ended. The machine and the water loop are held until the technician checks dialysis fluid temperature and conductivity and the water for chlorine and chloramine. Kinked or faulty blood tubing has caused outbreaks.

Return the blood?

Never.

Escalation and transfer

The attending nephrologist at once; the Medical Director for any suspected water or dialysis fluid fault or cluster. Emergency transfer for a high potassium, ECG change or unstable patient.

Restart

Not until the cause is found and the water and dialysis fluid are confirmed safe, on a different machine if needed, on the physician’s order.

Document and follow up

Time, blood color, symptoms, machine number, dialyzer and tubing lot numbers, water and fluid test results, other patients checked. Incident report; device faults reported to the supplier and regulator per policy.

Emergency quick card 7

Validate against your own unit protocols

Severe dialyzer reaction or anaphylaxis

Recognize: Within the first minutes of treatment, or soon after a drug, any of: itching, hives, flushing, throat tightness, hoarseness, wheeze, breathlessness, swelling of the face or lips, low blood pressure or collapse.

  1. 1
    Chair nurse Stop the blood pump and clamp the lines. Do not return the blood.
  2. 2
    At the same time · Charge nurse Call for help and the emergency cart; start the unit’s anaphylaxis protocol.
  3. 3
    Authorized nurse or POD Give intramuscular epinephrine (adrenaline) into the outer thigh as the protocol directs. It is the first-line treatment; do not wait for antihistamines or steroids.
  4. 4
    At the same time · Second nurse Protect the airway; give oxygen; position per protocol (flat with legs raised unless breathing is hard); attach the monitor.
  5. 5
    Second nurse Call the physician on duty (POD) and the attending nephrologist; arrange emergency transfer.
  6. 6
    Technician Keep the dialyzer and its packaging; record the model, lot number and sterilization method.
⚠ Critical When anaphylaxis is possible, treat it now. Epinephrine comes first; antihistamines and steroids do not replace it.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

A milder, later (type B) reaction with chest or back pain, diagnosed only after cardiac, embolic and hemolytic causes are excluded; low blood pressure from fluid removal (Card 3); air (Card 5); hemolysis (Card 6). Drugs given during dialysis, such as intravenous iron, antibiotics or heparin, can also cause anaphylaxis.

Reassess and monitor

Airway, breathing, blood pressure and oxygen saturation until handover; further epinephrine doses at the protocol’s interval if symptoms persist; watch for symptoms returning.

Circuit and access

Blood pump stopped, lines clamped, treatment ended. That dialyzer type is not used again for this patient until the physician reviews it.

Return the blood?

No, in a severe reaction: the circuit blood carries the trigger. A mild type B reaction may continue if the nephrologist agrees.

Escalation and transfer

Emergency transfer for anaphylaxis. The attending nephrologist for every reaction. The receiving team decides on blood tests such as tryptase.

Restart

Not on the same dialyzer type or sterilization method until the attending nephrologist decides the next session’s dialyzer and any precautions.

Document and follow up

Onset time relative to the start, symptoms, drugs given with times, dialyzer model, lot and sterilization method, drugs given in the hour before. Update the allergy record. Report the reaction to the supplier and regulator per policy. Clinical review required: positioning wording and the unit’s epinephrine protocol (dose, route, repeat interval) to be confirmed against the unit protocol.

Emergency quick card 8

Validate against your own unit protocols

Chest pain during dialysis

Recognize: New chest pain, pressure or heaviness during dialysis, or its equivalents: breathlessness, sweating, a fall in pressure or a new abnormal pulse, with or without pain.

  1. 1
    POD or chair nurse Go to the patient: alertness, breathing, color; attach the monitor; pressure, pulse, oxygen saturation.
  2. 2
    At the same time · Chair nurse Check the circuit: air, oddly colored blood, or early itching and wheeze point to Card 5, 6 or 7.
  3. 3
    POD Record a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) within 10 minutes and compare it with the last one on file.
  4. 4
    Chair nurse Start the unit’s chest pain protocol, which usually includes stopping ultrafiltration (UF) and oxygen if saturation is low.
  5. 5
    At the same time · Any staff Bring the defibrillator to the chair. Call the attending nephrologist for pain with ECG change, low pressure, a new rhythm, breathlessness or fainting.
  6. 6
    POD Stop per protocol and arrange emergency transfer for ongoing pain, ECG change, low pressure or an unstable rhythm.
⚠ Critical Not every chest pain in the chair is a fluid-removal problem. Treat it as a heart attack, air, hemolysis or a reaction until the ECG and the circuit check say otherwise.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

Acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia (Card 9), heart muscle strain from low pressure, pericarditis, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, air, hemolysis, a type B dialyzer reaction; muscle strain or reflux only after the others are excluded.

Reassess and monitor

Continuous monitor, repeat ECG if pain continues or changes, blood pressure and symptoms. Troponin in kidney failure needs cautious, trend-based interpretation by the receiving team.

Circuit and access

Stop UF per protocol. The blood pump keeps running unless a circuit cause is suspected or the treatment is being ended.

Return the blood?

If the treatment is ended, return the blood per protocol when the circuit is sound, unless air, hemolysis or a severe reaction is suspected.

Escalation and transfer

Where an ECG cannot be obtained or repeated, the chest pain guideline advises referral to an emergency department. Transfer for ongoing pain or ECG change.

Restart

Only when the pain has settled, the ECG is unchanged, the patient is stable and the attending nephrologist agrees, under close observation.

Document and follow up

Times of pain onset, ECGs and their findings, vital signs, fluid removed, actions and calls.

Emergency quick card 9

Validate against your own unit protocols

Symptomatic arrhythmia

Recognize: A new fast, slow or irregular pulse or a monitor rhythm change, with palpitations, dizziness, chest discomfort, breathlessness, low pressure or confusion.

  1. 1
    Any staff Check for a pulse. No pulse and not breathing normally: go to Card 1.
  2. 2
    Any staff Bring the defibrillator and monitor to the chair before you telephone; attach them.
  3. 3
    POD Record a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) and judge stability: low pressure, chest pain, breathlessness or reduced alertness means unstable.
  4. 4
    POD Unstable fast rhythm: follow the advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) tachycardia algorithm; cardioversion only by a trained provider, per protocol.
  5. 5
    POD Unstable slow rhythm: follow the ACLS bradycardia algorithm per protocol, and treat it as high potassium until proven otherwise (Card 10).
  6. 6
    POD Stable: keep the monitor on, check the latest potassium, stop ultrafiltration (UF) per protocol, and call the attending nephrologist. Do not start rhythm drugs from memory.
⚠ Critical A wide-QRS or very slow rhythm is an emergency even when the patient says they feel fine.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

Atrial fibrillation or flutter with a fast rate (common around dialysis and after the long gap), ventricular tachycardia, heart block, high or low potassium (a low-potassium dialysis fluid can push potassium too low), drug effects such as beta-blockers or digoxin, ischemia, low pressure.

Reassess and monitor

Continuous rhythm monitoring; save strips; repeat ECG after any change or treatment; potassium, calcium and magnesium as the physician orders.

Circuit and access

Stable: UF stopped per protocol, blood pump running. Unstable: treatment stopped per protocol while the team follows ACLS.

Return the blood?

Return the blood per protocol if the treatment is ended and the circuit is sound. If high potassium is the cause and the patient is stable, dialysis is the treatment: do not stop it without the attending nephrologist.

Escalation and transfer

Emergency transfer for any unstable rhythm and after any shock. A new stable atrial fibrillation is for the attending nephrologist: blood thinners are not started in the chair today.

Restart

Only for a stable patient, on the attending nephrologist’s order, with the dialysis fluid potassium reviewed.

Document and follow up

Rhythm strips with times, ECG findings, electrolytes, dialysis fluid potassium, drugs and shocks with times, calls.

Emergency quick card 10

Validate against your own unit protocols

Suspected severe hyperkalemia

Recognize: A high potassium result, or missed sessions or the long gap with weakness, a slow or irregular pulse, or electrocardiogram (ECG) changes (peaked T waves, wide QRS, absent P waves).

  1. 1
    Any staff Check for a pulse. No pulse: Card 1. With a pulse: continue here.
  2. 2
    POD Attach the monitor; record a 12-lead ECG. Repeat a doubtful (hemolyzed) sample, but never delay treatment for an abnormal ECG.
  3. 3
    At the same time · POD Call the attending nephrologist at once for any ECG change, weakness, slow or irregular pulse, or a value above the unit’s action threshold; start the order set while you call.
  4. 4
    POD Protect the heart: intravenous calcium as the unit’s order set directs when the ECG shows potassium changes. Calcium does not lower potassium.
  5. 5
    POD Shift potassium into cells: insulin with glucose, inhaled salbutamol, or both, per the order set. Arrange glucose checks for several hours after insulin.
  6. 6
    Chair nurse Remove potassium with dialysis. A connected, stable patient stays on the machine; do not stop dialysis without the attending nephrologist.
⚠ Critical Dialysis is the treatment. Stopping the session in a stable patient removes the only real potassium removal; calcium and insulin only buy time.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

A falsely high result from a hemolyzed sample; hemolysis in the circuit (Card 6); a slow pulse from drugs. ECG changes are often absent even at high levels, so a normal ECG does not make a high value safe.

Reassess and monitor

Continuous ECG; potassium rechecked when the physician orders, including after dialysis; capillary glucose for several hours after insulin, because hypoglycemia followed in about 13% of treated episodes in one hospital series, at a median of 2 hours.

Circuit and access

Keep dialyzing unless the rhythm is unstable or the patient arrests. The dialysis fluid potassium is the physician’s decision. A patient not yet connected is connected promptly on the physician’s order.

Return the blood?

Only relevant if the session must end (arrest or unstable rhythm): see Card 1.

Escalation and transfer

Emergency transfer when dialysis cannot start promptly, ECG changes persist, or the rhythm is unstable. Cardiac arrest from high potassium: Card 1 (guidelines differ on calcium and bicarbonate during arrest; the unit protocol decides).

Restart

Not applicable: the physician sets the dialysis fluid, duration and recheck after an emergency session.

Document and follow up

Potassium values with times, ECG findings, drugs given as ordered with times, glucose series, dialysis fluid potassium, missed sessions and diet history.

Seizure during dialysis

Recognize: Jerking of the limbs, stiffening, staring with no response, or a sudden fall in consciousness with twitching, during or soon after dialysis.

  1. 1
    Chair nurse Protect the patient and the access: guard the head, hold the needle arm steady, move hard objects away. Nothing in the mouth.
  2. 2
    At the same time · Second nurse Time the seizure. Call for help and the physician on duty (POD).
  3. 3
    Chair nurse Stop the blood pump and clamp the lines per protocol, so a needle cannot pull out.
  4. 4
    Chair nurse When the jerking stops, turn the patient on the side; airway and oxygen per protocol.
  5. 5
    Second nurse Check a capillary glucose and treat a low value (Card 11).
  6. 6
    POD Seizure over 5 minutes, or repeated without recovery: status epilepticus. Give only the antiseizure drug the protocol names, and arrange emergency transfer.
⚠ Critical Give only the antiseizure drug your unit’s protocol names, by the person it authorizes. Doses differ in kidney failure.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

Low sugar, dialysis disequilibrium in early sessions with a very high urea, low sodium, calcium or magnesium, very high blood pressure, stroke or bleeding in the head (heparin), drug toxicity (for example cefepime), low oxygen or low pressure from an arrhythmia, epilepsy. Start and end times, what was seen, glucose, vital signs, drugs given with times, injuries, needle sites, urea and session number if new to dialysis.

Fever, rigors or suspected sepsis during dialysis

Power fails during dialysis

Recognize: The main power fails with patients connected: machines alarm on battery, lights flicker or go out. The generator may or may not start.

  1. 1
    Every nurse Stay with your patients. Check airway, alertness and the access; uncover every needle site and look for bleeding.
  2. 2
    At the same time · Technician Report the power status: has the generator started, is it stable, and how long will its fuel last?
  3. 3
    Charge nurse + POD Decide. Continue on the generator only if the technician confirms it is stable. Wait only as long as your protocol allows; otherwise begin the emergency take-off.
  4. 4
    Chair nurse Without power, return the blood by hand crank, one staff member per patient, watching the venous drip chamber and line until the clamp is closed.
  5. 5
    Chair nurse Clamp and cut, do not return, after air, hemolysis, a clotted circuit, or when there is no time. Close catheter clamps before separating the lines.
  6. 6
    Charge nurse Count and record every patient: time off, method, blood returned or not.
  7. 7
    POD Call the attending nephrologist and the Medical Director (SBAR); emergency transfer for anyone unstable.
⚠ Critical Without power the air detector and venous clamp may not work: one staff member watches each venous line until the clamp is closed. Never force a clotted circuit back. Patients never disconnect themselves.
Details: other causes, monitoring, circuit, blood return, escalation, restart, records

Think of these too

A tripped breaker on one circuit (only some machines down); a generator that runs the machines but not the water booster pump (low water pressure alarms minutes later); a water failure rather than a power failure.

Reassess and monitor

Blood pressure, alertness and access sites during and after the return. The generator’s fuel and load; the water pressure.

Circuit and access

No new connections. On a stable generator, sessions may continue per protocol. With a known end time (fuel running out), end sessions in order while the generator still runs, so the alarms still work.

Return the blood?

Yes, by hand crank, if the circuit is sound and there is time. No after air, hemolysis, a clotted circuit, or when there is no time. Blood that may contain air, is hemolyzed, or follows a severe reaction is never returned. Otherwise return it if quick and safe, or clamp and disconnect. Escalation and transfer

The attending nephrologist and the Medical Director by SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation); emergency transfer for any unstable patient. The technician calls the fuel supplier and the utility.

Restart

No new starts until power is stable and the water system has been checked. If the unit closed: only through the five signed restart gates.

Document and follow up

Time of failure, power status reports, each patient’s time off, method, blood returned or not and why, and condition; calls made.

Earthquake during dialysis

Recognize: The ground shakes while patients are connected.

  1. 1
    Everyone During shaking, protect in place. Patients stay seated and connected, covering head and neck with the free arm or a pillow. Staff duck, cover and hold beside the nearest patient, shielding the head and steadying the access arm.
  2. 2
    At the same time · Everyone Do not disconnect during shaking. Nobody runs.
  3. 3
    Chair nurse When it stops, check every patient first: needles and lines secure, no blood at the connections. Press and clamp any dislodged needle.
  4. 4
    At the same time · Technician Check the machines: upright, running, no water leak; then the water room and the power.
  5. 5
    Charge nurse Check the room: gas smell, fire, new cracks, exits. Count everyone.
  6. 6
    Charge nurse Evacuation ordered, or the building unsafe? No: continue or end each session per protocol. Yes: emergency take-off by staff with the blood rule, then out by the stairs (Card 17 for the census).
  7. 7
    POD Examine anyone hurt or unwell; call the attending nephrologist.
⚠ Critical Never disconnect during shaking. Never return blood that may contain air, is hemolyzed, or follows a severe reaction. Expect aftershocks: protect in place again each time.

An aftershock; a fall or injury; a dislodged needle hidden under a blanket; a gas or water leak; damage in the water room.

About these cards

Sudden cardiac death is the most common cause of death in people on hemodialysis, and in a United States registry of cardiac arrests in outpatient dialysis clinics, most patients received staff-started cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), but a defibrillator was applied in only about six in ten. The first minute is decided by people who have rehearsed it. Each card follows the Academy modules linked at its foot, where the reasoning, the evidence and practice cases are.

Each card lists who acts, which circuit step to take, and whether the blood is returned. Steps marked “At the same time” run alongside the step before them. Every card names someone to watch the other patients on the floor. Print a set for the crash cart and the nurses’ station, and run a five-minute drill from one card at the start of a shift.

The AHA (American Heart Association) and ERC (European Resuscitation Council) guidelines below apply in the dialysis unit as everywhere; the ERC guideline has a section on cardiac arrest in the dialysis unit. Where sources disagree, the card says so and leaves the choice to the unit protocol. References were checked on November 27, 2026.
