The Basics

Gross vs. Microscopic Hematuria — Two Very Different Problems

The first question is not why there is blood in your urine — it is how much. Gross hematuria (visible to the naked eye) and microscopic hematuria (only detectable on urinalysis) have very different causes, different urgency, and different workup paths. Assuming they are the same thing is the most common mistake patients and even some clinicians make.

Gross Hematuria

Visible red, pink, or brown urine — enough blood to change the color. It only takes 1 mL of blood in 1 litre of urine to turn it visibly red. This is never normal in an adult and always requires evaluation.

  • May be painless (bladder cancer, IgAN) or painful (stones, infection)
  • Clots in urine suggest a significant upper or lower urinary tract source
  • Single episode still warrants full workup, especially age >40
  • Brown or cola-coloured urine often means blood has been present longer — or points to glomerular origin (dysmorphic RBCs)

Microscopic Hematuria

≥3 RBCs per high-power field (HPF) on properly spun mid-stream urine — invisible to the eye. Often discovered incidentally on routine urinalysis.

  • Confirm with 2 separate samples before working up — transient causes are common (vigorous exercise, menstruation, Foley catheter)
  • Dysmorphic RBCs or RBC casts = glomerular bleeding → nephrology, not urology
  • Isomorphic RBCs = lower tract bleeding → urology workup
  • In adults ≥35 with no benign explanation: cystoscopy + upper tract imaging
⚠️

Things that mimic hematuria — and are not blood

  • Beetroot, red camote, blackberries: turns urine red or pink — no blood on dipstick
  • Rifampicin (TB medication): orange-red urine — expected side effect, not blood
  • Myoglobinuria (after severe muscle injury): dipstick tests positive for blood but urinalysis shows no RBCs
  • Menstrual contamination: always collect mid-stream urine; repeat after menstruation ends
  • Rule of thumb: dipstick positive for blood + no RBCs on microscopy = not hematuria
Localizing the Bleed

The Three-Tube Test — Where is the Blood Coming From?

Before imaging and cystoscopy, a simple bedside manoeuvre can narrow the source of bleeding to the urethra/prostate, the bladder, or the upper tract (kidneys/ureters). This is called the three-glass (three-tube) test — ask the patient to urinate continuously into three sequential containers.

Tube 1 — Initial stream

Red

Blood clears in tubes 2 and 3. Bleeding is from the urethra or anterior urethra — urethritis, urethral carcinoma, urethral polyp.

Tubes 1 & 2 — Mid stream

Clear, then red in Tube 3

Terminal hematuria — blood appears only at the end of voiding. Source is the bladder neck or posterior urethra — bladder tumour, posterior urethral lesion, BPH.

All 3 tubes

All equally red

Total hematuria — blood throughout. Source is the bladder body or upper tract (kidneys, ureters). Requires cystoscopy + upper tract imaging.

💡

The three-tube test in the age of cystoscopy

Modern flexible cystoscopy has largely replaced the three-tube test in clinical practice, but the concept remains invaluable for helping patients describe their symptoms accurately and for guiding initial triage in settings where cystoscopy is not immediately available. When a patient reports blood "only at the start" vs. "throughout," they are giving you the same information.

RBC Morphology — The Glomerular Clue

When urine microscopy is performed on a properly spun specimen by an experienced observer, the shape of the red blood cells matters as much as the count.

Dysmorphic RBCs / Acanthocytes

Irregular, distorted, or "Mickey Mouse" ear–shaped cells (acanthocytes). These are RBCs that have been deformed by passage through the glomerular basement membrane and the renal tubules. ≥5% acanthocytes or RBC casts = glomerular hematuria → nephrology referral, not urology. Causes: IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, ANCA vasculitis, thin basement membrane disease.

Isomorphic (uniform) RBCs

Normal biconcave disc shape — these cells have not passed through the glomerulus. Isomorphic RBCs = lower urinary tract or non-glomerular upper tract bleeding. Causes: bladder cancer, transitional cell carcinoma of the ureter, kidney stones, benign prostatic hyperplasia, urinary tract infection, arteriovenous malformation.

IgA Nephropathy — The Most Common GN Worldwide

The Synpharyngitic Pattern — IgA Nephropathy's Calling Card

IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide and a leading cause of kidney failure in young adults. It has a distinctive and recognizable presentation that patients and families can identify — if they know what to look for. The Philippines has a high prevalence due to genetic and environmental factors.

The Classic Presentation

🦠

Day 0–1: Upper respiratory infection begins

Sore throat, tonsillitis, pharyngitis — often viral, sometimes bacterial strep. Mucosal IgA antibodies are produced in response to the infection.

🩸

Day 1–3: Gross hematuria appears (synpharyngitic)

Unlike post-infectious GN (which follows strep by 2–3 weeks), IgAN hematuria occurs during or within 3 days of the infection. This near-simultaneous timing is the key diagnostic clue. The urine turns cola-coloured or frankly red.

🔬

Between episodes: Microscopic hematuria persists

Even when the urine clears visibly, urinalysis typically shows persistent microscopic hematuria and often mild proteinuria (<1 g/day in early disease). This is what gets missed — patients feel well, and hematuria between episodes goes undetected without routine urinalysis.

⚠️

Years later: Progressive CKD if untreated

20–40% of IgAN patients progress to end-stage kidney disease over 20 years. KDIGO 2021 guidelines now recommend SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) and optimized supportive care. Sparsentan and targeted-release budesonide (Nefecon) are newer options. Early diagnosis and treatment change outcomes.

🔎

The IgAN checklist — when to suspect it

  • Young adult (age 15–40) with recurrent episodes of gross hematuria
  • Each episode occurs during or within 1–3 days of a respiratory infection or gastroenteritis
  • Persistent microscopic hematuria between episodes on routine UA
  • Mild proteinuria (spot urine PCR 100–500 mg/mmol)
  • Normal complement levels (C3, C4) — distinguishes from PSGN where C3 is low
  • Family history of kidney disease or deafness (consider Alport syndrome in the differential)
  • Diagnosis requires kidney biopsy showing mesangial IgA deposits on immunofluorescence
⚠️

Don't confuse IgAN with post-strep GN — the timing is different

Post-infectious (post-strep) GN also follows a throat or skin infection — but the latent period is 2–3 weeks after the infection resolves. It presents with low complement (C3), cola urine, hypertension, and edema (nephritic syndrome). It is self-limiting in children but can be serious in adults. IgAN, by contrast, occurs during the infection, complements are normal, and it is a chronic, progressive disease.

Clinical Decision-Making

When a Urine Culture Is Enough — and When It Isn't

The most important triage decision in hematuria is whether this is a simple infection requiring only a urine culture, or whether cystoscopy, imaging, and nephrology referral are needed. Getting this wrong in either direction has real consequences: over-investigating simple UTI wastes resources and causes anxiety; under-investigating bladder cancer causes fatal delays.

❓ Is there hematuria + dysuria + frequency + fever in a young woman with no prior UTI?
↓ Yes
✓ Urine culture and sensitivity + empiric antibiotics. Repeat UA 6 weeks after treatment. If hematuria resolves and culture was positive → UTI confirmed. No further workup needed.
❓ Did hematuria persist 6 weeks after successful treatment of UTI?
↓ Yes
⚠️ Do not attribute residual hematuria to the treated UTI. Proceed to full hematuria workup: upper tract imaging + cystoscopy.
❓ Is there gross hematuria with no UTI symptoms, age ≥35, or any risk factor for malignancy?
↓ Yes
🔴 Do not wait for a urine culture. Refer urgently to urology for cystoscopy + CT urogram. Bladder cancer is painless and culture-negative.
❓ Is there dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, proteinuria, or hypertension with hematuria?
↓ Yes
🔴 Glomerular hematuria. Nephrology referral — cystoscopy is not indicated. Kidney biopsy may be needed.

Risk Factors That Always Demand Full Workup

High-risk features — do not manage conservatively

  • Smoking history (current or past) — the #1 risk factor for bladder cancer
  • Age ≥35 with any degree of hematuria (gross or microscopic, even single episode)
  • Male sex with gross hematuria at any age
  • Occupational exposure: dye industry, rubber, leather, paint, petroleum workers (aromatic amine exposure)
  • Prior pelvic radiation therapy
  • Cyclophosphamide or ifosfamide chemotherapy history
  • Chronic phenacetin or analgesic use (analgesic nephropathy)
  • Recurrent gross hematuria at any age — even if previous workup was negative
  • Gross hematuria with blood clots — always high-risk
  • Unexplained weight loss + hematuria
💡

The "one-time" episode — why it still matters

A single episode of gross hematuria, even if it completely resolves and the patient feels well, has a significant probability of underlying bladder cancer — especially in adults over 40. Guidelines from the AUA and EAU both recommend cystoscopy and upper tract imaging after even one episode of unexplained gross hematuria in an adult. "It only happened once and it went away" is the most common story bladder cancer patients tell at diagnosis.

Differential Diagnosis

The Major Causes of Hematuria at a Glance

Urological (Non-Glomerular)

  • Urinary tract infection (most common overall)
  • Urolithiasis (kidney stones)
  • Bladder cancer (transitional cell carcinoma)
  • Renal cell carcinoma
  • Urothelial cancer of ureter or renal pelvis
  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)
  • Prostate cancer
  • Urethral stricture or caruncle
  • Pyelonephritis
  • Renal cysts (simple; polycystic)
  • Renal arteriovenous malformation
  • Nutcracker syndrome
  • Trauma
  • Exercise-induced

Glomerular (Nephrology)

  • IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease)
  • Post-infectious GN (PSGN)
  • Lupus nephritis (class III/IV)
  • ANCA-associated vasculitis (GPA, MPA)
  • Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture)
  • Thin basement membrane disease
  • Alport syndrome (hereditary nephritis)
  • Membranoproliferative GN
  • Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
  • Henoch-Schönlein purpura nephritis
  • C3 glomerulopathy

Systemic / Other

  • Anticoagulation therapy (warfarin, rivaroxaban, apixaban) — always investigate the cause even if anticoagulated
  • Sickle cell disease or trait
  • Papillary necrosis (DM, NSAID, sickle)
  • Schistosomiasis (endemic areas)
  • Tuberculosis of the urinary tract
  • Leptospirosis
  • Radiation cystitis
  • Cyclophosphamide cystitis
  • Endometriosis of the bladder
  • Coagulopathy / thrombocytopenia
⚠️

Anticoagulants do not explain hematuria — they unmask it

A common mistake: a patient on warfarin or a DOAC has hematuria and is told "it's from your blood thinner." Anticoagulation does not cause hematuria — it lowers the threshold for bleeding from an underlying lesion. Studies consistently show that 25–40% of anticoagulated patients with significant hematuria have an underlying urological malignancy. Full hematuria workup is indicated regardless of anticoagulation status.

Getting the Right Specialist

When to See a Urologist, Nephrologist, or Both

🔬 See a Nephrologist when:

  • Dysmorphic RBCs or RBC casts on urine microscopy
  • Proteinuria >500 mg/day accompanying hematuria
  • Hematuria + hypertension in a young person
  • Recurrent gross hematuria with synpharyngitic pattern (IgAN)
  • Hematuria + edema (nephrotic/nephritic syndrome)
  • Rising serum creatinine with hematuria
  • Hematuria with low complement levels (C3/C4)
  • Family history of kidney failure + hematuria + deafness (Alport)
  • Suspected systemic disease: lupus, vasculitis, Goodpasture

🏥 See a Urologist when:

  • Gross hematuria, age ≥35, no obvious benign cause
  • Any gross hematuria with clots
  • Microscopic hematuria with no proteinuria, no casts, age ≥35
  • Smoker with any unexplained hematuria
  • Occupational risk factors (dye, rubber, petroleum workers)
  • Colicky flank pain + hematuria (stones — CT KUB first)
  • Hematuria with urinary obstructive symptoms in men >50
  • Hematuria persisting after successful UTI treatment
  • Pelvic radiation or cyclophosphamide history
💡

The standard workup for unexplained hematuria

  • Urine microscopy — RBC morphology (dysmorphic vs isomorphic), casts, culture
  • Spot urine protein:creatinine ratio — quantifies proteinuria quickly
  • CT urogram (CT of the kidneys, ureters, and bladder with and without contrast) — the gold standard for upper tract imaging; detects stones, tumours, structural lesions
  • Cystoscopy — direct visualisation of the bladder and urethra; cannot be replaced by imaging for detecting flat carcinoma in situ (CIS)
  • Urine cytology — may detect high-grade urothelial malignancy but has poor sensitivity for low-grade tumours
  • Serum creatinine, eGFR, serum electrolytes — assess kidney function
  • If glomerular hematuria suspected: ANA, ANCA, anti-GBM, C3, C4, anti-dsDNA, hepatitis B and C serology, ASO titre
Emergencies

Red Flags — Go to the Emergency Room Today

🆘

Seek emergency care immediately for any of the following:

  • Gross hematuria with clots causing urinary retention — unable to urinate despite urge; bladder distension and pain. Requires urgent catheterisation and bladder irrigation.
  • Hematuria + severe flank pain + vomiting + fever >38.5°C — obstructed infected kidney (pyonephrosis) is a surgical emergency; can cause septic shock within hours.
  • Hematuria + confusion, severe headache, or seizures — hypertensive emergency from malignant GN; creatinine may be rising rapidly.
  • Frank blood + severe hemodynamic instability — haemorrhage from renal trauma, ruptured AVM, or papillary necrosis; may require embolisation or surgery.
  • Hematuria + new facial puffiness + breathlessness in a child — nephritic syndrome; urgent nephrology assessment needed.
  • Hematuria + haemoptysis (coughing blood) — Goodpasture syndrome or ANCA vasculitis causing pulmonary-renal syndrome; life-threatening without immediate immunosuppression.

The bottom line

Blood in the urine is never a colour problem — it is an information problem. The location (gross vs. microscopic), timing (during infection vs. weeks after), accompanying symptoms (pain vs. painless), and cell morphology (dysmorphic vs. isomorphic) together tell a story that leads to the correct diagnosis. The most dangerous thing you can do is wait and see if it happens again. One episode of unexplained hematuria in an adult is one episode too many to ignore.
