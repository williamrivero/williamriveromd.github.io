Overview · Framework

A Systematic 9-Step Clinical Framework

Hematuria is one of the most common referral triggers in nephrology and urology — and one of the most frequently mishandled. The fundamental clinical error is treating hematuria as a single entity rather than as a symptom that immediately bifurcates into two completely divergent pathways. Getting the first decision right — gross versus microscopic, and glomerular versus non-glomerular — determines every subsequent step. Conflating the two pathways delays cancer diagnosis in one direction and delays immunosuppression in the other.

This reference is organized as nine sequential steps. Steps 1–2 apply to all presentations: confirm true hematuria, classify the presentation, and make the pivotal glomerular/non-glomerular distinction. Steps 3–5 cover the nephrology pathway (glomerular origin confirmed): syndrome-driven GN serology, IgA nephropathy risk stratification and treatment, and kidney biopsy protocol. Steps 6–7 cover the urology pathway (non-glomerular): urothelial malignancy risk stratification and cystoscopy/imaging indications. Steps 8–9 complete the framework: integrated referral thresholds and long-term monitoring for patients without a definitive diagnosis.

⚠️

Before any workup — exclude pseudo-hematuria

Confirm true hematuria with urine microscopy (≥3 RBC/hpf on a fresh, properly spun mid-stream specimen). A positive dipstick without RBCs on microscopy indicates myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis), hemoglobinuria (haemolysis), or dietary/drug pigmenturia (beets, rifampicin, phenazopyridine, senna). In premenopausal women, menstrual contamination must be excluded — repeat with a catheterised or post-menstrual specimen. Dipstick alone is insufficient to confirm or initiate workup for hematuria.

Step 1 · The Pivotal Distinction

Glomerular vs. Non-Glomerular — The Decision That Drives Everything

Every hematuria evaluation pivots on a single question: is the bleeding from the glomerulus or from the urothelium? The answer determines the entire subsequent pathway — nephrology (biopsy + immunology) vs. urology (cystoscopy + CT urography). Phase contrast urine microscopy is the most powerful triage test; acanthocytes >5% have 98% specificity for glomerulonephritis. In the Philippines, this is available at NKTI and most university nephrology centres. When unavailable, proteinuria ≥1+ on dipstick with dysmorphic RBCs on bright-field microscopy is a sufficient trigger for nephrology referral.

Where this step leads

Glomerular features confirmed (dysmorphic RBCs >80%, acanthocytes >5%, RBC casts, or proteinuria ≥500 mg/day): proceed to the Nephrology Pathway — Steps 3–5 (GN serology → IgAN/disease-specific management → biopsy).

No glomerular features (eumorphic RBCs, absent or trace proteinuria, gross hematuria, urological risk factors): proceed to the Urology Pathway — Steps 6–7 (urothelial risk stratification → cystoscopy + upper tract imaging).

⚠️

Anticoagulant-associated hematuria — do not dismiss

Hematuria in a patient on anticoagulation (warfarin, DOAC) at therapeutic levels is NOT explained by the anticoagulant alone. AUA 2020 explicitly states full urological evaluation is still required — anticoagulation unmasks, not causes, bleeding lesions. Document INR/anti-Xa; if supratherapeutic, correct first, then evaluate.

Nephrology Pathway — Steps 3–5

Enter this pathway when Step 1 confirms glomerular origin: dysmorphic RBCs >80%, acanthocytes >5%, RBC casts, or proteinuria ≥500 mg/day. The three steps that follow move in sequence: syndrome-driven serology first (Step 3), then disease-specific risk stratification and treatment for the most common entity — IgAN (Step 4), then biopsy decision-making for cases where histology will change management (Step 5).

⚠️

Complement interpretation pitfall

In PSGN, C3 is consumed but returns to normal within 6–8 weeks. If C3 remains low at 8 weeks, this is NOT PSGN — consider C3 glomerulopathy (C3G), MPGN, or lupus nephritis. Persistently low C4 with normal C3 favors hereditary complement deficiency or SLE (C4 null alleles common in Filipinos). Do not discharge a hypocomplementemic patient without 8-week C3 repeat.

💡

Philippine context — serologic test availability

ANCA (MPO + PR3), anti-GBM, anti-dsDNA, C3/C4, and Hep B/C panels are available at PGH, NKTI, and all major private tertiary centers. C3 nephritic factor and genetic panel (COL4A3/4/5) are available at selected centers (St. Luke's, Asian Hospital, Makati Medical Center) and via referral to NKTI. Anti-PLA2R for membranous nephropathy is available at PGH and select private labs at ₱1,500–₱2,500.

Step 4 · KDIGO 2021 · KDIGO 2024 Controversies Conference

IgA Nephropathy — Diagnosis, Oxford MEST-C Scoring & Treatment Algorithm

IgAN is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide and disproportionately affects Asian populations. It accounts for an estimated 30–40% of primary GN biopsies in the Philippines. The therapeutic landscape changed dramatically in 2021–2024 with SGLT2 inhibitors, sparsentan, and targeted-release budesonide reaching approval — making updated risk stratification essential before any treatment decision.

💡

Optimized supportive care — the mandatory foundation

No immunosuppression should be started without first achieving optimized supportive care for a minimum of 3 months. This means: (1) ACEi or ARB at maximum tolerated dose, (2) BP <130/80 mmHg (or <125/75 if UPCR >1 g/g), (3) SGLT2i if eGFR ≥20, (4) dietary sodium <2 g/day, (5) smoking cessation. Proteinuria often falls to low-risk levels with supportive care alone, avoiding unnecessary immunosuppression — the STOP-IgAN trial demonstrated this clearly.

🇵🇭

Philippine availability note

Sparsentan (Filspari®) is FDA-approved in the US (February 2023) and EU but is not yet registered in the Philippines as of mid-2026. Targeted-release budesonide (Nefecon/Tarpeyo®) is similarly unavailable locally. Standard corticosteroid protocols (Pozzi) and ACEi/ARB remain the mainstay. Dapagliflozin (Forxiga) is widely available in the Philippines at ₱60–₱90/tablet; empagliflozin (Jardiance) at ₱70–₱100/tablet. PhilHealth Z-benefit covers dialysis but not GN immunosuppression — patient cost-sharing for corticosteroid courses remains modest.

Step 5 · Procedural Guide

Kidney Biopsy — Indications, Contraindications & Pre-Procedural Protocol

Renal biopsy is the definitive diagnostic tool for glomerular hematuria — but it carries a 1–3% risk of clinically significant bleeding and a <0.1% risk of nephrectomy or death. It should be reserved for situations where the histologic result will change management. Isolated hematuria with no proteinuria, normal eGFR, and normal BP rarely requires biopsy; the yield is low and the result (usually TBMN) rarely changes management.

Indications (biopsy is likely to change management)

Indication Urgency Rationale
Glomerular hematuria + UPCR >0.5 g/g, persistent >3 months Elective (within 2–4 weeks) Histologic diagnosis directs immunosuppression decision; separates IgAN from lupus, FSGS, MN
Rapidly progressive GN (AKI + active sediment + RBC casts) Urgent (within 24–48 h) Crescentic GN — immunosuppression window is narrow; delay worsens outcome
Nephrotic syndrome with hematuria (mixed nephritic-nephrotic) Elective (within 1–2 weeks) MPGN, lupus Class III/IV, C3GN — all require histology for classification
Unexplained AKI with active urine sediment after ruling out pre-renal and obstructive causes Urgent Acute interstitial nephritis vs. crescentic GN — different treatment; delay causes irreversible fibrosis
Suspected lupus nephritis (any class, especially with hematuria) Elective (within 1–2 weeks) ISN/RPS class determines mycophenolate vs. cyclophosphamide induction; class I/II may not need immunosuppression
Familial hematuria with progression (proteinuria, HTN, or eGFR decline) Elective (non-urgent) EM for GBM thickness; IF for IgA; genetic testing preferred first in young males (Alport)

Pre-Biopsy Checklist

✔️

Pre-procedure (day before + day of)

  • CBC with platelets, PT, APTT, INR — within 48 hours of procedure
  • Blood type and screen (crossmatch if high-risk single kidney or coagulopathy)
  • Serum creatinine and electrolytes
  • Renal ultrasound same day — document kidney size (cm), echogenicity, cortical thickness, bilateral kidneys present, absence of hydronephrosis, no perirenal collection
  • Hold anticoagulants per protocol above (confirmed day-of)
  • IV access secured — 18G or larger
  • Nil by mouth for light snack if IV sedation planned (not routinely needed)
  • Patient consent — explain 1–3% transfusion risk, <0.1% nephrectomy risk, post-biopsy monitoring requirements
  • Plan for post-biopsy monitoring: 4–6 h supine bed rest, BP + HR q30min × 4 h, urine colour check each void, repeat USS at 4 h if gross hematuria or hypotension
💡

Tissue handling — maximise yield

A minimum of 10 glomeruli (ideally ≥20) is needed for adequate Oxford MEST-C scoring. Request: (1) light microscopy (PAS, silver methenamine, Masson trichrome, H&E), (2) direct immunofluorescence (IgA, IgG, IgM, C3, C1q, fibrin, kappa, lambda), (3) electron microscopy if TBMN, Alport, or membranous suspected. In the Philippines, NKTI and PGH pathology provide full IF; EM is available at NKTI and select private laboratories. Arrange in advance — tissue must reach the EM lab in glutaraldehyde within 30 min of biopsy.

Step 6 · AUA 2020 · Philippine Oncology Context

Urothelial Malignancy — Risk Stratification & Workup Pathway

Bladder cancer is the 10th most common cancer in the Philippines (GLOBOCAN 2022). Hematuria — gross or microscopic — is the presenting symptom in >85% of bladder cancers. The AUA 2020 Microhematuria Guidelines introduced a formal three-tier risk stratification replacing the previous binary model. Philippine clinicians should be aware of aristolochic acid nephropathy as a locally significant risk factor for upper tract urothelial carcinoma.

⚠️

Urine cytology — use it selectively, not routinely

Sensitivity for detecting bladder cancer overall is only 35–65%; sensitivity for low-grade papillary tumour is <25% — a negative cytology does not exclude low-grade disease. However, sensitivity for high-grade TCC and carcinoma in situ (CIS) is 80–90%, which is exactly where cystoscopy most commonly misses lesions (flat CIS is invisible on white-light cystoscopy). Order urine cytology in high-risk patients: smokers, occupational exposure, gross hematuria, prior TCC. Collect three morning mid-stream voided specimens or a catheterised specimen. Not useful as a screening tool in low-risk patients.

💡

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) — do not miss

UTUC (renal pelvis and ureter) accounts for ~10% of all urothelial cancers and is disproportionately associated with aristolochic acid exposure — a Philippine-relevant concern. CT urography (excretory phase) is the diagnostic standard; sensitivity ~80–90% for UTUC. Retrograde pyelography is indicated when CT urography shows a filling defect or when CT urography is non-diagnostic and clinical suspicion is high. Ureteroscopy with biopsy is the definitive diagnostic step if imaging is suspicious.

Step 7 · AUA 2020 Criteria

Cystoscopy & Upper Tract Imaging — When and Which

Cystoscopy and CT urography are complementary, not interchangeable. Cystoscopy visualises the urothelium from the bladder neck to the ureteral orifices; CT urography evaluates the upper tracts (renal parenchyma, renal pelvis, ureters). In the Philippines, CT urography (triple-phase) is available at most provincial hospitals; when unavailable, retrograde pyelography at cystoscopy is an acceptable substitute for the excretory phase. Blue-light cystoscopy, which improves carcinoma in situ (CIS) detection by ~25%, is available at NKTI and select private tertiary centres.

Three-phase CT urography — the standard upper tract study

Unenhanced (stones) + nephrographic (parenchymal lesions) + excretory phase (urothelial filling defects). Single-phase CT or renal USS alone is insufficient for urothelial tumour evaluation. In centres where CT urography is not available (common in provincial Philippines), retrograde pyelography at the time of cystoscopy is an acceptable substitute for the excretory phase.

Step 9 · Long-Term Monitoring

Follow-Up Protocol — Persistent Microscopic Hematuria & Thin Basement Membrane Nephropathy

A significant proportion of patients with microscopic hematuria will complete a full urological and nephrological workup and remain without a definitive diagnosis. In this group, the most likely underlying entity is thin basement membrane nephropathy (TBMN) / Alport spectrum disease — largely benign in heterozygotes but with a minority risk of progression. The clinical challenge is identifying the progressor before irreversible proteinuria or eGFR decline develops.

When TBMN is not benign — identifying progressors

TBMN progressors develop proteinuria (>500 mg/day) before eGFR decline. Annual UPCR is the single most important follow-up test. Red flags for progression: (1) family history of ESRD in a first-degree relative with hematuria; (2) development of hypertension; (3) new proteinuria on annual check; (4) COL4A3/A4 digenic or compound heterozygous mutations (reclassify as autosomal recessive Alport). These patients warrant nephrology referral, RAS blockade, and SGLT2i.

Genetic testing for Alport spectrum in the Philippines

COL4A3/A4/A5 sequencing is available at selected centers (The Medical City Genomics, St. Luke's BGC Genetics, Makati Medical Center, and via referral to NKTI collaborating laboratories). Cost ranges ₱15,000–₱45,000 depending on scope (panel vs. whole-exome). For a young male with isolated hematuria and a family history of male relatives developing renal failure, genetic testing is preferred over biopsy as the first diagnostic step — it avoids the procedural risk and provides prognostic information for family members simultaneously.
