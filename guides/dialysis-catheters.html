Scope of this guide

A layered bedside field guide to dialysis-access catheters for the whole unit — dialysis and intensive-care-unit (ICU) nurses, internal-medicine (IM) residents, and nephrology fellows and consultants. It covers the non-tunneled hemodialysis catheter (NTHC), the tunneled dialysis catheter (TDC, also called the tunneled cuffed catheter), continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) circuit access, and the peritoneal dialysis (PD) catheter — and the end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) Life-Plan that frames all four. Every page carries a core layer (what you do, at the chair, tonight) and, where the evidence is deep or contested, a deeper-dive layer (the trial, the effect size, the grade). Out of scope: arteriovenous fistula (AVF) and arteriovenous graft (AVG) creation and cannulation technique. Guideline anchors: KDOQI (Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative) 2019 for vascular access, KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2012 for acute kidney injury (AKI), IDSA (Infectious Diseases Society of America) 2009 for catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI), the CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) Core Interventions, and ISPD (International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis) for the PD catheter.

⚠️

Clinical use & disclaimer

This is an educational reference intended to support — not replace — your facility's protocol, the device instructions-for-use, and bedside clinical judgment. Catheter devices, lock formulations, and CRRT machines (Prismaflex/PrisMax, NxStage, Aquarius, Fresenius) differ in specification, priming volume, alarm behavior, and material compatibility. Drug doses (alteplase, heparin, citrate, antibiotics) are illustrative starting points from the published literature and common protocols; verify every dose and dwell against a current pharmacology reference and your pharmacist. Your unit's protocol wins.

⛔

One number you must never confuse

Alteplase appears at two doses in this guide. 2 mg per lumen is the treatment dose for a dysfunctional catheter (KDOQI 22.4). 1 mg per lumen once weekly is the prophylactic lock dose (PreCLOT / KDOQI 21.6). They are never interchangeable and must never be given as if they were. When in doubt, ask what you are treating: an occluded catheter now, or the prevention of future malfunction.

Part I · The Access Life-Plan

Access Is a Life-Plan, Not a Procedure

A dialysis catheter is not a piece of plastic. It is a loan drawn against the patient's vascular future, at compound interest paid in infection and central-vein stenosis. Every catheter-day is a decision to keep borrowing. The three questions this guide answers are simple to ask and hard to answer well: Should it be in? Is it working? Is it clean?

1.1 What replaced "Fistula First"

KDOQI 2019 retired the rigid "Fistula First" hierarchy and replaced it with the individualized ESKD Life-Plan, built on a P-L-A-N (Patient Life-Plan → corresponding Access Needs). The question changed from "which access is first?" to "what is next?" across a lifetime of kidney replacement therapy. This reframing is why a catheter is no longer automatically a failure — but it is also why an unplanned catheter always is.

🔬

Read the grade before you quote the rule

KDOQI 2019 statement 2.3 suggests AV access in preference to a catheter in most incident and prevalent HD (hemodialysis) patients on infection grounds — but it is graded Conditional, low quality of evidence. It is a suggestion, not a mandate, and that softening is deliberate: AV-access superiority is largely observational and confounded by the fact that healthier patients get fistulas. Do not overstate it.

1.2 The catheter burden — the numbers

The United States Renal Data System (USRDS) 2024 Annual Data Report (data year 2022) recorded that 74.3% of US patients initiated hemodialysis with a catheter alone, a further 9.3% with a catheter plus a maturing fistula, and only 12.5% with a functioning AVF. So roughly five in six incident HD patients have a catheter in place at first dialysis. The catheter-alone rate has risen from a 60.3% nadir in 2013 — we are getting worse, not better.

⚠️

Verify before you quote

These USRDS figures are from data year 2022 and were the most recent available at writing. The USRDS report is republished annually; confirm against the current-year chapter before citing, and do not present them as "2026 data." No guideline-grade global (non-US) incident-catheter figure exists — do not state one.

1.3 When the catheter is the RIGHT answer

KDOQI 2.2 and 2.15 make tunneled catheters reasonable for long-term or indefinite duration when there are exhausted AV options, anatomy precluding an AVF or AVG, limited life expectancy, or a valid patient preference where AV access would severely limit quality of life or life goals. KDOQI 2.10 prefers a tunneled over a non-tunneled catheter whenever the need is not emergent, on lower-infection-risk grounds (Expert Opinion).

✅

Action

A catheter chosen on purpose and documented in the life-plan is good medicine. A catheter arrived at by default is a system failure. The plastic is identical; the difference is the sentence you wrote in the chart.

1.4 Vein real estate — what you spend when you cannulate

Every central cannulation is a withdrawal from a finite account: the patient's central veins and the arm veins downstream of them. A subclavian catheter can cost the patient the entire ipsilateral arm for future access, permanently. This single idea — that you are spending an irreplaceable asset — is what separates nephrology from routine ICU line practice, and it governs every site decision in Part III.

📌

Bottom line

The catheter is never the asset. The asset is the vein you are spending to place it. Choose the catheter on purpose, write the exit strategy in the chart, and the plastic becomes a plan instead of a debt.

Part II · Physics & Device Anatomy

Why Geometry — Not Effort — Decides Whether Dialysis Is Delivered

2.1 The central vein map

The territory: internal jugular (IJ) → brachiocephalic (innominate) → superior vena cava (SVC) → cavoatrial junction → right atrium (RA); and femoral → iliac → inferior vena cava (IVC). The left side is a longer, more angulated journey because the left brachiocephalic vein crosses the mediastinum transversely — remember this, because it explains half of this guide. The subclavian vein sits in a bony vice between the clavicle and the first rib, which is exactly why it scars.

2.2 Anatomy of a dialysis catheter

From proximal to distal a tunneled catheter runs: hubs (red = arterial/withdrawal, blue = venous/return) → extension limbs → clamps → suture wing → skin exit → Dacron cuff → subcutaneous tunnel → venotomy → intravascular shaft → tip. The priming volume is printed on every hub. It is not decoration: it is the volume of lock you must instil, and the volume you must aspirate before use.

Which tip wins?

Tip design is a recirculation-versus-wall-apposition trade-off, and no design has convincingly won on hard outcomes. Choose on operator familiarity and what your unit stocks — not on marketing.

2.3 Why a triple-lumen won't dialyze — Hagen–Poiseuille

Flow through a tube obeys the Hagen–Poiseuille relationship: Q ∝ (ΔP · r⁴) / (η · L). Flow scales with the fourth power of the radius and inversely with length. Halve the radius and flow falls to one-sixteenth. This is why a 7-French (Fr) triple-lumen ICU line cannot support the 300–450 mL/min of hemodialysis, and a 12–14 Fr dialysis catheter can.

⚠️

Don't overclaim the "16-fold"

The 16× figure (2⁴) is the outer-diameter idealization. The true single-lumen ratio between a 7 Fr triple-lumen and a 14 Fr dual-lumen catheter is considerably greater than 16×, because one lumen of a triple is far smaller than half a lumen of a dual. Say "≥16-fold," or frame it explicitly as outer diameter.

The same law is why a long left-sided or femoral catheter pays a flow penalty for its length — and why you cannot compensate with a bigger pump. Turning up the pump against a length- or wall-limited catheter simply generates a more negative access pressure and collapses the vein onto the tip.

2.4 Red pulls, blue pushes

Red = arterial = withdrawal: blood leaves the patient here, pre-pump, at negative pressure. Blue = venous = return: blood comes back here, post-filter, at positive pressure. There is no artery involved — the names are a legacy of circuit convention, not anatomy — and this confuses learners every single time. Name it out loud once and it never confuses you again.

2.5 Recirculation and line reversal

Recirculation is freshly dialysed blood returning from the venous limb being immediately re-aspirated by the arterial limb without ever visiting the body. The patient's blood looks clean; the patient is not clean. Urea reduction ratio (URR) and Kt/V fall while the machine reports a perfect treatment. Causes: tips too close together, tip against a wall, catheter too short (a femoral not in the IVC), lines reversed, or low cardiac output.

Reversing the lines — running blood out of the blue and back into the red — is a legitimate rescue for a poorly-withdrawing catheter, but it deliberately increases recirculation, classically on the order of ~10% or more of delivered dose depending on tip design.

✅

Action

An unexplained fall in delivered Kt/V with a "working" catheter is recirculation until proven otherwise — not underdialysis. Line reversal is a bridge, not a plan: reversed lines mean the catheter is failing. Document it and start the dysfunction algorithm; do not simply reverse it again next session.

2.6 Tip position physics — why the atrium

The target is the mid-to-deep right atrium, or the cavoatrial junction, with the withdrawal port oriented away from the wall. Why not the SVC? A tip in the SVC sits in a narrow, low-flow, compliant tube; under negative pressure the wall is sucked onto the port, so the catheter "works" at low flow and fails at high flow. The atrium is a large, high-flow chamber where the tip floats free.

🔬

Attribute the tip target honestly

KDOQI 2019 contains no numbered tip-position statement. It says only: use image-guided insertion (9.1, Conditional/Moderate) and confirm the tip by alternative imaging if fluoroscopy is not used (9.2, Expert Opinion). The right-atrium target comes from KDOQI 2006 and the observational literature (Premuzic 2017; Soh 2022) — not from a 2019 statement.

2.7 Laterality — the left-sided penalty

A left-sided catheter must turn a corner: left IJ → left brachiocephalic (crossing the mediastinum) → SVC → RA. That corner is where the tip abuts the SVC wall. Left-sided catheters terminating high (SVC or cavoatrial junction) have higher dysfunction and infection rates and need to sit deeper, in the mid-to-deep RA (Premuzic 2017). But deeper is not universally better: for right-sided catheters, over-advancement into the low RA is itself associated with dysfunction (Soh 2022, "Deeper may not be better"). The Cathedia dysfunction analysis (Crit Care Med 2010) confirmed the left IJ is the bad seat — left jugular versus femoral, adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 1.89 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.12–3.21).

Part III · The Non-Tunneled Catheter

The NTHC and the Two-Week Clock

3.1 The clock

KDIGO 5.4.1 recommends initiating renal replacement therapy (RRT) in AKI via an uncuffed, non-tunneled catheter rather than a tunneled one (2D). KDOQI 10.10 then limits non-tunneled catheters to a maximum of 2 weeks, for emergent access only (Expert Opinion); KDOQI 2.11 repeats this for the IJ. Contrast KDOQI 10.11: for a TDC that is the patient's appropriate permanent access, there is no maximum time limit — but regular re-evaluation is required.

✅

Action

The day you place an NTHC, write the removal-or-conversion plan in the chart. Day 14 is not a suggestion.

3.2 Site selection — the KDIGO hierarchy

KDIGO AKI 2012, statement 5.4.2 (Not Graded), lists insertion sites in order of preference:

Note what this hierarchy is optimising for: it is not infection. It is future access preservation.

3.3 Never the subclavian — central vein stenosis

⛔

Why subclavian is forbidden in kidney disease

Subclavian catheterization causes central venous stenosis at the costoclavicular pinch-point, where the vein is repeatedly compressed between clavicle and first rib. A stenosed subclavian or brachiocephalic vein permanently jeopardises every future AV access in that arm: build a fistula downstream of a central stenosis and you get a swollen, painful, unusable arm. This is an access-preservation rationale, not an infection one. KDOQI 26.1: if asymptomatic central venous stenosis is found, do not treat it — treating it accelerates restenosis.

3.4 The 3SITES paradox — when the evidence and the guideline disagree

🔬

The infection data favour the site nephrology forbids

3SITES (Parienti, NEJM 2015; 3,471 catheters) found the subclavian had the lowest rate of CRBSI plus symptomatic deep vein thrombosis (DVT): femoral versus subclavian HR 3.5 (95% CI 1.5–7.8), jugular versus subclavian HR 2.1 (95% CI 1.0–4.3) — but the subclavian had the highest pneumothorax (1.5% vs 0.5%). Parienti's meta-analysis (Crit Care Med 2012) agreed: subclavian catheter-associated infection 1.3 versus 2.7 per 1,000 catheter-days, incidence density ratio (IDR) 0.50 (95% CI 0.33–0.74). The resolution: we are not optimising the same outcome. The intensivist protects this admission; the nephrologist protects the next fifteen years of that arm. Nephrology knowingly accepts a worse infection profile to preserve access (Clark & Barsuk, "We still go for the jugular," Kidney Int 2016). This is the intellectual core of the guide — teach it as a lesson in whose outcome you are optimising.

3.5 Cathedia — BMI and laterality

Cathedia (Parienti, JAMA 2008; n=750) compared jugular versus femoral for acute RRT. There was no overall difference in tip colonisation (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.62–1.16) or CRBSI, but more haematomas with jugular (3.6% vs 1.1%, P=0.03). The prespecified body-mass-index (BMI) interaction (P for interaction <0.001) is the memorable finding:

Bottom line

Femoral is not the infectious pariah it was taught to be. Lean patient → femoral is acceptable. Obese patient → jugular. Either way → avoid the left IJ for flow. Citation hygiene: there is no "Cathedia Lancet 2012" paper — the trio is JAMA 2008 → Crit Care Med 2010 → CJASN 2012.

3.6 Ultrasound guidance — the 1A

KDIGO 5.4.3 is the strongest recommendation in the whole topic: "We recommend using ultrasound guidance for dialysis catheter insertion. (1A)" — strong recommendation, high-quality evidence. The Cochrane review (Rabindranath 2011; 7 studies, 767 patients) showed real-time two-dimensional ultrasound significantly reduced first-attempt placement failure, arterial puncture, haematoma, and time to venipuncture. KDOQI 9.1 is softer (Conditional/Moderate) — but both recommend it, and in 2026 real-time ultrasound is the standard of care and a medicolegal expectation.

✅

Action at the probe

Vein is compressible, thin-walled, non-pulsatile. Artery is round, thick-walled, pulsatile, non-compressible. Compress before you puncture — every time.

3.7 Insertion, confirmation, and the first hour

Use a maximal sterile barrier — cap, mask, sterile gown, sterile gloves, full-body drape (not a small "central-line kit" drape). Skin antisepsis is alcohol-based chlorhexidine >0.5% (2% chlorhexidine gluconate [CHG] in 70% alcohol); let it dry — the drying time is the antisepsis. Position in Trendelenburg for the IJ, with the head turned only slightly (over-rotation collapses the IJ onto the carotid). Follow the Seldinger sequence: needle → J-wire → nick → dilator → catheter → wire out.

⛔

The wire is the most dangerous object in the room

Confirm the wire is in the vein before you dilate — ultrasound it in the long axis, or transduce pressure. A dilated carotid is a surgical emergency; a punctured carotid is not. If you dilated an artery: leave the catheter in, do NOT pull it, and call vascular surgery immediately. Watch the monitor — dysrhythmia is a wire-too-deep problem; withdraw.

KDIGO 5.4.4 (1B): obtain a chest radiograph (CXR) promptly after placement and before first use of an IJ or subclavian catheter — looking for tip position, pneumothorax, malposition (azygos, contralateral brachiocephalic, internal mammary), and kink. Femoral catheters do not need a CXR but must be long enough to reach the IVC. Electrocardiogram (ECG)-guided tip confirmation is a validated alternative to fluoroscopy but is unreliable in atrial fibrillation, paced rhythms, or absent P waves — know the rhythm before you trust the technique.

⛔

First-hour red flags

Air embolism (sudden dyspnoea, hypotension, mill-wheel murmur): clamp the line, place the patient left lateral decubitus + Trendelenburg, give 100% oxygen, call for help. Pneumothorax (dyspnoea, hypoxia, absent breath sounds): subclavian > IJ. Femoral haematoma / falling haemoglobin after a femoral stick is a retroperitoneal bleed until proven otherwise.

Part IV · The Tunneled Cuffed Catheter

Cuff, Tunnel, Exit Site — The Least-Bad Catheter

4.1 TDC anatomy and why tunnelling works

The venotomy (where the catheter enters the vein) and the exit site (where it leaves the skin) are deliberately separated by a subcutaneous tunnel that runs in the fat plane superficial to the clavicle — a tunnel cannot pass deep to the clavicle. The Dacron cuff sits in the tunnel ~2 cm from the exit site; fibroblasts grow into it over 2–4 weeks, creating a mechanical anchor and a biological barrier. Until the cuff has ingrown, the catheter is only as secure as its sutures and only as clean as an NTHC.

🔬

Two roads in, two defences

Extraluminal infection travels down the outside of the catheter from the skin — the tunnel makes that journey long, tortuous, and blocked by the cuff. Intraluminal infection travels down the inside from a contaminated hub — the tunnel does nothing about this; that is what the lock and "scrub the hub" are for. This is why a tunneled catheter is safer than a non-tunneled one (KDOQI 2.10), and why it is still the most infection-prone access we have. The cuff only guards one door.

✅

Action

A catheter that has "grown longer" at the exit site has a cuff that has pulled out of the tunnel. It is no longer protected and is at risk of migrating out entirely. Measure and document the external length at every dressing change — a lengthening catheter is a migrating cuff.

4.3 No time limit — but never unexamined

✅

Action

Put a standing item on every monthly review: "Is this still the right access? ▢ Yes ▢ No — and why." Document the answer. That single line is the difference between a chosen catheter and a forgotten one.

4.4 Exit-site care & the dressing decision

The sequence: hand hygiene → mask on patient and staff → sterile gloves → remove the old dressing peeling toward the exit site (never away, or you will pull the catheter out) → inspect → antisepsis with alcohol-based CHG >0.5% (CDC Core Intervention 7), let dry → new dressing. Inspect every time for erythema, tenderness, induration, exudate, cuff visibility, and catheter length at the skin. KDOQI 11.16 sets dressing change at clinician discretion, minimum once weekly, and always if damp, soiled, or loose; KDOQI 11.13 finds inadequate evidence to prefer transparent over non-transparent dressing (choose transparent for inspectability).

🔬

CHG dressing vs ointment — grade your confidence

CDC Core Intervention 9 applies antibiotic or povidone-iodine ointment to the exit site each dressing change (named agents: povidone-iodine, or bacitracin/gramicidin/polymyxin B — the latter not currently available in the US). Mupirocin raises resistance concerns and lacks gram-negative cover. Always check catheter-material compatibility before applying any ointment — some degrade polyurethane. The CHG-impregnated dressing is CDC's stated alternative; Timsit (JAMA 2009) showed major catheter-related infection HR 0.39 (95% CI 0.17–0.93), CRBSI HR 0.24 (95% CI 0.09–0.65), number needed to treat (NNT) 117 catheters, at a cost of severe contact dermatitis in 5.3 per 1,000 catheters. That evidence is ICU-derived; the HD-specific data (Apata 2017) is quality-improvement (QI) grade, not a randomized controlled trial (RCT).

4.5 The bundle at the chair

Every connection and disconnection is a chance to inoculate the bloodstream, and there are roughly six of them per session. The connect sequence: mask (patient and staff) → hand hygiene → sterile gloves → scrub the hub → aspirate the lock → connect. The disconnect sequence: scrub → flush → instil the lock to the exact priming volume → cap.

⛔

Aspirate the lock — never flush it in

Heparin locks are concentrated (1,000–5,000 units/mL) and high-concentration citrate locks can cause symptomatic hypocalcaemia. The lock is meant to sit in the catheter, not enter the patient. Aspirate it and the clot it holds; do not push it.

Part V · Locks, Patency & Dysfunction

The Dead Space, the Lock Menu, and the Failing Catheter

5.1 What is a lock?

Between sessions the catheter sits full of something. If that something is blood, it clots; if it is saline, blood diffuses in and clots. So we fill the dead space with a lock — a solution that prevents thrombosis and, increasingly, prevents infection. The volume you instil must equal the priming volume printed on the hub: under-fill and blood enters the tip; over-fill and you have given the patient a systemic bolus of concentrated heparin or citrate.

⚠️

Two things people get wrong about locks

KDOQI 21.4 leaves the choice between citrate and heparin to clinician judgement — there is inadequate evidence to distinguish them. And the CDC specifies no lock solution at all: its intraluminal recommendation is hub disinfection, not a named lock. There is no "CDC default lock." tPA (tissue plasminogen activator, here alteplase) appears twice — remember 1 mg weekly is prophylaxis; 2 mg per lumen is treatment (§5.5).

5.3 Citrate — it's the additive, not the citrate

🔬

The honest reading of the citrate data

Zhao (AJKD 2014; 13 RCTs, 1,770 patients, 221,064 catheter-days) found pooled CRBSI relative risk (RR) 0.39 (95% CI 0.27–0.56) favouring citrate over heparin — impressive, and misleading. The effect is driven entirely by the antimicrobial additive: citrate + gentamicin, + taurolidine, or + methylene blue all beat heparin; citrate alone did not (P=0.2). Concentration matters too: low (1.04–4%) and moderate (4.6–7%) citrate reduced CRBSI, but high-concentration (30–46.7%) citrate did not (P=0.3) — and it is the high concentration that can kill by hypocalcaemia. Citrate did reduce bleeding episodes (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.30–0.76), a real advantage in the high-bleeding-risk dialysis patient. Mai (2019) updates the same conclusion (antimicrobial-containing citrate RR 0.34, 95% CI 0.24–0.49).

📌

Bottom line

If you use citrate, use it low-concentration (<5%), and understand you are choosing it for bleeding safety, not for a proven infection benefit over heparin.

5.4 The antimicrobial locks that do work — LOCK IT-100 and PreCLOT

Taurolidine/heparin (LOCK IT-100; Agarwal, CJASN 2023). A randomised, double-blind, active-control phase 3 trial across 70 US sites, n=795 maintenance HD patients with a central venous catheter (CVC). CRBSI occurred in 9/397 (2%) on taurolidine/heparin versus 32/398 (8%) on heparin — 0.13 versus 0.46 per 1,000 catheter-days, HR 0.29 (95% CI 0.14–0.62), a 71% relative risk reduction, P<0.001 — stopped early for efficacy by the data safety monitoring board (DSMB). There was no benefit on time to catheter removal or loss of patency: it is an infection drug, not a patency drug. Taurolidine is a non-antibiotic antimicrobial and does not induce resistance — a real stewardship advantage over gentamicin locks. The FDA approved DefenCath on 15 November 2023 as the first and only approved antimicrobial catheter lock in the US.

🔬

PreCLOT — why a thrombolytic prevents infection

PreCLOT (Hemmelgarn, NEJM 2011; n=225) gave recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) 1 mg per lumen once weekly (midweek), with heparin the other two sessions, versus heparin 5,000 units/mL three times weekly. Catheter malfunction fell to 20.0% versus 34.8% (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.13–3.22, P=0.02), and — unexpectedly — catheter-related bacteraemia fell too, 4.5% versus 13.0% (P=0.02). Why would a thrombolytic prevent infection? Because the fibrin sheath is the biofilm's scaffold: dissolve the scaffold and the bacteria have nowhere to anchor. This trial is the evidence behind KDOQI 21.6 and 24.5. Citation hygiene: LOCK IT-100 is in CJASN, not "Hemodialysis"; and there is no NEJM 2023 paediatric taurolidine trial — the real intestinal-failure RCT is Tribler (Am J Clin Nutr 2017), in adults.

🔬

The honest counterweight — REDUCCTION

REDUCCTION (Kotwal, BMJ 2022), a stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial of a comprehensive HD catheter-care bundle across Australian units, did not significantly reduce CRBSI compared with baseline practice. The individual components (CHG, hub disinfection, taurolidine lock) have strong evidence, but bundling and mandating them is an implementation intervention, and implementation interventions hit ceilings. If your unit's rate is already low, a new bundle will not move it; if it is high, the bundle is not your problem — your culture is. Cite this when someone proposes a bundle as a substitute for staffing, training, or catheter reduction.

5.7 Alteplase — dose, dwell, repeat

KDOQI 22.4 prefers alteplase 2 mg per port over 1 mg; 22.5 accepts the dwell or push method; 22.3 pairs alteplase (or urokinase) with 4% citrate per limb. 5.8 The three imposters

Three entities all present as "the catheter isn't working" and have three completely different answers. The discriminating bedside question is absurdly simple: "Can you flush it but not pull back?" If yes, you are looking at a sheath.

🔬

The fibrin sheath — a one-way valve, and honestly graded

A fibrin sheath is a sleeve of fibrin, collagen, and endothelial cells that forms on the catheter within 24 hours of insertion — a foreign-body response, not a failure. On infusion, positive pressure lifts the sleeve off the port and fluid escapes; on withdrawal, suction sucks the sleeve onto the port and seals it. Hence: flushes fine, won't aspirate. Management is over-the-wire exchange ± balloon disruption (KDOQI 26.3), but KDOQI 22.7 states there is inadequate evidence on the efficacy of, or the method of, fibrin sheath disruption, and 22.6 leaves it to operator discretion — it is Expert Opinion. Do it, but do not oversell it (supportive series: Voiculescu 2022).

6.4 Scrub the hub, and 2% chlorhexidine

CDC Core Intervention 8, verbatim: "Scrub catheter hubs with an appropriate antiseptic after cap is removed and before accessing. Perform every time the catheter is accessed or disconnected." There are roughly six access events per HD session, three sessions a week — about 936 opportunities to inoculate this patient's bloodstream every year. Friction and time both matter: a wipe is not a scrub.

🔬

CLEAN — the single best citation for "2% CHG, always"

CLEAN (Mimoz, Lancet 2015; 2,349 ICU patients, 5,159 catheters including haemodialysis catheters) compared 2% chlorhexidine–70% isopropyl alcohol with 5% povidone-iodine–69% ethanol: catheter-related infection 0.28 versus 1.77 per 1,000 catheter-days, HR 0.15 (95% CI 0.05–0.41), P=0.0002 — an ~85% relative reduction. Skin scrubbing with detergent before the antiseptic conferred no benefit; stop doing it. Severe skin reactions were more frequent with chlorhexidine (3% vs 1%) — real, but a fair trade. KDOQI 11.12, honestly, finds inadequate evidence to recommend a specific chlorhexidine formulation.

6.6 The usual suspects — and the salvage decision

⛔

Memorise the three that mandate removal

Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas species, and Candida species — the catheter comes out, always (IDSA Rec. 57, A-II). S. aureus seeds tricuspid endocarditis, epidural abscess, septic arthritis, and discitis, so examine the spine and get an echocardiogram; Candida gets an ophthalmology review. Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) — the commonest, often indolent, frequently salvageable. Gram-negative bacilli other than Pseudomonas — may be salvageable.

6.6a The IDSA salvage decision tree flag: guideline is 17 years old

IDSA 2009 remains the operative CRBSI guideline in 2026 — cite it, but flag its age.

Removal is mandatory when…

Rec. 57 (A-II): HD CRBSI due to S. aureus, Pseudomonas, or Candida — always remove, and place a temporary catheter at a different site. If no alternative site exists → exchange over a guidewire (B-II). Rec. 32: also remove for severe sepsis, suppurative thrombophlebitis, endocarditis, bacteraemia persisting >72 h on susceptible antimicrobials, or mycobacteria.

Salvage may be attempted when…

Rec. 59 (B-II): for other pathogens (CoNS, non-Pseudomonas gram-negatives) you may start empirical IV antibiotics without immediate removal. If symptoms and bacteraemia resolve within 2–3 days with no metastatic infection → exchange over a guidewire for a new tunneled catheter (B-II). Rec. 60 (B-II): alternatively, retain the catheter and add an antibiotic lock as adjunctive therapy after each dialysis session for 10–14 days.

The antibiotic-lock rules

Rec. 68 (B-II): antibiotic lock only for a long-term catheter with no exit-site or tunnel infection, where salvage is the goal. Rec. 69 (B-II): never use it alone — always with systemic therapy, both for 7–14 days. Rec. 70 (B-II): dwell ≤48 h; in HD, renew the lock after every session. Why a lock at all? Systemic antibiotics do not penetrate biofilm at meaningful concentrations, whereas the lock delivers a concentration inside the lumen orders of magnitude above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Why it fails: if the infection is extraluminal (tunnel, exit), the lock is on the wrong side of the wall — which is exactly why Rec. 68 excludes tunnel infection.

Follow-up

Rec. 67 (B-III): if you retained the catheter, draw surveillance blood cultures one week after finishing antibiotics — positive → remove it. KDOQI 25.2 is deliberately softer: individualise, especially if this catheter is the patient's final access.

6.7 Blood cultures without burning veins

✅

Part VII · CRRT Circuit Access

Reading the Circuit as a Fault-Localising Map

7.1 CRRT vs IHD — what the circuit demands

Intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) asks the catheter to work hard, briefly: 300–450 mL/min for 3–4 hours, three times a week. CRRT asks it to work gently, forever: ~150–250 mL/min (commonly 100–250) continuously, for days. The paradox is that CRRT tolerates a marginal catheter longer than IHD does, because it asks for less flow — but stasis and clotting are far bigger problems, because the blood is in the circuit continuously. So a catheter that "passed" on IHD can still destroy filters on CRRT: do not reassure yourself with the pump speed. The CRRT tip target is the cavoatrial junction or the IVC (Juncos, Semin Dial 2021).

7.4 Filter life and access site — the inversion

🔬

For CRRT specifically, femoral may win

Crosswell (Crit Care Resusc 2014; 131 patients, 191 access episodes, 870 filters) measured mean filter run times by site: subclavian 14.4 h < internal jugular 17.1 h < femoral 20.2 h < tunnelled IJ 25.2 h < extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) circuit 29.0 h. The authors concluded that for temporary dialysis catheters the femoral site should be favoured in ICU patients, and if CRRT is likely to continue for an extended period, a tunnelled IJ line should be considered. This inverts the standard HD teaching. Be honest about the grade: this is retrospective observational data. The randomised counterpoint is Cathedia (Crit Care Med 2010), which found CRRT downtime identical between femoral and jugular (median 1.17 h per patient-day, P=0.98). And remember Part I: even if femoral wins on filter life, it still costs you vein.

7.5 The CRRT troubleshooting algorithm

7.5 Alarm → read which pressure → localise → act

Access alarm (too negative)

Check clamps and kinks → check volume status (is the patient dry?) → reposition the patient → reduce blood flow rate (buys time, costs dose) → reverse the lines (costs recirculation) → alteplase → image/exchange.

Return alarm (too positive)

TMP rising

The filter is clogging → check anticoagulation → check the filtration fraction (FF, keep <20–25%) → prepare to change the filter — but first check the access-pressure trend (§7.3).

Remember: downtime is dose. Every hour the circuit is clotted is an hour of clearance the patient did not receive. Track delivered dose on the flowsheet, not prescribed, and track circuit life — a unit that does not know its mean circuit life cannot improve it.

7.6 Anticoagulation cannot rescue a bad catheter

Regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) infuses citrate pre-filter to chelate ionised calcium (iCa), anticoagulating the circuit only; calcium is replaced post-filter, restoring the patient's coagulation. Monitor circuit iCa (target low, ~0.25–0.35 mmol/L) and systemic iCa (target normal) — two different numbers with two different targets; confusing them is the classic error. Citrate accumulation (in hepatic failure or shock) shows a rising total-calcium-to-ionised-calcium ratio (>2.5) with a widening anion gap. But the point of this page: if the access pressure is screaming, no amount of citrate will save the filter. Fix the plumbing first.

Part VIII · The Peritoneal Dialysis Catheter

A Different Organ, A Different Catheter

8.1 The Tenckhoff catheter

The PD catheter is not a vascular device and it does not fail like one. It has no bloodstream to infect and no vein to stenose — it has a peritoneum to inflame and a pelvis to migrate out of. Tenckhoff anatomy runs: intraperitoneal segment (with side-holes) → deep cuff (in the rectus sheath / preperitoneal space) → intramural segment → superficial cuff (~2–3 cm from the exit site) → external segment → titanium adapter → transfer set. The tip target is the pelvis — the rectovesical or rectouterine (Douglas) pouch. The exit site should point downward or laterally; an upward-facing exit collects debris and moisture and gets infected. Network meta-analysis (Zhao, PDI 2025) suggests straight-tip designs have lower mechanical dysfunction and migration.

8.2 Placement and break-in

ISPD 2019 (Crabtree): insert through the rectus muscle — this reduces leak and hernia and avoids the inferior epigastric vessels; a paramedian insertion outside the rectus sheath leaks. Deep cuff in the preperitoneal space / rectus sheath; superficial cuff ~2–3 cm from the exit. Laparoscopic, open, percutaneous (Seldinger), and fluoroscopy-assisted approaches are all acceptable in experienced hands; laparoscopy permits adjuncts — adhesiolysis and omentopexy, which reduces migration and outflow failure. Give a single pre-procedure dose of prophylactic antibiotic (e.g., cefazolin), and leave the initial dressing undisturbed for 5–7 days.

💡

The clock runs the other way

ISPD 2019 (1B): a break-in period of at least 2 weeks before elective PD start — the cuffs need to ingrow and the wound needs to seal. If urgent start at <2 weeks is unavoidable (1C): a modified prescription with low-volume, supine exchanges (both reduce the intra-abdominal pressure that drives leak and hernia). In HD you count down to removal (≤2 weeks); in PD you count up to safe use (≥2-week break-in).

8.3 Infection — exit, tunnel, peritonitis

🔬

The 2023 downgrade — a "known fact" that expired

For years the teaching was daily topical antibiotic (mupirocin or gentamicin) to every PD exit site (Bernardini, JASN 2005). The ISPD 2023 Catheter-related Infection Recommendations downgraded routine topical exit-site antibiotic to a context-specific recommendation, because resistance emerged with routine use — including gentamicin resistance. Do not present daily mupirocin or gentamicin as a strong universal recommendation in 2026: it is now a decision, not a default, based on your unit's organisms. This is the clearest example in the guide of checking the date on your knowledge.

Exit-site infection: purulent discharge at the exit, with or without erythema (purulent drainage alone is sufficient; erythema alone may be benign). Tunnel infection: erythema, oedema, or tenderness over the subcutaneous tunnel — often occult; ultrasound it if you suspect it. ISPD 2023 sets an exit-site infection target of ≤0.40 episodes per patient-year and adds two catheter-preserving options before removal: external cuff removal/shaving and exit-site relocation.

⛔

Peritonitis — ISPD 2022

Diagnosis requires ≥2 of 3: (1) clinical features (abdominal pain and/or cloudy effluent); (2) effluent white blood cell (WBC) count >100/µL after a dwell ≥2 h with >50% polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMN); (3) positive effluent culture. Cloudy bag + abdominal pain → send the bag and start empirical intraperitoneal antibiotics; do not wait for culture. Targets: overall peritonitis ≤0.40 episodes per patient-year, and >80% of patients peritonitis-free per year. Enteric peritonitis (polymicrobial, gut organisms, anaerobes) means look for a surgical abdomen — that is the one that kills.

8.4 Mechanical failure — and when the catheter must come out

The four mechanical failures: leak (early, usually a break-in violation or paramedian insertion — stop PD, rest the abdomen); hernia (driven by intra-abdominal pressure — reduce fill volume, repair before it incarcerates); migration (the tip floats out of the pelvis, classically from constipation — a laxative fixes more migrated catheters than a laparoscope does); and outflow failure (differential: constipation, migration, omental wrap, fibrin/clot, kink). The commonest cause of a "blocked" PD catheter is a full rectum — check the abdominal film and the bowel habit before you refer for surgery.

📌

The principle — save the membrane, not the catheter

Remove the PD catheter for refractory peritonitis (no clearing after 5 days of appropriate antibiotics), relapsing peritonitis (same organism within 4 weeks), fungal peritonitis (immediately), or a refractory exit-site/tunnel infection. In HD you remove the catheter to save the patient; in PD you remove it to save the membrane. The peritoneum is the organ; the catheter is disposable. Never trade the membrane for the catheter.

Part IX · Synthesis, Cases & Quick Reference

The Catheter Is a Cardiovascular Decision

9.1 Cross-organ synthesis

The catheter is not a plumbing problem in a plumbing chapter. It sits at the intersection of the kidney, the heart, the immune system, and nutrition:

  • Catheter → right atrium → endocardium. An S. aureus CRBSI seeds tricuspid endocarditis, epidural abscess, discitis, and septic arthritis. The catheter is a direct conduit to the endocardium — which is why S. aureus mandates removal and why you examine the spine.
  • Catheter → central vein stenosis → the arm. A stenosed central vein makes a fistula unusable, which makes the catheter permanent, which makes the stenosis worse — a closed causal loop, and how patients end up catheter-dependent for life.
  • Catheter dependence → chronic inflammation. Persistent exposure raises C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), drives protein-energy wasting, erythropoietin (EPO) hyporesponsiveness, and vascular calcification. The catheter is an inflammatory organ, not just an infection risk.
  • Catheter → inadequate dose → uraemia → cardiovascular death. A dysfunctional catheter delivering Kt/V 1.0 instead of 1.4 is not an inconvenience; it is a slow cardiovascular event.

9.2 Three bedside cases

Case 1 — The catheter that flushes but won't pull. A 68-year-old on maintenance HD via a right IJ TDC, in situ 14 months, can be flushed easily on both lumens but cannot achieve a blood flow above 180 mL/min. Alteplase 2 mg last week gave brief improvement, then recurrence. No fever, exit site clean. What is going on, and what does KDOQI honestly say about the next step?
The discriminating feature is that it infuses but will not withdraw — the one-way valve. The diagnosis is a fibrin sheath. The next step is over-the-wire catheter exchange ± balloon disruption of the sheath (KDOQI 26.3) — but KDOQI 22.7 explicitly states there is inadequate evidence for the efficacy or the method of sheath disruption. Do it, but do not oversell it.
Case 2 — Rigors at the start of dialysis. A 54-year-old with a TDC develops rigors and a temperature of 38.9 °C twenty minutes into her session; the exit site looks normal. Circuit blood cultures grow S. aureus at 14 hours. Can this catheter be salvaged with an antibiotic lock, and what else must you do?
The rigors started when the pump started because the pump flushed organisms off the intraluminal biofilm into the circulation — rigors at the start of dialysis are a positive blood culture until proven otherwise. No, it cannot be salvaged: IDSA Rec. 57 (A-II) mandates removal for S. aureus, plus a temporary catheter at a different site. Also get an echocardiogram (tricuspid endocarditis) and examine the spine — S. aureus seeds it.
Case 3 — The filter that keeps clotting. A patient on continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) via a left IJ 12 Fr catheter is on his third filter in 18 hours; each time the TMP climbs before the circuit clots, and the nurse has been increasing the citrate. The access pressure has run at −230 mmHg throughout, uncommented. Where is the primary lesion?
The catheter, on the withdrawal side. An access pressure of −230 mmHg is the machine telling you the pump cannot pull; low flow → stasis → the filter clots. The filter is the victim, not the culprit — anticoagulation cannot rescue a bad catheter, so fix the plumbing first. Two structural predictors: it is left-sided (the angulated course and Cathedia's worst dysfunction seat, adjusted HR 1.89) and only 12 Fr (flow ∝ radius⁴); per Crosswell, a tunnelled IJ line should have been considered for extended CRRT.

9.3 Red flags — call now

⛔

Escalate immediately

  • Rigors or fever at the start of dialysis → blood cultures now; assume CRBSI.
  • Sudden dyspnoea, hypotension, mill-wheel murmur during line manipulation → air embolism. Clamp the line, left lateral decubitus + Trendelenburg, 100% oxygen, call for help.
  • Bright, pulsatile, high-pressure blood on insertion after you have already dilated → arterial injury. Leave the catheter in; do not pull it; call vascular surgery.
  • New ipsilateral arm, face, or breast swelling → central vein stenosis or thrombosis. Image it.
  • Purulent discharge or induration tracking along the tunnel → tunnel infection. The catheter comes out.
  • Falling haemoglobin after a femoral stick → retroperitoneal bleed until proven otherwise.
  • Cloudy PD effluent + abdominal pain → send the bag, start intraperitoneal antibiotics, do not wait for culture.

Terms

Cavoatrial junction
Where the superior vena cava meets the right atrium — the shallow end of the acceptable tip zone.
Central vein stenosis
Narrowing of a central vein (classically after subclavian cannulation) that jeopardises all downstream arm access.
Costoclavicular pinch-point
The bony space between clavicle and first rib where a subclavian catheter repeatedly compresses and scars the vein.
Dialysis Event
The NHSN outpatient-HD reporting construct — an IV antimicrobial start, a positive blood culture, or pus/redness/swelling at the access.
Douglas pouch
The rectovesical/rectouterine pelvic recess — the PD catheter's tip target.
Dacron cuff
The felt collar on a tunneled catheter that fibroblasts grow into over 2–4 weeks, anchoring it and blocking extraluminal infection.
Effluent
In PD, the drained dialysate examined for cloudiness and cell count when peritonitis is suspected.
Fibrin sheath
A sleeve of fibrin and cells forming on the catheter within 24 h; acts as a one-way valve (infuses, won't withdraw) and scaffolds biofilm.
Hagen–Poiseuille
The physical law by which flow scales with radius⁴ and inversely with length — why bore, not effort, sets flow.
Kt/V
A dimensionless measure of delivered dialysis dose (clearance × time ÷ volume of distribution).
Lock solution
The fluid instilled into the catheter dead space between sessions to prevent thrombosis and, increasingly, infection.
Omentopexy
Surgical fixation of the omentum at PD-catheter placement to prevent omental wrap and outflow failure.
Priming volume
The internal volume of a catheter lumen, printed on the hub — the exact volume of lock to instil and to aspirate.
Recirculation
Freshly dialysed return blood re-aspirated by the arterial limb without reaching the body — lowers effective dose.
Seldinger technique
The guidewire-based method for catheter insertion (needle → wire → dilator → catheter).
Tenckhoff catheter
The standard peritoneal-dialysis catheter, with intraperitoneal side-holes and one or two Dacron cuffs.
Taurolidine
A non-antibiotic antimicrobial lock agent that does not induce resistance; the active component of DefenCath.
Venotomy
The point where a tunneled catheter enters the vein, separated by the tunnel from the skin exit site.
