Clinical Reference · Sustainability · Dialysis Units

Green Nephrology — reducing the footprint of kidney care without compromising it.

Hemodialysis, hemodiafiltration, and peritoneal dialysis are among the most resource-intensive therapies in medicine — on the order of 400–500 L of water per HD session and an estimated 5–7 tonnes CO₂e per in-center patient-year. In a high-burden, climate-exposed health system, lowering the water, energy, and plastic footprint of dialysis is both an environmental and a resilience imperative. This is a practical reference for nephrologists, dialysis-unit managers, and trainees, aligned with the 2026 KDIGO Green Dialysis report and the ERA 2026 Glasgow agenda.

The Clinical & System Case

Hemodialysis is among the most resource-intensive treatments in medicine, delivered in one of the most climate-exposed countries on Earth.

The Philippine dialysis burden is large and rising, driven predominantly by diabetes and hypertension, delivered mainly through outpatient hemodialysis (HD) centers, and funded substantially through PhilHealth. Set against this is acute climate exposure — typhoons, El Niño heat, brownouts, and water rationing. The same forces that endanger patients render a water- and power-intensive therapy operationally fragile, so greening dialysis functions simultaneously as disaster resilience.

Water intensity

Generating ultra-pure dialysis water requires reverse-osmosis (RO) purification, which rejects a large fraction of feed water directly to drain. A single machine can discharge hundreds of liters per session.

Energy intensity

Machines, water heating, and air-conditioning run for hours each session. This electrical demand leaves units vulnerable to brownouts; loss of power mid-treatment is a direct patient-safety threat.

Consumable waste

Bloodlines, dialyzers, fluid bags, gloves, and packaging are predominantly single-use. Each session generates a substantial volume of plastic and PVC waste requiring safe disposal, multiplied across thousands of sessions daily nationwide.

🌱

Greening as operational resilience

Most green interventions — treated-water storage, solar with battery backup, waste reduction — also harden a unit against the next typhoon, brownout, or water shortage. The disaster-resilience dimension is developed in the companion guide El Niño, Extreme Heat & Dialysis.

Clinical Prioritization

The greenest kidney care is the kidney failure that never occurs — a hierarchy from prevention to resilience.

Impact is not evenly distributed across green interventions. Recycling a single bloodline is negligible beside preventing diabetic kidney disease at the source. The levers below are ordered from highest to lowest environmental yield; upstream prevention dominates.

  1. PREVENT — interrupt CKD onset and slow established progression. This conserves the greatest carbon, water, and cost.
  2. CONSERVE — conservative kidney management (supportive care without dialysis) is an appropriate, dignified option for selected patients and carries a near-negligible environmental footprint.
  3. TRANSPLANT — successful transplantation is the lowest-carbon long-term kidney-replacement therapy, displacing years of water- and power-intensive dialysis.
  4. HOME / PD — peritoneal dialysis (PD) and home HD ("PD-First") reduce travel, plant load, and per-patient in-center footprint.
  5. GREEN IN-CENTER HD — apply the 5 R's (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Rethink, Research) within in-center HD, where most Filipino patients are actually treated.
  6. RESILIENCE — climate- and disaster-proof the unit so care continues safely through typhoons, heat, brownouts, and water shortages.
🪜

Each stakeholder has a tier of action

Policymakers, unit managers, and clinicians each operate at a different level of this hierarchy. Acting decisively at the relevant tier matters more than attempting all six.

Tier 1 — Prevent

Prevention is the highest-yield environmental lever — every progression delayed is tonnes of CO₂ and cost never spent on dialysis.

Diabetes and hypertension drive the Philippine dialysis epidemic. Early detection, optimized blood-pressure and glycemic control, and kidney-protective pharmacotherapy keep patients off the highest-footprint therapy. Prevention is climate action delivered at the level of the clinic.

Early detection

eGFR and urine ACR screening identify CKD before symptom onset, when progression is most modifiable. Prioritize screening in diabetic and hypertensive populations.

BP and glycemic control

Achieving guideline BP and glycemic targets remains the single highest-impact intervention for renal preservation and dialysis avoidance.

RAAS blockade & SGLT2 inhibitors

RAAS blockade (ACE inhibitor or ARB) plus SGLT2 inhibitors meaningfully slow CKD progression across diabetic and non-diabetic etiologies, and should be initiated where indicated.

GLP-1 receptor agonists

GLP-1 receptor agonists offer additional cardiorenal and metabolic benefit in selected patients with diabetes and obesity; add where clinically indicated.

Avoid nephrotoxins

Minimize unnecessary NSAID exposure and unverified herbal preparations, which remain frequent and avoidable causes of acute and chronic kidney injury.

Predominantly plant-based nutrition

A predominantly plant-based, kidney-appropriate diet aligns with the KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline and reduces proteinuria and metabolic acidosis while slowing progression — and carries a markedly lower environmental footprint than meat-heavy patterns. Titrate with dietetic support to keep potassium and phosphorus within range.

The companion guides Slowing CKD Progression and Diabetes & the Kidneys develop each lever in depth.

🩺

Dialysis avoided is the dominant environmental and clinical win

Systematic screening and timely initiation of kidney-protective therapy keep patients off the highest-footprint therapy. No downstream green intervention rivals prevention for combined renal and environmental benefit.

Tiers 2–4 — Conserve, Transplant, Home

Lower-footprint kidney-replacement therapy — why transplantation and home dialysis carry a lighter environmental burden.

The environmental footprint of kidney-replacement options differs enormously. Modality selection is a clinical and patient-centered decision first, yet several lower-footprint options also align well with Philippine geography and logistics.

Conservative kidney management

Supportive care without dialysis — the lowest-footprint pathway and a valid, dignified option for selected frail or elderly patients.

Transplantation

The lowest-carbon long-term kidney-replacement therapy, though donor access remains limited in the Philippines.

Peritoneal dialysis & "PD-First"

Home-based, with substantially reduced patient travel and associated emissions — well matched to an archipelago of 7,000+ islands and remote provinces.

Home hemodialysis

For selected patients — fewer center transfers and a reduced travel-emission footprint.

Incremental & decremental dialysis

Personalized dosing — initiating with fewer or shorter sessions while residual kidney function persists (incremental), and de-escalating dose when clinically safe (decremental) — can reduce water, power, and consumable burden per the KDIGO Green Dialysis framework, with incremental PD showing comparable survival. This is a deliberate, outcome-preserving clinical strategy and must be distinguished sharply from resource-constrained under-dialysis, which is not a green strategy and compromises outcomes.

🩺

Outcomes first

Modality is a clinical and patient-centered decision. A lower-footprint option is appropriate only when it fits the individual patient; environmental considerations never override adequacy or safety.

Operational Framework — In-Center HD

The 5 R's — operationalizing footprint reduction without compromising safety or adequacy.

The majority of patients receive in-center HD, making it the principal site for sustainability intervention. A five-part framework structures unit-level action.

REDUCE

Right-size prescriptions, eliminate low-value tests and imaging, default to LED lighting, deploy efficient HVAC, and minimize paper workflows.

REUSE

Reclaim reverse-osmosis (RO) reject water for cleaning and grounds maintenance; recover transport packaging.

RECYCLE

Segregate clean plastic, paper, and cardboard from clinical waste streams.

RETHINK / RESEARCH

Expand home therapies and telehealth, adopt on-site solar, implement greener procurement, and measure the unit's carbon footprint.

REFUSE

Decline unnecessary single-use items and excess packaging at the point of procurement.

Default dialysate flow to Qd 400 ml/min (REDUCE)

The 2026 KDIGO report found minimal clinical difference between Qd 700, 500, and 400 ml/min with modern dialyzers. Units should therefore default to Qd 400 ml/min: each step up adds tens of liters of dialysate — and corresponding source water and energy — per session. Lowering Qd is among the highest-yield, lowest-cost greening levers available in-center.

✅

Sterility and adequacy are never compromised

None of the 5 R's — including the Qd default — touches sterility or dialysis adequacy. Clinical safety and prescribed treatment targets remain the overriding constraints; sustainability measures are layered on top and must be verified against delivered Kt/V and water-quality standards.

Water Reclamation

RO reject reclamation and rainwater harvesting — the highest-yield water intervention for HD units.

Producing ultra-pure dialysis water requires RO systems that reject a substantial fraction of feed water, typically discharged directly to drain. In settings subject to recurrent water rationing, capturing and reusing this stream is the most locally relevant intervention and confers operational resilience.

Capture the RO reject

Store in dedicated tanks for non-potable applications: toilet flushing, floor and equipment cleaning, landscaping, cooling, and laundry.

Harvest rainwater

Abundant in a typhoon climate; suitable for the same non-potable applications.

Resilience

On-site storage and reuse allow continued operation through rationing and supply interruptions.

Circuit isolation

Reclaimed water is strictly non-potable and never enters the treatment circuit; ultra-pure water contacting the dialysis pathway is never reused.

💧

Established practice, not experimental

Units in Australia (Geelong, Agar) have reused RO reject water for years, and the 2026 KDIGO report recommends that reuse be universally considered. RO systems reject approximately 20–80% of feed water, and one UK unit saved about 4,492,000 L/yr through reclamation. Water-scarce settings have correspondingly greater incentive to adopt it.

Unit-level water-reclamation actions

  • Install an RO-reject capture tank.
  • Clearly label non-potable taps and outlets.
  • Add rainwater-harvesting barrels.
  • Train staff on safe non-potable reuse protocols.
  • Meter and record water consumption per session.
Energy

On-site solar and continuity power — decarbonization and operational resilience combined.

High year-round insolation favors on-site generation, while grid instability threatens dialysis continuity. On-site clean generation addresses both the environmental footprint and treatment-continuity risk.

Rooftop solar PV

Offsets daytime load from HD machines and HVAC.

Battery / UPS continuity

Maintains supply through brownouts and prevents mid-session treatment aborts.

Efficiency

LED lighting, efficient HVAC, heat recovery on water heating and RO, demand-driven RO permeate production, and equipment standby modes.

Resilience

On-site generation and storage sustain operation through outages (see the El Niño & Extreme Heat guide).

⚡

Continuity power is a safety essential, not solely a green measure

Power loss during dialysis carries clinical risk. Solar paired with battery/UPS storage addresses decarbonization and continuity simultaneously.

Waste & Procurement

Plastic, waste, and procurement — reducing inbound volume and segregating outbound streams.

HD generates a large single-use plastic and PVC stream. Much of it — boxes, wrap, paper — is never patient-contacting and can be managed more cleanly within DOH and DENR medical-waste regulations.

Segregate clean recyclables

Separate uncontaminated plastic, paper, and cardboard from clinical (infectious) waste, within DOH/DENR rules.

Greener procurement

Bulk and central acid-concentrate supply, package right-sizing, reduced packaging, and shortened supply chains.

Refuse the unnecessary

Decline unnecessary single-use items and excess packaging at procurement.

Safe, regulated reuse

Adopt reuse only where permitted by regulation and demonstrated to be safe.

⚠

Infection control and medical-waste regulations are non-negotiable

Only genuinely clean, non-clinical material is recyclable. Segregation must be performed correctly by trained staff and must never breach infection-control or medical-waste requirements.

Telenephrology

Telenephrology across an archipelago — cutting travel emissions for patients and clinicians.

Across 7,000+ islands, patient travel to specialist centers imposes substantial cost and carbon. Tele-consultation reduces both patient and clinician travel while extending specialist reach into remote provinces.

Stable-CKD tele-follow-up

Routine surveillance of stable CKD without long-distance referral to a tertiary center.

PD telesupport

Remote monitoring and coaching of home peritoneal-dialysis patients.

Reduced travel burden

Fewer in-person visits lower transport-related emissions, patient out-of-pocket cost, and lost work time.

Access for remote provinces

Specialist input delivered to island and rural populations distant from any nephrology center.

💻

The principle applied at scale

The 2026 ERA Congress in Glasgow was itself delivered as a hybrid, livestreamed event expressly to reduce travel-related carbon — the same logic that supports telenephrology in routine practice.

Global Precedent

Established international practice — these interventions are proven, not theoretical.

Each recommendation in this guide has a working international precedent. The task for the Philippines is localization, not invention.

United Kingdom — Green Nephrology programme

Delivered by the Centre for Sustainable Healthcare, the first specialty-wide sustainability initiative in medicine — driven by clinical champions and an awards scheme, and now aligned with the NHS net-zero target.

Australia (Geelong) — RO reject reuse, solar dialysis, Green Dialysis Survey

Work by Agar and Barraclough on RO reject-water reclamation, solar-assisted dialysis, and the free Green Dialysis Survey (GDS) self-assessment scorecard — the most directly transferable toolkit for Philippine units.

International Society of Nephrology

The ISN links kidney health, climate, and equity, with explicit emphasis on lower-resource settings.

ERA 2026 Glasgow — sustainability on the main stage

The European Renal Association's 63rd Congress (Glasgow, 3–6 June 2026, theme "Open your mind") placed sustainability on the main stage and modeled it: catering with no single-use plastics, a hybrid livestreamed format to reduce travel carbon, and promotion of rail travel — cited as saving roughly 85% of emissions versus flying. Its sessions took up the same green-dialysis themes as the KDIGO report — prevention, water and energy savings, personalized and incremental dialysis, and reduced plastic — evidencing a converging global agenda.

KDIGO 2026 — Green Dialysis report

KDIGO convened a Controversies Conference on Green Dialysis (Berlin 2025; report published 2026). Its conclusions align: earlier detection and slowing of kidney disease, plant-based dietary patterns, dialysis matched to individual need, and reduction of water, energy, and plastic use — with a call for broad international adoption.

🌍

Implication for units

The major international kidney organizations now treat environmental sustainability as core nephrology rather than a peripheral concern. A Philippine unit can begin immediately by completing the free Green Dialysis Survey.

Implementation

A phased implementation roadmap for a Philippine dialysis unit — low-cost first.

A practical, phased plan for any unit, sequenced from the lowest-cost, highest-value interventions.

  1. Phase 0 — Measure & champion: appoint a sustainability lead, baseline water, energy, and waste, and complete the Green Dialysis Survey.
  2. Phase 1 — Zero/low-cost wins: LED lighting, elimination of low-value labs and imaging, waste segregation, refusal of excess disposables, and staff education.
  3. Phase 2 — Water: RO-reject capture and rainwater harvesting for non-potable reuse — the highest local payoff.
  4. Phase 3 — Energy: rooftop solar with battery/UPS to prevent brownout-related treatment interruption.
  5. Phase 4 — System & policy: advocacy for PD-First, telenephrology build-out, PhilHealth alignment, and primary-care partnership on prevention.
📊

"You can't improve what you don't measure."

Re-run the Green Dialysis Survey annually, select 2–3 targets, and track water (liters), energy (kWh), and waste per session.

Counseling and Engaging Patients

Counseling and engaging patients.

Patient-facing actions are real levers, and the clinical encounter is where they are activated. Prioritizing prevention, framing modality choice within shared decision making that now includes environmental considerations, and reducing avoidable travel together compound the unit-level gains.

Prioritize prevention and kidney protection — the single biggest lever

Reinforce adherence to kidney-protective therapy and tight control of glycemia, blood pressure, salt intake, and weight. Preventing or delaying progression to dialysis is the highest-yield environmental and clinical intervention available.

Offer home and PD options in shared decision making

Present peritoneal dialysis and home therapies routinely, framing modality choice within shared decision making that now incorporates environmental considerations alongside clinical and lifestyle factors. These options substantially reduce recurring patient and family travel.

Reduce avoidable travel

Support transport pooling and shared rides for in-center patients, and consolidate appointments and remote follow-up where clinically appropriate, to cut both cost and travel-related emissions.

Advise on waste reduction

Encourage reusable bags and bottles and minimizing avoidable single-use plastic in the home setting as a complement to unit-level waste measures.

Enable patient advocacy

Welcome patient questions about water reuse and renewable energy at the center, and use them to reinforce that sustainability measures are additive and never compromise treatment safety.

🌱

Small actions compound

Preventing or delaying dialysis remains the greenest and healthiest outcome that counseling can influence.

Common Questions and Objections

Common questions and objections.

Frequently raised concerns from clinicians, managers, and trainees, with the evidence-based responses.

Does green dialysis compromise safety?

No. Sterility and dialysis adequacy are non-negotiable and always take precedence; sustainability measures are strictly additive. There is no expected effect on treatment quality.

Won't it increase cost?

Many measures are cost-saving through reduced water, energy, and low-value testing. Capital items such as solar and water-recovery systems carry an upfront cost but deliver a defined payback period and add disaster-resilience value.

Can reclaimed water contact the circuit?

No. Reclaimed water is strictly non-potable and restricted to uses such as cleaning, sanitation, and grounds. It never contacts the treatment circuit; the ultrapure water pathway is never reused.

Where should a unit start?

Measure first to establish a baseline, name a sustainability champion, run the Green Dialysis Survey to benchmark and prioritize, and implement low-cost wins before major capital investment.

For Clinicians · The Case

The clinical and system case for sustainable nephrology.

Kidney replacement therapy is among the most resource-intensive of all chronic treatments. In-center hemodialysis in particular carries a large water, energy, and consumables footprint — three to four sessions per week, each requiring purified water on the order of hundreds of liters, single-use bloodlines and dialyzers, and continuous machine and facility power. The highest-yield environmental intervention is therefore upstream: preventing CKD and slowing its progression, so that fewer patients ever reach dialysis. Where KRT is needed, the modality hierarchy — conservative/supportive care, transplantation, home therapies and peritoneal dialysis, then in-center HD — maps closely onto carbon intensity, with the most facility- and travel-dependent option also being the most carbon-intensive.

A green hierarchy for kidney care

A practical ordering of effort runs Prevent → Conserve → Transplant → Home/PD → Green in-center HD → Resilience. Prevention and conservative (supportive) care avoid the footprint entirely or defer it; transplantation offers the lowest long-term carbon among replacement options once the perioperative phase is past; home therapies and PD reduce both facility energy and — importantly in an archipelagic setting — recurring patient-travel emissions, since they remove three weekly round-trips to a center. Greening the in-center HD that remains, and building resilience against climate-related disruption (heat, flooding, power loss), then completes the ladder.

What the 2026 KDIGO Green Dialysis report adds

The KDIGO Controversies Conference on Green Dialysis: Environmentally Sustainable Care, Growth, and Innovation (Barraclough KA et al.; Berlin, April 2025; Kidney International, 2026; doi:10.1016/j.kint.2026.01.015) reinforces this ordering. It evaluates dialysis through a life-cycle-assessment lens and a "triple bottom line" of environmental, social, and financial impact, framing hemodialysis, hemodiafiltration, and PD as among the most resource-intensive therapies in medicine. It concludes that the largest gains come from detecting kidney disease earlier and slowing progression, supported by more plant-based nutrition. For patients who reach dialysis, it emphasizes personalizing dose and frequency — incremental starts (fewer or shorter sessions while residual kidney function persists; cited studies report survival on incremental PD comparable to standard practice) and decremental dialysis (planned dose/frequency reduction in settings such as kidney-function recovery or palliative care) — alongside water reuse, renewable energy, and reduced single-use materials. The report notes that green-dialysis initiatives remain variably implemented across the UK, EU, and Australia, and calls for wider adoption together with new technologies and care models.

Two cautions from the report matter especially in the Philippines. First, personalized changes in dialysis frequency or duration are framed as a deliberate, patient-centered choice that must maintain or improve outcomes — explicitly distinguished from low-dose regimens driven by resource constraints, which represent a compromise in care, not a green strategy. Second, the report frames sustainability as a matter of equity, asking how dialysis access in lower- and middle-income countries (LMIC) can be expanded without worsening environmental injustice — directly relevant to a high-burden, climate-exposed setting. Its nutrition guidance aligns with the KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline, which favors varied, predominantly plant-based dietary patterns and minimizing ultra-processed foods.

📊

Treat these numbers as estimates, and measure locally first

Every figure above is a literature estimate and varies widely by setting — water recovery rates, grid carbon intensity, dialyzer reuse policy, and travel distances all shift the totals. Local measurement is itself the first intervention: establish a baseline for water, energy, and waste per session before claiming any savings, and verify against local utility and procurement data rather than published averages.

For Clinicians · Operations

The unit manager's green checklist.

Operationalizing sustainability means translating the 5 R's — Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Rethink/Research, and Refuse — into concrete levers, each paired with a metric and a self-assessment scorecard. Reduce consumption and low-value care; reuse and reclaim where safe; recycle clean non-clinical waste; rethink prescriptions and pathways; and refuse unnecessary packaging and single-use items. Quick-start operational checklist

  • Name a sustainability lead with a clear mandate and time allocation.
  • Baseline current water, energy, and waste per session before any intervention.
  • Run the Green Dialysis Survey (GDS) self-assessment to benchmark and prioritize.
  • Install RO-reject capture and route reclaimed water to non-potable uses.
  • Default dialysate flow to Qd 400 ml/min where clearance targets allow.
  • Pilot rooftop solar paired with battery/UPS to cover load and bridge outages.
  • Segregate non-clinical recyclables within DOH/DENR medical-waste rules.
  • Review prescriptions and the frequency of routine tests and imaging for low-value care.
♻

Safety and water quality are non-negotiable

Reclaimed and harvested water is strictly non-potable and never enters the treatment circuit: the dialysis water pathway must remain to ultrapure standard, and microbiological and chemical quality testing continues unchanged. Sustainability here is purely additive — it captures waste streams and offsets facility load, and must never compromise patient safety, dialysis adequacy, or water-quality compliance.

📈

Worked examples from the KDIGO 2026 report

Concrete, published savings the report highlights: RO reject-water recycling — one UK service saved ~4,492,000 L/year (RO systems reject roughly 20–80% of feed water); lower dialysate flow — much in-center practice runs Qd 700–800 ml/min, yet modern dialyzers show minimal difference between Qd 700, 500, and 400 ml/min, so Qd 400 ml/min is the recommended default to reduce dialysate and source water, since each step up adds tens of liters of dialysate and source water per session; bulk/central acid concentrate and online priming fluid — one 15-chair unit saved ~21.5 tons of waste and ~26.8 tonnes CO₂e/year, and right-sizing acid-concentrate bags (4.5→3.5 L) avoided ~14,350 L of waste/year; and a reusable PD effluent reservoir (vs. single-use drainage bag) saved ~148.6 kg plastic and ~151 kg CO₂e per patient/year.

For Clinicians · Policy

System and policy levers in the Philippine context.

Unit-level wins compound when paired with system change suited to an archipelagic, high-HD-burden, climate-exposed country. The Philippines carries a heavy and growing dialysis load, long inter-island travel distances, and frequent exposure to heat, typhoons, and grid disruption — conditions that make both lower-footprint modalities and resilient infrastructure doubly valuable. The following levers move the system, not just a single center.

PD-First advocacy

Peritoneal dialysis and home therapies carry a lower infrastructure and patient-travel footprint and are well suited to remote islands where reaching an HD center three times weekly is impractical. A PD-first culture reduces both facility load and recurring transport emissions.

PhilHealth alignment

Benefit design should not inadvertently favor the highest-carbon modality. Reimbursement that supports PD, home therapy, transplantation, and conservative care on equal footing lets the lower-footprint options compete on clinical merit.

Prevention partnerships

Primary-care control of diabetes and hypertension is the single largest carbon-and-cost lever, because it keeps patients off dialysis altogether. Partnering with community and primary-care services to detect and slow CKD upstream outperforms any in-unit efficiency.

Telenephrology

Remote consultation cuts both patient and clinician travel emissions — substantial across an archipelago — while improving access for distant communities. Routine follow-up and triage are natural candidates for virtual care.

Procurement standards

A greener supply chain — bulk purchasing, local sourcing, reduced packaging, and supplier environmental criteria — lowers embodied carbon across consumables, the largest single material stream in a dialysis unit.

Regulatory compliance

All initiatives operate within DOH and DENR medical-waste and infection-control rules. Compliant segregation and disposal protect staff, patients, and communities while enabling legitimate recycling of clean non-clinical streams.

These levers gain traction when organized. A national green nephrology network and awards scheme, modeled on the UK Green Nephrology program and coordinated with the Philippine Society of Nephrology, could share baselines, spread proven interventions, and recognize leading units. The wider field offers momentum to draw on: the 2026 KDIGO Green Dialysis report and the ERA 2026 Glasgow Congress — which took up the same green-dialysis themes (prevention, water and energy savings, personalized and incremental dialysis, reduced single-use plastics) — together with the ISN/ERA call to action on environmental sustainability signal a maturing, converging international agenda that a Philippine network can adapt to local realities.

⚖

Patient safety and compliance come first

Every policy and operational change is subordinate to clinical standards. Sustainability initiatives must never reduce dialysis adequacy, compromise sterility, or restrict access to care — they are pursued only where they leave safety, quality, and equity fully intact.
