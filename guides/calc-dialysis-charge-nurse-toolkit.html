Dialysis Charge Nurse Toolkit

Eight working tools for the Renal Care Academy Dialysis Charge Nurse track: station cover, assignment balance, breaks, lost minutes, a heparin pump check, fall risk, a pre-dialysis risk sorter, and a pressure-drop tracker. Each one shows its formula and what the number does, and does not, tell you. Clinical decisions stay with the prescriber and your unit’s protocol; always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Nothing you enter leaves this page.

A teaching and checking aid. These tools do not authorize staffing, prescribe treatment, or replace your unit’s protocol. Values that set limits are entered by your unit. Use synthetic or de-identified data only; nothing you enter leaves this page. Where the protocol is silent, ask the attending nephrologist.

1 · Station cover check

Modules 01, 03

Formula. Stations left for staff nurses = stations − charge nurse’s own chairs. Per staff nurse now = that number ÷ (nurses − 1). Worst break window = same stations ÷ (nurses − 1 − nurses on break). Rules shown side by side. DOH AO 2012-0001: no more than 1 nurse to 4 stations per shift, a licensing condition. PSN 2024: 1 nurse to 4 patients, or 1 to 6 with a nursing assistant, professional guidance. This tool checks against the limit you enter and never reports a staffing level as authorized.

2 · Acuity-weighted assignment balance

Module 03

Example scale (edit to your unit): stable 1; catheter 3 instead of 1; new or problem access +1; frequent low pressure or arrhythmia +2; isolation +2; needs help walking, confused, or new to dialysis +1; transfusion or IV medicine +1. Honest limit. No acuity tool for outpatient hemodialysis has been shown in trials to improve outcomes. Points make load visible; judgment decides.

3 · Staggered break planner

Module 03

Rule. One break at a time, never inside the protected windows after the last connection or before the first disconnection, when most low-pressure, needle, and bleeding problems happen. Check the worst window with tool 1.

4 · Late starts and lost patient-minutes

Modules 02, 06, 17

Why. Delivered time drives dialysis dose; shortened and missed sessions were associated with higher mortality in DOPPS (Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study) data. Minutes added back at the end are not lost; this tool counts what was not added back.

5 · Heparin pump arithmetic check

Module 09

Formula. Pump rate (mL/h) = ordered units per hour ÷ syringe units per mL. Total units = units per hour × hours. This is a plausibility check, not a dose. The dose is the prescriber’s. Any mismatch with a second nurse’s independent calculation, or a result ten times larger or smaller than usual, means stop and check the order.

6 · Post-dialysis fall-risk screen

Modules 05, 07

Morse Fall Scale (Morse 1989): six items, 0 to 125. Commonly used bands: 0–24 low, 25–44 moderate, 45 or more high; units set their own. Limit. Morse was developed in hospital inpatients and is not validated specifically for hemodialysis; use it as a prompt. Older patients on hemodialysis fell about 1.6 times per person-year in a prospective cohort (Cook 2006). The three dialysis prompts are not scored; any “yes” means walk beside the patient and check standing pressure if your protocol asks.

7 · Pre-dialysis risk sorter and ultrafiltration rate

Module 05

Tick every finding present

Lanes. Now: call the physician before connection. Today: tell the physician during this session. This month: route to the attending or team. Rate. UFR (ultrafiltration rate) = mL ÷ hours ÷ kg. Above 13 mL/kg/h was associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in an observational cohort (Flythe 2011); no trial has shown that capping the rate saves lives. Thresholds for potassium and weight gain are your unit’s. The fluid goal is the prescriber’s.

8 · Intradialytic pressure-drop tracker

Module 07

Definitions. European Best Practice Guideline: a fall in systolic pressure of 20 mm Hg or more, or in mean arterial pressure of 10 mm Hg or more, with symptoms or a need for nursing intervention. In a large cohort, a nadir below 90 mm Hg (below 100 if the session started at 160 or more) was most strongly associated with death (Flythe 2015). Mean arterial pressure = diastolic + (systolic − diastolic) ÷ 3. First response follows your unit’s protocol; always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Check glucose in any drowsy patient on insulin or a sulfonylurea.

Important: These tools do arithmetic, not decisions. They do not authorize staffing or replace your unit’s protocols, current regulations, or the attending nephrologist, who is always asked for guidance. Draft, reviewed November 24, 2026.
