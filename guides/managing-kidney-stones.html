Managing Kidney Stones

Why kidney stones form, what the different types mean, how to pass one safely — and the dietary and medical strategies to make sure it never happens again.

The Basics

What Are Kidney Stones?

Kidney stones form when minerals and salts in the urine become too concentrated and crystallize into solid masses inside the kidney. They range from the size of a grain of sand to a golf ball. Small stones may pass unnoticed; larger ones cause excruciating pain — often described as among the worst pain a person can experience.

Kidney stones are extremely common in the Philippines — made worse by hot weather, inadequate hydration, high-purine diets, and the high prevalence of metabolic syndrome, gout, and diabetes. Once you have had one stone, your lifetime recurrence risk without prevention is over 50%.

Why do stones form?

Three conditions must be present: supersaturation of stone-forming minerals, low urine volume (concentrated urine), and absence of natural inhibitors (citrate, magnesium). When minerals exceed their solubility threshold in concentrated urine, crystals nucleate and grow into stones over weeks to months.

Risk factors in Filipinos

Inadequate water intake, high-purine diet (organ meats, seafood), high oxalate foods (kangkong, camote tops, nuts), hot climate causing increased insensible fluid loss, metabolic syndrome, hyperuricemia, and primary hyperparathyroidism all significantly increase stone risk.

Stone Composition

Types of Kidney Stones — Why the Type Matters

The type of stone determines the cause, the best prevention strategy, and the medications needed. Stone analysis (from a passed or surgically removed stone) is the most reliable way to identify type — always try to collect and bring any passed stone to your doctor.

70%
Calcium Oxalate
Most common. Caused by high oxalate diet, low citrate, low fluid intake, hyperoxaluria, or hypercalciuria. Hard, spiky — visible on plain X-ray.
10%
Calcium Phosphate
Associated with renal tubular acidosis, primary hyperparathyroidism, or high urinary pH. Forms in alkaline urine. Requires metabolic workup.
10%
Uric Acid
Caused by gout, high-purine diet, low urine pH, dehydration, and metabolic syndrome. Radiolucent — NOT visible on plain X-ray. Preventable with citrate and febuxostat.
5%
Struvite (Infection)
Caused by urease-producing bacteria (Proteus, Klebsiella). Form "staghorn" calculi filling the entire renal pelvis. Require antibiotics AND surgical removal.
1–2%
Cystine
Rare genetic disorder (cystinuria). Form repeatedly from childhood. Require very high fluid intake (3–4 L/day), urinary alkalinization, and specific chelating agents.
💡

Always try to collect a passed stone

Strain your urine through a fine mesh strainer or clean cloth when you know a stone is passing. Place the stone in a clean dry container and bring it to your doctor. Stone analysis by laboratory is the most accurate way to determine type and guide specific prevention — far more reliable than diet history or urine chemistry alone.

Clinical Presentation

Symptoms — What Kidney Stones Feel Like

The symptoms of a kidney stone depend on its size and location. Small stones in the kidney may cause no symptoms at all and be found incidentally on imaging. A stone that moves into the ureter triggers the classic renal colic attack.

1

Renal colic — the classic attack

Sudden onset of severe, cramping pain in the flank (side of the back, below the ribs) that radiates to the groin, inner thigh, or genitals as the stone moves down the ureter. The pain comes in waves, is not relieved by position change, and may be accompanied by nausea and vomiting. It is one of the most severe pains known to medicine.

2

Blood in the urine (hematuria)

The stone scratches the delicate lining of the ureter, causing bleeding. Urine may appear pink, red, or cola-colored. Even microscopic hematuria (blood only visible under microscope) is a common finding. Blood in the urine alone, without pain, still requires evaluation.

3

Urinary symptoms

As the stone approaches the bladder junction, it causes urinary urgency, frequency, burning on urination, and the feeling of incomplete bladder emptying — symptoms easily confused with a urinary tract infection. A urine culture distinguishes the two.

4

Fever and chills — a serious sign

A kidney stone with fever indicates an infected, obstructed kidney (obstructive pyelonephritis or pyonephrosis). This is a urological emergency — the infected urine cannot drain, and sepsis can develop rapidly. Go to the ER immediately. This is not a "wait and see" situation.

Acute Management

Passing a Stone — What to Do

Stones smaller than 5 mm pass spontaneously in 70–90% of cases. Stones 5–10 mm pass in about 50%. Stones larger than 10 mm rarely pass without intervention. The goal of conservative management is pain control, hydration, and facilitating stone passage.

1

Pain control — do not suffer unnecessarily

Renal colic is severe pain that requires adequate analgesia. Ketorolac (IV/IM) or diclofenac are highly effective for acute pain if kidney function allows. Paracetamol is safe across all kidney function levels. Opioids (tramadol, morphine) are used for severe pain not controlled by NSAIDs. Do not avoid pain medications out of fear — undertreated pain is dangerous and prolongs the ordeal.

2

Hydration — drink aggressively

Aim for 2.5–3 liters of fluid per day during stone passage. High urine flow helps flush the stone through the ureter. Water is the best choice — avoid carbonated drinks, which are acidic and can worsen certain stone types. If vomiting prevents oral intake, IV fluids in the ER are needed.

3

Alpha-blockers for medical expulsive therapy

Tamsulosin 0.4 mg once daily relaxes the ureteral smooth muscle, widening the passage and increasing the chance of spontaneous stone passage for stones 5–10 mm. It is most effective for distal ureteral stones and significantly reduces the time to stone passage and need for analgesics. Your doctor may prescribe this at ER discharge.

4

Strain urine and collect the stone

Use a fine mesh strainer or coffee filter to catch the stone when it passes. This is usually felt as a small, gritty sensation. Place the stone in a clean dry container and bring it to your doctor for stone analysis — this single step gives your doctor the most accurate information to prevent your next stone.

5

Follow-up imaging after 4 weeks

If a stone has not passed after 4 weeks of conservative management, or if pain is not adequately controlled, intervention is needed. Do not assume the stone has passed just because pain has subsided — it may have lodged silently and be causing damage without symptoms.

When Surgery or Procedures Are Needed

ProcedureHow it worksBest for
ESWL (Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy) High-energy sound waves directed at the stone from outside the body — breaks it into smaller fragments that pass in urine over days to weeks Stones <2 cm in the kidney or upper ureter; calcium oxalate and uric acid stones. Less effective for hard stones (calcium oxalate monohydrate, cystine).
Ureteroscopy (URS) with Laser Lithotripsy A thin flexible scope is passed through the urethra and bladder into the ureter; laser breaks the stone into dust or fragments Ureteral stones of any size; stones in lower kidney pole; failed ESWL. High success rate. No incision required.
PCNL (Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy) A small incision in the back allows direct access to the kidney with a nephroscope; large stones fragmented and removed Large stones (>2 cm), staghorn calculi, stones resistant to ESWL, or complex anatomy. Most invasive but most effective for large stone burden.
Ureteral Stent (Double-J Stent) A soft plastic tube placed inside the ureter to bypass obstruction and allow urine to drain around a stone Emergency relief of infected, obstructed kidney (with or without immediate stone removal); also placed after ureteroscopy to aid healing.
⚠

Obstructed + infected kidney = surgical emergency

If a stone is blocking urine flow AND the kidney is infected (fever, elevated WBC, positive urine culture), urgent decompression is required — either a ureteral stent or nephrostomy tube. This cannot wait for ESWL or elective ureteroscopy. Delaying drainage in an obstructed infected kidney leads to sepsis, shock, and irreversible kidney loss.

Long-Term Prevention

Preventing the Next Stone — Diet, Fluids, and Medications

Stone prevention is highly effective — recurrence rates drop by 50–80% with proper treatment. The strategy depends on stone type. These are the evidence-based interventions that apply to most Filipino patients with calcium oxalate stones (the most common type).

💧

Hydration is the single most important intervention

Drink enough fluid to produce at least 2.5 liters of urine per day. In hot weather or when exercising, increase intake further. The easiest way to check: your urine should be pale yellow — almost clear. Dark yellow urine means you need more water. Spread drinking throughout the day, including a large glass before bed and another if you wake at night.

For Calcium Oxalate Stones (Most Common)

Eat freely and increase

  • Water — 3+ liters per day; distributed throughout the day
  • Citrate-rich foods: calamansi, dalandan, lemon juice — citrate inhibits crystal formation
  • Calcium-rich foods: low-fat milk, yogurt, tofu — dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut, REDUCING urinary oxalate
  • Fruits and vegetables (except high-oxalate ones)
  • Adequate protein from low-purine sources

Limit or avoid

  • High-oxalate foods: kangkong, kamote tops (talbos ng kamote), peanuts, almonds, cashews, dark chocolate, milo, tea in excess
  • Excess animal protein (beef, pork, organ meats) — increases urinary calcium and uric acid
  • Excess sodium — raises urinary calcium excretion
  • Vitamin C supplements >1,000 mg/day — metabolized to oxalate
  • Soft drinks (especially cola) — high phosphate, low pH
⚠

Do NOT restrict dietary calcium for calcium stones

This is one of the most common — and harmful — misconceptions about kidney stones. Restricting dairy and calcium-rich foods actually increases urinary oxalate (because less calcium is available in the gut to bind oxalate) and worsens stone recurrence. Eat normal amounts of dairy. It is excess oxalate and sodium that need restricting — not calcium.

Medications for Stone Prevention

MedicationStone typeHow it prevents recurrence
Potassium citrate (Uralyt-U, potassium citrate sachets) Calcium oxalate, uric acid, cystine Raises urinary citrate (natural crystal inhibitor) and urinary pH. Citrate binds calcium in urine, preventing crystal nucleation. Most broadly useful stone prevention medication.
Thiazide diuretics (Hydrochlorothiazide, Chlorthalidone) Calcium oxalate with hypercalciuria Reduces urinary calcium excretion by enhancing tubular reabsorption. Significantly reduces recurrence in patients with high urine calcium.
Febuxostat 80 mg / Allopurinol 100–300 mg Uric acid stones; calcium oxalate with hyperuricosuria Reduces uric acid production. Febuxostat preferred in CKD. Also helps calcium oxalate recurrence when uric acid crystals act as nucleation seeds.
Sodium bicarbonate / Potassium bicarbonate Uric acid stones Alkalinizes urine above pH 6.5 — uric acid is soluble at higher pH. Can dissolve existing uric acid stones over weeks to months with consistent use.
Magnesium supplementation Calcium oxalate Magnesium binds oxalate in the gut (similar to calcium) and in urine, reducing crystal formation. Magnesium glycinate or oxide 300–400 mg daily.
Emergency Warning Signs

When to Go to the ER Immediately

⚠ Do not wait — go to the ER for:

Fever or chills with flank pain — possible infected obstructed kidney
Pain so severe it cannot be controlled with oral medications
Inability to keep fluids down due to vomiting
No urine output for more than 8 hours
Visible blood clots in urine
Known solitary kidney with stone symptoms
Pain persisting beyond 4–6 weeks without stone passage
Confusion, rapid heart rate, or low blood pressure with stone pain
