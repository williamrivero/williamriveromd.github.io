Overview

Climate extremes as a kidney-care continuity problem

Kidney patients sit at the intersection of two climate exposures. The first is physiologic: heat stress narrows an already reduced renal reserve, provokes volume depletion or, in dialysis and heart failure, volume and potassium shifts, and can precipitate acute kidney injury (AKI). The second is operational: typhoons, floods, and grid failures interrupt the power, treated water, staff, supplies, and transport on which hemodialysis (HD), peritoneal dialysis (PD), and immunosuppression depend. Mortality signals in dialysis cohorts after heat and storm exposure reflect both channels at once. Recent commentary frames this as a test of health-system readiness for AKI as much as for dialysis (Claure-Del Granado & Lumlertgul, 2026), and the World Health Organization (WHO) 2023 operational framework treats climate resilience and low-carbon care as one agenda (WHO, 2023).

This page is a companion to the Green Nephrology guide, whose emphasis is preventing chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression and reducing resource use. Here the center of gravity is preparation, continuity, and recovery, with low-carbon measures included where they also strengthen continuity. Detailed PAGASA signal checklists sit in Typhoon & Disaster Preparedness, and brownout and cool-dialysate protocols in El Niño & Extreme Heat.

The heat and storm estimates are observational, mostly from the United States, and describe relative risk across populations. They support preparedness; they do not quantify individual risk or transfer directly to Philippine typhoons. Life-cycle assessment (LCA) results describe specific centers and system boundaries, not local benchmarks. Heat Physiology

From thermoregulation to tubular injury

Normal response. Heat load triggers cutaneous vasodilation and sweating. Cardiac output rises and is redistributed to the skin, so splanchnic and renal blood flow fall. Sweat losses of water and sodium activate the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and vasopressin, concentrating the urine. In a healthy kidney this is well tolerated.

What CKD and dialysis change. Reduced nephron mass narrows the margin before glomerular filtration rate (GFR) falls; diuretics, RAAS blockade, and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors further blunt compensatory responses during volume loss; impaired thirst and autonomic function are common in older and diabetic patients. In patients on dialysis, the problem may be the opposite: interdialytic fluid gain with hypotension-prone hemodynamics, potassium accumulation, and higher cardiovascular vulnerability. Proposed mechanisms for heat-associated CKD progression, including recurrent subclinical tubular injury, hyperuricemia, vasopressin signaling, and the polyol–fructokinase pathway, remain mechanistic hypotheses supported mainly by occupational and experimental data (McMullen et al., 2026).

Severe heat illness. Exertional heat stroke and rhabdomyolysis add myoglobin-mediated tubular toxicity, inflammation, and coagulopathy to hypoperfusion. These are emergencies; rapid cooling and volume management take precedence over everything else.

Evidence on medicine plans

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) heat-and-medications guidance reflects expert opinion; it recommends medication review, storage counseling, and avoiding abrupt discontinuation without a plan. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2024 chronic kidney disease guideline remains the reference for medicine management in CKD (KDIGO, 2024). Sick-day pausing of SGLT2 inhibitors, RAAS blockers, diuretics, and metformin is consensus-based rather than trial-proven; individualize and document the restart plan.

Workers. In a Nicaraguan sugarcane workforce, staged rest, shade, hydration, and hygiene coincided with a fall in clinical AKI among burned-cane cutters from 20 to 8 per 1,000 worker-months, and incident kidney injury from 21% to 1% across four harvests (Hansson et al., 2025). The design is before-and-after, so secular change may confound; it still supports employer protections for outdoor workers. Hydration volumes from that program do not apply to CKD or dialysis.

Stewardship

Fewer resources, same quality: guardrails first

The KDIGO 2026 Controversies Conference on green dialysis (Barraclough et al., 2026) supports metering, safe reuse of reverse osmosis (RO) reject water, energy efficiency, and waste reduction, balanced against outcomes and access. ⛔

Never trade quality for carbon

No reduction in dialysis time, dialysate flow, or indicated treatment for environmental savings; no change to personal protective equipment, cannulation technique, or reprocessing based on modeled savings. Modality choice follows clinical suitability and patient preference.

What the LCAs show. Four European centers reported 1,829 to 4,894 kg carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e) per patient-year using 156 four-hour sessions, about 12 to 31 kg CO₂e per session on the same denominator; patient travel, consumables, and electricity dominated, and a modeled full shift to public transport cut patient-travel emissions by 57.9% to 64.7% (Fehintola, Jakubowska, et al., 2026). A single-center Canadian LCA estimated in-center HD climate impact at 74% above deceased-donor transplant and 46% above automated PD, excluding pharmaceuticals (Saleem et al., 2026). In Utrecht, automated draining of bloodlines and dialyzers before disposal reduced the modeled per-session climate impact by more than 9% within that process boundary; clinical outcomes were not assessed (Fehintola, Van Gelder, et al., 2026). None of these are Philippine benchmarks or reasons to choose a modality. For a full footprint, the international in-center HD carbon calculator is a ready comparator; check its boundaries and emission factors before use.

Philippine Context

Local systems to build around

Link patients to official sources only: PAGASA heat index, tropical cyclone bulletins, and flood advisories; HazardHunterPH for site planning (not live road passability); and the barangay or city DRRMO for evacuation and cooling sites. Avoid hard-coding live warnings or telephone numbers in public materials; clinic contacts belong on the patient's card. Where future tools show alerts, display region or station, issue and validity time, source, and time zone, and default to “check the latest official advisory” when data are stale rather than carrying forward an all-clear.

Plan explicitly for island and ferry routes, out-of-pocket transport cost when public transport stops, intermittent signal, and patients who cannot read text messages. Dialysis-water obligations sit under DOH Administrative Order 2013-0003, implementing Administrative Order 2012-0001; confirm current issuances with licensing and infection-prevention leads.
