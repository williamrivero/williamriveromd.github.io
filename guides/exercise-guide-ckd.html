Why It Matters

Exercise as Medicine in CKD

CKD patients who exercise regularly have slower progression of kidney disease, lower cardiovascular mortality, better blood pressure control, and improved quality of life — independent of medications. Exercise is not something to avoid in CKD. Done safely, it is one of the most powerful interventions available.

Even 30 minutes of walking five days a week produces measurable kidney and cardiovascular benefits within 8–12 weeks. The key is consistency and starting gently.

CKD-specific muscle wasting

Uremic toxins, metabolic acidosis, and inactivity cause accelerated muscle breakdown (sarcopenia) in CKD. Resistance exercise is the only proven intervention to reverse it — and muscle mass is a strong independent predictor of survival in dialysis patients.

Exercise and dialysis adequacy

Exercise during hemodialysis sessions (intradialytic exercise) improves toxin clearance by increasing blood flow to muscles — improving effective Kt/V by 5–10% without changing the dialysis prescription. Even gentle pedaling during HD is beneficial.

The inactivity spiral

Fatigue → inactivity → muscle loss → worsening fatigue → more inactivity. This self-reinforcing cycle is extremely common in CKD and progresses rapidly. Gentle daily movement — even 10 minutes — breaks the cycle and rebuilds functional capacity incrementally.

Before You Begin

Safety Rules — Non-Negotiable

The Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale is your primary safety guide. CKD patients should stay between 3–5 out of 10 — breathing noticeably harder but still able to speak in full sentences.

STOP exercising immediately and rest if you experience any of the following:

  • Chest pain, tightness, or pressure — even mild
  • Shortness of breath that does not improve within 2 minutes of stopping
  • Dizziness, lightheadedness, or feeling faint
  • Palpitations or irregular heartbeat you can feel
  • Sudden severe muscle cramps — especially in dialysis patients (electrolyte shift)
  • Access site pain, swelling, or bleeding in dialysis patients
  • Sudden severe headache
  • Any symptom that feels "not right" — trust your body

Check before every session

Blood pressure before exercise: if systolic >180 mmHg or diastolic >110 mmHg — rest and recheck in 30 minutes. If still elevated, skip exercise for the day and contact your nephrologist. If systolic <90 mmHg — do not exercise. Dialysis patients: do not exercise within 2 hours after HD (hypotension risk).

Conditions requiring medical clearance first

Unstable angina or recent heart attack (<4 weeks) · Uncontrolled arrhythmia · Severe symptomatic aortic stenosis · Decompensated heart failure · Active infection with fever · Recent surgery · Severe anemia (Hgb <80 g/L) · Uncontrolled diabetic neuropathy with loss of foot sensation.

Phase 1 — 5–10 Minutes

Warm-Up Exercises

The warm-up prepares your cardiovascular system, increases blood flow to muscles, and reduces injury risk. In CKD, it also allows blood pressure to adjust gradually — critical for patients prone to orthostatic hypotension.

Seated Marching in Place
Seated2 minAll stages
Gentle rhythmic movement to elevate heart rate and warm up leg muscles without standing stress.
  1. Sit upright on a chair, feet flat on the floor
  2. Alternately lift each knee up to hip height
  3. Swing opposite arm gently with each step
  4. Pace: 1 lift per second, steady rhythm
  5. Duration: 1–2 minutes
Shoulder Circles
Seated or Standing1 minAll stages
Loosens the shoulder and upper back — important for dialysis patients with catheter or fistula sites.
  1. Sit or stand comfortably upright
  2. Roll both shoulders forward in slow circles × 8
  3. Then roll backward × 8
  4. Keep movements smooth, no jerking
  5. Breathe normally throughout
Ankle Pumps and Circles
Seated1 minAll stages
Prevents blood pooling in the legs and reduces risk of orthostatic hypotension when standing. Especially important for dialysis patients and the elderly.
  1. Sit with feet slightly elevated or flat on floor
  2. Pump both feet up and down (flex and point) × 15
  3. Then circle both ankles clockwise × 8, then counter-clockwise × 8
  4. Move slowly and deliberately
Slow Walk in Place
Standing2–3 minStages 1–4
Gradual transition to standing activity. Allows blood pressure to stabilize in upright position before progressing to walking.
  1. Stand near a wall or chair for support
  2. March gently in place at a very slow pace
  3. Gradually increase pace over 2–3 minutes
  4. Check that you feel stable before moving away from support
⚠ Rise slowly — sit for 30 seconds before standing if prone to dizziness
Neck Rolls and Head Tilts
Seated1 minAll stages
Releases tension in the cervical spine and upper trapezius — common tension areas in sedentary CKD patients.
  1. Sit tall with shoulders relaxed
  2. Slowly tilt right ear toward right shoulder — hold 3 sec
  3. Return to center, tilt left — hold 3 sec
  4. Chin to chest — hold 3 sec, return
  5. Do NOT roll head backward (cervical stress)
  6. Repeat 3–4 times each direction
Wrist and Hand Warm-Up
Seated1 minFistula patients
Important pre-exercise warm-up for AVF patients — gentle wrist movement improves fistula blood flow and reduces post-exercise access soreness.
  1. Gently circle both wrists clockwise × 8, then counter-clockwise × 8
  2. Open and close fingers slowly × 10
  3. Gentle prayer stretch: press palms together, lower hands — hold 5 sec
  4. Avoid any movement that causes fistula site pain
⚠ Never compress or put weight on the fistula arm
Phase 2 — 5 Minutes

Breathing Exercises

Diaphragmatic breathing reduces sympathetic nervous system activity, lowers blood pressure, reduces anxiety, and improves oxygenation. For CKD patients with fluid overload or pulmonary congestion, breath training also strengthens respiratory muscles and improves dyspnea tolerance.

Diaphragmatic breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system — reducing heart rate and blood pressure within minutes. Practice twice daily: once before exercise, once before sleep.

Diaphragmatic Belly Breathing
Supine or Seated5 minAll stages
The foundation of all breathing exercises. Trains the diaphragm to do the work of breathing rather than accessory neck and shoulder muscles.
  1. Lie flat or sit with one hand on chest, one on belly
  2. Inhale slowly through nose for 4 counts — belly should rise, chest stays still
  3. Exhale slowly through pursed lips for 6 counts — belly falls
  4. The hand on your chest should barely move
  5. Repeat 8–10 cycles, 2× daily
Pursed Lip Breathing
Any position3 minAll stages
Slows respiration rate, maintains positive airway pressure, and is the most effective technique for relieving shortness of breath during exertion in CKD and heart failure patients.
  1. Relax your neck and shoulders
  2. Inhale through nose for 2 counts
  3. Pucker lips as if about to whistle
  4. Exhale slowly through pursed lips for 4 counts
  5. Use during any activity that causes breathlessness
Box Breathing (4-4-4-4)
Seated3–5 minAll stages
Equal-duration breath phases create autonomic balance — reduces pre-exercise anxiety, lowers cortisol, and improves heart rate variability. Used in cardiac rehabilitation programs.
  1. Inhale through nose for 4 counts
  2. Hold breath for 4 counts
  3. Exhale through mouth for 4 counts
  4. Hold empty for 4 counts
  5. Repeat 4–6 cycles
⚠ If breath-holding causes discomfort, reduce to 2-2-4 pattern
Coordinated Breathing with Movement
Seated3 minAll stages
Trains the habit of exhaling on exertion — critical for blood pressure safety during resistance exercise and daily activities like lifting.
  1. Sit tall, arms at sides
  2. Inhale as you raise both arms overhead (2 counts)
  3. Exhale as you lower arms back down (3 counts)
  4. Never hold your breath during any exertion
  5. Repeat 10 times, then rest
Phase 3 — 10 Minutes

Stretching and Flexibility

CKD patients have significantly reduced flexibility due to inactivity, fluid retention, and uremic neuropathy. Regular stretching reduces muscle cramping (a major complaint during and after dialysis), improves circulation, and maintains functional range of motion for daily activities.

Seated Hamstring Stretch
Seated30 sec each legAll stages
Reduces leg cramping — the most common musculoskeletal complaint in hemodialysis patients.
  1. Sit at edge of chair, extend one leg forward
  2. Keep foot flexed (toes pointing up)
  3. Sit tall and hinge slightly forward at hips until you feel a gentle pull behind the knee
  4. Hold 20–30 seconds — breathe normally
  5. Repeat on the other leg
Calf Stretch at Wall
Standing30 sec eachStages 1–4
Targets the gastrocnemius and soleus — the muscle groups responsible for nocturnal leg cramps in CKD and dialysis patients.
  1. Stand facing a wall, hands flat on wall for support
  2. Step one foot back, keep heel flat on floor
  3. Bend front knee gently until you feel calf stretch in back leg
  4. Hold 30 seconds — do not bounce
  5. Switch sides and repeat
Chest Opener Stretch
Seated or Standing20 sec × 3All stages
Counteracts the forward posture common in dialysis patients seated for 4+ hours. Opens the chest for better breathing capacity.
  1. Clasp hands behind your back
  2. Gently squeeze shoulder blades together
  3. Lift chest upward slightly — breathe in deeply
  4. Hold 20 seconds — exhale slowly
  5. Release and repeat 3 times
Hip Flexor Stretch (Seated)
Seated30 sec eachAll stages
Relieves hip tightness from prolonged sitting — improves gait stability and reduces fall risk in elderly CKD patients.
  1. Sit sideways at the edge of the chair
  2. Let the outer leg hang off the edge with knee bent, foot toward floor
  3. Sit tall and feel a gentle stretch in the front of that hip
  4. Hold 20–30 seconds, switch sides
  5. Hold chair arm for safety
Upper Back and Thoracic Stretch
Seated20 sec × 3All stages
Targets thoracic kyphosis and upper back stiffness — common in CKD patients with renal osteodystrophy and low vitamin D.
  1. Sit in chair, cross arms and hug your own shoulders
  2. Round forward gently, chin to chest
  3. Feel stretch between shoulder blades
  4. Hold 20 seconds — breathe into your back
  5. Then sit tall and open arms wide to reverse
Quadriceps Stretch (Supported)
Standing30 sec eachStages 1–4
Maintains knee extension strength needed for safe stair climbing, chair rising, and fall prevention.
  1. Stand near a wall or chair for support
  2. Bend one knee, bring heel toward buttock
  3. Hold ankle with same-side hand (use a strap if needed)
  4. Keep knees together, stand tall
  5. Hold 30 seconds, switch sides
⚠ Skip if knee pain — do seated leg extension instead
Phase 4 — 5–10 Minutes

Balance and Proprioception Exercises

CKD patients have 2–3× higher fall rates than age-matched healthy adults — due to peripheral neuropathy, muscle weakness, postural hypotension, and medication side effects. Balance training is essential and should be included every session.

Single-Leg Stand (Supported)
Standing10–30 sec eachAll stages
The single most effective exercise for fall prevention. Even 10 seconds per leg builds proprioceptive pathways that protect against falls.
  1. Stand near wall or chair, fingertips on surface for safety
  2. Shift weight to one foot, lift other foot slightly off floor
  3. Hold 10–30 seconds — eyes open first
  4. Aim for 3 repetitions per leg
  5. Progress: reduce finger contact → eyes closed when confident
⚠ Never let go of support completely until physician-cleared
Tandem Standing (Heel to Toe)
Standing20–30 secStages 1–3
Narrows the base of support to challenge balance — used in geriatric fall prevention programs worldwide.
  1. Stand with one foot directly in front of the other, heel touching toes
  2. Arms out to sides for balance
  3. Hold 20 seconds, switch which foot is in front
  4. Keep fingertips near wall for safety
  5. Progress: tandem walking — 10 steps heel-to-toe along a line
Weight Shifting Side to Side
Standing2 minAll stages
Trains lateral balance reactions — the movements your body makes automatically to prevent a fall to the side. Excellent starting exercise for very deconditioned patients.
  1. Stand feet shoulder-width apart, hands on chair back
  2. Slowly shift weight to right foot — lift left heel slightly
  3. Hold 3 seconds, shift to left — lift right heel slightly
  4. Rhythm: slow and controlled, not swaying
  5. 10 repetitions each side
Sit-to-Stand (Chair Rise)
Chair + Standing× 5–10All stages
The single most important functional exercise for CKD patients — predicts fall risk, hospitalization risk, and mortality better than most clinical tests. Also builds quadriceps strength.
  1. Sit at front half of chair, feet flat, hip-width apart
  2. Lean slightly forward, nose over toes
  3. Push through heels and stand — exhale as you rise
  4. Pause fully upright for 2 seconds
  5. Lower slowly back to seat — inhale as you lower
  6. Start with hands on armrests, progress to arms crossed on chest
Heel and Toe Raises
Standing× 15 eachAll stages
Strengthens ankle dorsiflexors and plantarflexors — the muscles responsible for "catching" yourself when you trip. Also promotes calf muscle pump to reduce lower limb edema.
  1. Stand behind chair, hands lightly on back for support
  2. Rise up on tiptoes — hold 2 seconds
  3. Lower slowly — count 3 seconds going down
  4. Then rock back on heels, lift toes off floor — hold 2 seconds
  5. 15 repetitions of each movement
Sideways Step-Outs
Standing× 10 eachStages 1–4
Trains the hip abductors — the muscles that prevent the "Trendelenburg" sideways fall. Weakness here is a major fall risk factor in CKD patients.
  1. Stand near wall for support
  2. Step one foot out to the side — shoulder-width
  3. Bring the other foot to meet it — controlled
  4. 10 steps right, 10 steps left
  5. Progress: add light resistance band around ankles
Phase 5 — 20–30 Minutes

Aerobic / Cardiovascular Exercise

Aerobic exercise is the most important component for cardiovascular protection in CKD. KDIGO 2024 recommends ≥150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity. Start with whatever duration you can manage — even 5 minutes — and build slowly.

🚶

Walking is the gold standard for CKD

Walking requires no equipment, is low-impact on the kidneys and joints, can be done anywhere, and has the strongest evidence base for CKD cardiovascular benefit. Start with 5–10 minutes daily and add 2 minutes per week until reaching 30 minutes. Outdoor walking in the morning is ideal — natural light helps regulate the circadian clock, which independently benefits kidney function.

Brisk Walking
Outdoor / Indoor10–30 minAll stages
Primary recommended exercise for CKD Stages 1–4. Target: pace where you can talk but not sing — RPE 3–5.
  1. Begin at comfortable pace for 5 minutes
  2. Increase to brisk pace — slightly breathless but conversational
  3. Maintain for target duration
  4. Cool down at slow pace for 3–5 minutes
  5. Hydration: small sips only (per fluid restriction if on dialysis)
⚠ Dialysis patients: walk on non-HD days or ≥2 hours after HD session
Stationary Cycling
Seated15–30 minAll stages
Ideal for CKD patients with joint pain, peripheral neuropathy, or balance issues. Can be performed during dialysis sessions (intradialytic cycling) — improves Kt/V and reduces fatigue.
  1. Set resistance to very low initially
  2. Pedal at comfortable cadence — 50–60 rpm
  3. Maintain RPE 3–5
  4. Progress: increase duration before increasing resistance
  5. During HD: pedal for 30 min during first 2 hours of session
Chair Aerobics
Seated15–20 minESKD / elderly
Fully seated cardiovascular workout — suitable for patients who cannot stand safely for extended periods. Combines arm and leg movements to elevate heart rate without standing.
  1. Seated marching with arm swings — 2 min
  2. Seated punches alternating arms — 1 min
  3. Seated knee lifts with opposite elbow touch — 2 min
  4. Seated step-outs with arms reaching overhead — 2 min
  5. Repeat circuit 2–3 times with 1-min rest between rounds
Interval Walking
Outdoor / Indoor20 minStages 1–3
Alternates comfortable and brisk pacing — more time-efficient than steady-state walking and produces greater cardiovascular adaptation. Well-tolerated by CKD patients in clinical trials.
  1. Walk at comfortable pace for 3 minutes
  2. Walk at brisk pace for 2 minutes (RPE 4–5)
  3. Return to comfortable pace for 3 minutes
  4. Repeat 3–4 cycles total
  5. Build up to: 1 min easy / 1 min brisk as fitness improves
Phase 6 — 15–20 Minutes

Strength and Resistance Exercises

Muscle wasting (sarcopenia) affects up to 60% of dialysis patients and is a major driver of functional decline, hospitalization, and mortality. Resistance training 2–3× per week is the only intervention proven to reverse it. Use light resistance — bodyweight, resistance bands, or water bottles as weights.

⚠

Key safety rules for resistance exercise in CKD

  • Always exhale on exertion (the effort phase) — never hold your breath (Valsalva maneuver raises BP dangerously)
  • Start with bodyweight only for 4 weeks before adding any external resistance
  • Do not exercise the fistula arm with weights — use opposite arm only
  • Stop if any access site discomfort, excessive muscle cramping, or BP symptoms
Seated Leg Press (Wall)
Seated× 10–15All stages
Builds quadriceps and gluteal strength — the largest muscle groups in the body and the ones most critical for functional independence.
  1. Sit in chair facing wall, feet flat on wall at knee height
  2. Push feet into wall — exhale as you press
  3. Hold 2 seconds at full extension
  4. Release slowly — inhale as you release
  5. 3 sets × 10–15 repetitions
Seated Bicep Curl
Seated× 10–15Non-fistula arm
Maintains upper limb strength for daily activities. Use water bottles (250–500 mL) as weights to start.
  1. Sit tall, hold weight in hand, palm facing up
  2. Curl weight toward shoulder — exhale
  3. Lower slowly to starting position — inhale (count 3 seconds)
  4. 3 sets × 12 reps
  5. Fistula arm: use resistance band gently or skip
⚠ Fistula patients: exercise NON-fistula arm only with weights
Mini Squat (Partial)
Standing× 10Stages 1–4
Builds leg strength for stair climbing and rising from chairs safely. Partial range only — never below 90° knee flexion in CKD patients with joint disease.
  1. Stand behind chair, hands lightly on back
  2. Feet shoulder-width apart, toes slightly out
  3. Bend knees to 30–45° only (not full squat)
  4. Exhale as you lower, inhale as you rise
  5. 3 sets × 10 repetitions
Seated Shoulder Press
Seated× 10–12Non-fistula arm
Strengthens deltoids and triceps for overhead reaching and pushing activities. Use very light resistance initially — overhead exertion raises BP more than other positions.
  1. Sit tall, hold light weight at shoulder height, palm forward
  2. Press upward — exhale as you push
  3. Lower back to shoulder — inhale, 3-second descent
  4. 3 sets × 10 reps
  5. Monitor: if BP feels like it surges — use even lighter weight or skip
Resistance Band Row (Seated)
Seated× 12All stages
Targets upper back and rear shoulder — counteracts forward posture and builds functional pulling strength.
  1. Sit at edge of chair, band looped around feet
  2. Hold band ends, arms extended forward
  3. Pull band toward waist, squeezing shoulder blades — exhale
  4. Return slowly — inhale, 3 seconds
  5. 3 sets × 12 reps
Calf Raises (Standing)
Standing× 15All stages
Builds the calf muscle pump — reduces lower limb edema in CKD patients and prevents nocturnal cramping. Also trains ankle stability for fall prevention.
  1. Stand behind chair, hands on back for support
  2. Rise up on tiptoes — exhale, hold 2 seconds at top
  3. Lower slowly — inhale, 3-second descent
  4. 3 sets × 15 reps
  5. Progress: single-leg calf raises when ready
Phase 7 — 5–10 Minutes

Cool-Down

The cool-down is not optional in CKD. Abrupt cessation of exercise can cause a sudden drop in blood pressure — particularly dangerous in patients on antihypertensives or those with autonomic neuropathy from diabetes.

Slow Walk — Gradual Deceleration
Walking3–5 minAll stages
After any aerobic session, spend 3–5 minutes walking at progressively slower pace before stopping completely.
  1. Reduce walking pace gradually over 3–5 minutes
  2. Never stop suddenly — keep moving until heart rate and breathing have settled
  3. End with 2 minutes of very slow strolling
  4. Sit only after heart rate has returned to near-resting level
Full Body Gentle Stretch — Sequence
Seated or Supine5 minAll stages
Post-exercise muscles are warm and most receptive to stretching. Hold each stretch longer than warm-up (30–45 seconds).
  1. Seated hamstring stretch × 30 sec each leg
  2. Seated calf stretch × 30 sec each leg
  3. Chest opener × 30 sec × 2
  4. Neck side tilts × 20 sec each side
  5. Deep breathing × 5 cycles while stretching
Supine Relaxation Breathing
Supine3–5 minAll stages
Activates the parasympathetic nervous system to bring heart rate, BP, and cortisol back to baseline. Especially beneficial for hypertensive CKD patients.
  1. Lie flat on back with pillow if needed
  2. Arms relaxed at sides, eyes closed
  3. Breathe in through nose for 4 counts
  4. Breathe out through mouth for 6 counts
  5. Continue for 3–5 minutes — let your body sink heavy into the floor

✅

Signs you are exercising at the right level

  • You finish the session feeling tired but not exhausted
  • You feel better (less fatigue, better mood) within 30 minutes of finishing
  • Muscle soreness the next day is mild and resolves within 24 hours
  • Your blood pressure 30 minutes after exercise is at or below your pre-exercise reading
  • You could carry on a short conversation throughout the session
  • Sleep quality and appetite are gradually improving over weeks
ImportantMahalagang PaalalaImportante nga Pahibalo: Always consult your nephrologist before starting a new exercise program, particularly if you have recent cardiovascular events, severe anemia (Hgb <80 g/L), uncontrolled blood pressure, or are on peritoneal dialysis. This guide is intended as general guidance and does not replace individualized medical advice. Exercise progression should be reviewed every 3 months.
