The Foundation

Anatomy of a Philippine Nutrition Facts Label

Philippine nutrition labels follow the format mandated by FDA Circular 2014-014. Most packaged foods sold in SM, Robinsons, Puregold, and palengke stalls follow this layout. For CKD patients, the label is a clinical tool — not just a calorie counter. You are specifically looking for sodium, potassium, phosphorus, protein, and serving size information. Most Filipino patients have never been taught how to read a label for their kidney condition — this guide changes that.

The Biggest Mistake

The Serving Size Trap — How Patients Are Misled

The single most common labeling error that CKD patients make is reading the nutrition facts for one serving while consuming multiple servings. Manufacturers are legally permitted to set serving sizes to make their numbers look better. A bag of chips listed at "3 servings" has sodium, potassium, and phosphorus that must be multiplied by 3 if the whole bag is eaten. Always check servings per container first — before reading any other number.

A single pack of instant noodles, fully consumed, delivers nearly the entire daily sodium allowance for a CKD patient — even though the per-serving number appears modest. Never read the label for just one serving without checking how many servings are in the package.

💡

The 3-second rule before buying any packaged food

Before putting any packaged item in your cart: (1) find "servings per container," (2) multiply sodium by that number, (3) if total sodium exceeds 400mg — put it back or portion it precisely. This 3-second habit alone can prevent most dietary sodium errors in CKD patients shopping independently.

Why % Daily Value misleads CKD patients

%DV on Philippine nutrition labels is calculated based on a 2,000 kcal diet for a healthy adult. The reference values used: sodium 2,300mg, potassium 4,700mg. But CKD Stage 4–5 patients need sodium <2,000mg and potassium <2,000mg — different reference points. A product showing "20% DV sodium" (460mg) is actually 23% of your CKD sodium limit. Always convert %DV back to milligrams and compare to your personal CKD targets — not the general population values.

Your personal CKD shopping targets — quick reference

  • Daily sodium limit: <2,000 mg/day (all stages)
  • Daily potassium: <2,000 mg (dialysis); <2,500 mg (Stage 3b–4); unrestricted if normal in Stage 1–3a
  • Daily phosphorus: <800 mg (dialysis); <900 mg (Stage 3b–4); zero inorganic phosphate additives (all stages)
  • Per-meal sodium budget: divide daily limit ÷ 3 meals = ~650 mg per meal maximum
  • Write these on an index card and carry it in your wallet while shopping
Reading the Ingredients List

The Ingredients List — Where Hidden Dangers Are Buried

The nutrition facts panel shows quantities — but the ingredients list shows what is actually in the food. For CKD patients, the ingredients list is more important than the nutrition facts for one reason: phosphate additives are almost never listed in the nutrition panel but are always visible in the ingredients list.

⚠

Ingredients are listed in order of weight — highest first

By law (Philippine FDA), ingredients are listed from most abundant to least abundant by weight. Salt (sodium chloride) appearing as the 2nd or 3rd ingredient means the product is extremely high in sodium. Any phosphate additive appearing in the first 5 ingredients means very high inorganic phosphate content. Even when listed last, phosphate additives are dangerous in CKD — there is no safe quantity of inorganic phosphate additives for Stage 3+ patients.

The Most Important Scan

Spotting Hidden Phosphate — The Complete List

Inorganic phosphate additives are absorbed at nearly 100% (vs 40–60% from natural food) and are the most dangerous dietary source of phosphorus for CKD patients. They are legally permitted in food without being listed in the nutrition panel — only in the ingredients list, often under chemical names that most patients would not recognize.

ALWAYS AVOID Words containing "phosphate" — any form
The simplest rule: if any ingredient contains the word "phosphate" — avoid the product. This catches the vast majority of inorganic phosphate additives regardless of their specific chemical name.
Examples: sodium phosphate, calcium phosphate, potassium phosphate, disodium phosphate, trisodium phosphate, monocalcium phosphate, pyrophosphate, polyphosphate, hexametaphosphate
ALWAYS AVOID E-numbers: E338 to E452
European food additive codes E338–E452 are all phosphoric acid or phosphate salts. Some Philippine products use E-numbers instead of chemical names. If you see any E3xx number in this range — it is a phosphate additive.
E338: Phosphoric acid · E339: Sodium phosphates · E340: Potassium phosphates · E341: Calcium phosphates · E343: Magnesium phosphates · E450: Diphosphates · E451: Triphosphates · E452: Polyphosphates
ALWAYS AVOID "Phosphoric acid" in carbonated drinks
Phosphoric acid (E338) is the acidifier in virtually all cola drinks (Coke, Pepsi, RC Cola, store-brand colas) and many energy drinks. It is a direct source of bioavailable inorganic phosphate. Even "diet" or "zero" versions contain the same phosphoric acid. This is the primary reason all carbonated dark drinks are prohibited in CKD.
Look for: "Phosphoric acid," "Acidity regulator (E338)," "Carbon dioxide" alone is fine — it is the phosphoric acid that is the problem
HIGH CAUTION "Modified starch" and "food starch"
Modified starches are sometimes treated with phosphate groups to improve texture and shelf life. "Phosphated distarch phosphate" (E1413) and "acetylated distarch phosphate" (E1414) are the most common. Look for "modified starch" as an ingredient — if it appears along with other phosphate additives, avoid. Alone, it may be lower risk but is still worth noting.
CAUTION Leavening agents in baked goods
Baking powder contains monocalcium phosphate (a phosphate salt) as its acid component. Bread, cakes, biscuits, and pastries made with baking powder contain inorganic phosphate in the leavening agent. Self-rising flour also contains phosphate leavening. This is why commercial breads and baked goods are higher-phosphate than home-cooked plain rice.
Look for: "Baking powder," "Raising agent," "Leavening (E450, E451)," "Monocalcium phosphate," "Sodium acid pyrophosphate (SAPP)"
SAFE Natural phosphorus from whole food ingredients
Phosphorus listed from whole food ingredients — "chicken," "fish," "soy flour," "milk" — is organic phosphorus bound to protein, absorbed at only 40–60%. This is far less dangerous than inorganic phosphate additives. Still counts toward your daily phosphorus total, but at much lower bioavailability. The distinction: whole food phosphorus in moderation = acceptable; any inorganic phosphate additive = avoid regardless of amount.
The Silent Mineral

Spotting Hidden Potassium in Ingredients

Potassium now appears on Philippine nutrition labels — but ingredient lists reveal additional hidden sources that do not always show up as "potassium" in the nutrition panel.

DANGEROUS Potassium chloride — "low sodium" products
This is the most dangerous hidden potassium source for dialysis patients. Products marketed as "low sodium" or "reduced salt" frequently replace regular salt (sodium chloride) with potassium chloride — trading sodium for potassium. For dialysis patients with potassium restriction, "low sodium" products with potassium chloride can actually be more dangerous than regular-salt versions. Always check if "potassium chloride" appears in the ingredients when a product claims to be low sodium.
Look for: "Potassium chloride," "KCl," "Salt substitute," "Mineral salt (508)," "Lo-Salt," "Nu-Salt" brands
AVOID Potassium-containing preservatives
Potassium sorbate (E202) and potassium benzoate (E212) are common preservatives that add to total potassium load. While the amounts per serving are smaller than from natural food sources, they contribute in CKD patients already near their limit. They appear in juices, jams, dairy products, and many processed condiments.
Look for: "Potassium sorbate," "Potassium benzoate," "Preservative (202)," "Preservative (212)"
CAUTION Tomato-based products — higher than they appear
Tomato paste, tomato sauce, and ketchup concentrate potassium significantly when cooked down. A single tablespoon of tomato paste contains approximately 160mg potassium. Filipino dishes using concentrated tomato products (menudo, mechado) accumulate potassium in the sauce. Use fresh tomato in small amounts instead of paste and discard the cooking liquid.
CAUTION Dried fruits and fruit concentrates
Drying concentrates potassium dramatically — 1 cup of raisins contains ~1,086mg potassium (more than half a dialysis patient's daily limit). Fruit juice concentrates used in beverages, energy bars, and flavored products add significant potassium. Check ingredients for: "fruit juice concentrate," "date syrup," "dried banana," "coconut sugar" (surprisingly high potassium).
Sodium Disguises

All the Ways Sodium Hides on Philippine Labels

Sodium chloride (table salt) is only one of many sodium-containing food additives. CKD patients counting sodium from the label alone will underestimate their intake if they do not recognize sodium's other forms.

Sodium forms to recognize

  • Sodium chloride (asin / table salt)
  • Monosodium glutamate (MSG / Vetsin / AJI-NO-MOTO)
  • Sodium bicarbonate (baking soda)
  • Sodium benzoate (preservative)
  • Sodium nitrate / nitrite (cured meats)
  • Disodium inosinate (flavor enhancer)
  • Disodium guanylate (flavor enhancer)
  • Sodium phosphate (preservative + phosphate)
  • Sodium citrate (acidity regulator)
  • Sodium lactate (humectant)

Philippine products very high in sodium

  • Instant noodles (Lucky Me, Nissin) — 1,500–2,000mg/pack
  • Processed meats (hotdog, longanisa, tocino) — 600–1,200mg/100g
  • Soy sauce (toyo) — 879mg/tbsp
  • Fish sauce (patis) — 1,400mg/tbsp
  • Shrimp paste (bagoong) — 3,000mg/tbsp
  • Oyster sauce — 570mg/tbsp
  • Canned corned beef — 890mg/100g
  • Commercial lechon sauce — 740mg/tbsp
  • Packaged seasoning sachets
  • Instant soups and stock cubes

Lower-sodium alternatives available locally

  • Coconut aminos — lower sodium than toyo, available at health stores
  • Calamansi juice — adds umami flavor with zero sodium
  • Garlic and ginger — aromatics with no sodium
  • Vinegar (sukà) — flavor without salt, low sodium
  • Unseasoned fresh/frozen fish — no added sodium
  • Homemade chicken broth (no salt) — control sodium yourself
  • "No added salt" canned tomatoes — check label, some have sodium citrate
  • Herbs: rosemary, thyme, oregano — zero sodium flavor
Supermarket Navigation

Aisle-by-Aisle Guide — Philippine Supermarket

Green = spend most time here. Yellow = proceed with caution, read every label. Red = high risk, approach with a specific list only.

The safe shopping zones

  • Fresh fish and meat counter — unmarinated, unseasoned fresh protein with no additives. This is always the safest protein source.
  • Fresh vegetable section — buy whole vegetables, not pre-cut or marinated. Pre-cut vegetables often have salt added.
  • Rice section — white rice, no seasoning sachets. Buy plain rice, not flavored rice mixes.
  • Egg section — plain eggs, no marinated balut or salted eggs for CKD.
  • Bottled water section — mineral water or purified water, always.

Aisles to approach with a list only

  • Canned goods — almost all are high sodium with phosphate preservatives. Exception: "no added salt" canned tuna in water (check label carefully).
  • Instant noodle / soup aisle — avoid entirely. No safe options for CKD Stage 3+.
  • Snack aisle — only plain unsalted crackers (Sky Flakes plain, Fita plain). Everything else: avoid.
  • Condiment aisle — buy only vinegar and olive oil. Read every other label before buying.
  • Processed meat / chilled section — no hotdogs, no longganisa, no tocino, no corned beef, no processed ham.

Printable Reference

Your CKD Shopping Checklist

Print this checklist and bring it to the supermarket. Use it until label reading becomes second nature — most patients internalize these rules within 4–6 shopping trips.

📋 Before picking up any packaged product:

Find "Servings per container" — write it down or remember it
Check Sodium per serving — multiply by servings per container for total
Check Potassium per serving — multiply by servings per container
Flip to ingredients list — scan for any word containing "phosphate"
Scan for "potassium chloride" — if present, this is a high-potassium product regardless of "low sodium" claim
Check total sodium contribution to your daily budget (<2,000mg/day)

🚫 Never buy regardless of label:

Any carbonated drink (phosphoric acid)
Instant noodles (any brand)
Processed meats: hotdog, longganisa, tocino, ham, corned beef
Flavored chips and crackers (plain unsalted only)
Bagoong, patis (even small bottles — too high sodium)
Sports drinks, energy drinks
Any product with phosphate listed in first 5 ingredients

✅ Always safe to buy (no label reading required):

Fresh fish (tilapia, labahita, bangus — unmarinated)
Fresh chicken (whole or breast — no seasoning injection)
Eggs (plain)
White rice (plain, no flavoring sachets)
Low-K vegetables: sayote, upo, patola, cabbage, pipino, labanos
Fresh tofu (tokwa — in water, no brine)
Mineral or purified water
Calamansi (fresh)
Garlic, ginger, onion (fresh aromatics)
Olive oil or canola oil (plain, no flavor infusions)
📱

A practical shortcut: photograph the label

Take a photo of any new product's nutrition facts and ingredients list before buying. Review it calmly at home before consuming. This is especially useful when shopping with family who may add items to the cart. If the product passes your label check at home, you can confidently buy it next time. Build your own personal list of approved products over time.

Important: Dietary limits vary by individual CKD stage, current lab values, and clinical condition. These guidelines represent general KDIGO 2024 targets. Your specific sodium, potassium, and phosphorus limits should be confirmed with your nephrologist and registered nutritionist-dietitian. When in doubt, choose fresh whole foods over packaged products — fresh food does not require a label.
