Hemodialysis Modalities Explained

Conventional HD, HDF, home hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, CRRT, SLED — what each modality does differently, when each is used, and what the evidence says about outcomes.

Fundamentals

How Dialysis Removes Waste — Two Principles

All dialysis modalities work through two physical principles: diffusion (molecules move across a semi-permeable membrane from high to low concentration) and convection (fluid and dissolved molecules are pushed through a membrane by pressure). The key difference between modalities is which molecules they clear most effectively — and this determines clinical outcomes.

Diffusion clears small molecules well (urea, creatinine, potassium) but misses middle molecules. Convection uses pressure to push fluid through a larger-pore membrane — clearing middle molecules like β2-microglobulin that cause dialysis amyloidosis and cardiovascular disease over time.

Standard Modality

Conventional Hemodialysis (CHD)

Standard in-center hemodialysis

Conventional Intermittent HD (3×/week)

The global standard — used in all Philippine dialysis centers

Blood is pumped at 250–400 mL/min through a dialyzer (artificial kidney) — a filter with thousands of hollow fiber membranes. Blood flows inside the fibers; dialysis solution flows in the opposite direction outside (countercurrent flow maximizes concentration gradient). Three sessions per week, 3.5–4.5 hours each.

What it clears well: Urea, creatinine, potassium, phosphorus (partially), bicarbonate, water. What it misses: Middle molecules (β2-microglobulin, cytokines, protein-bound uremic toxins like indoxyl sulfate) — these accumulate over years and drive dialysis-related amyloidosis and cardiovascular disease.

Blood flow: 250–400 mL/min Dialysate: 500–800 mL/min Session: 3.5–4.5 hrs Frequency: 3×/week spKt/V target: ≥1.4 PH: Standard of care
⚠

The inter-dialytic interval problem

The 68-hour gap between Friday and Monday sessions is the most physiologically stressful period for conventional HD patients — potassium and fluid accumulate dangerously. The Monday morning post-weekend session has the highest cardiovascular event and death rate of any dialysis session. This has driven interest in more frequent modalities.

Superior Clearance

Hemodiafiltration (HDF) — Adding Convection

High-volume online HDF

Online Hemodiafiltration (OL-HDF)

HDF = Hemodialysis + Hemofiltration simultaneously

HDF combines diffusion (like conventional HD) with high-volume convective transport — producing 20–25 liters of ultrafiltrate per session and replacing this with ultrapure substitution fluid prepared online from the dialysis water supply. The result: dramatically superior clearance of middle molecules (β2-microglobulin, FGF-23, light chains, cytokines) while maintaining small molecule clearance equivalent to conventional HD.

The ESHOL trial (2013) and subsequent meta-analyses showed high-volume OL-HDF reduces all-cause mortality by 30–35% and cardiovascular mortality by 33–45% compared to conventional HD — one of the strongest outcome signals in the dialysis literature. KDIGO 2024 now suggests HDF for patients tolerating conventional HD who want improved outcomes.

Substitution vol: ≥23 L/session Blood flow: 300–450 mL/min Session: 4–5 hrs β2-MG clearance: 3–4× better than CHD PH: Available at select centers
Home Modality

Home Hemodialysis (HHD)

Frequent / extended home HD

Short Daily HD or Nocturnal HD at Home

5–7 sessions/week — simulating near-continuous kidney function

Short daily home HD (5–6 sessions/week, 2–3 hours each) or nocturnal home HD (3–6 nights/week, 6–8 hours overnight while sleeping) provides dramatically more dialysis time than conventional in-center HD. The FHN daily trial and multiple cohort studies show: better BP control (often allowing antihypertensive reduction), superior phosphorus control, improved LV mass regression, better nutrition, higher quality of life, and improved survival.

Requires a trained home partner (family member or caregiver), home water treatment system, and machine. Not yet widely available in the Philippines — but represents the future direction of dialysis care for motivated patients with suitable home circumstances.

Frequency: 5–7×/week Nocturnal: 6–8 hrs/night Weekly Kt/V: much higher than conventional BP medications often reduced PH: Pilot programs only
Peritoneal Dialysis

Peritoneal Dialysis (PD) — Using Your Own Membrane

In PD, dialysate fluid is instilled into the peritoneal cavity through a surgically placed catheter. The peritoneum acts as the dialysis membrane — toxins and water move across it into the dialysate, which is then drained and replaced.

Manual PD

CAPD — Continuous Ambulatory PD

4 manual exchanges per day

Patient manually instills 2L of dialysate and allows 4–6 hours of dwell time before draining and refilling — 4 times daily. No machine needed. Freedom to move during dwell time. Continuous dialysis — no inter-dialytic toxin build-up. Available at lower cost in areas without electricity supply.

4 exchanges/dayNo machinePH: Available NKTI, select centers
Automated PD

APD / CCPD — Automated PD

Overnight machine-assisted exchanges

A cycler machine performs 8–12 exchanges during 8–10 hours of sleep. Patient is free during the day. Superior quality of life — dialysis happens while sleeping. Preferred for employed patients, children, and those with back pain limiting manual exchanges.

Overnight: 8–10 hrsCycler machineDay freedom
Intensive Care

Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT)

ICU — Critically ill patients

CRRT — CVVH / CVVHD / CVVHDF

Continuous 24-hour dialysis for hemodynamically unstable patients

Critically ill patients with AKI in the ICU often cannot tolerate the rapid fluid and solute shifts of conventional intermittent HD — they develop hypotension during treatment. CRRT runs continuously at slow rates (150–300 mL/min blood flow) — removing fluid and toxins gently over 24 hours, matching the body's capacity for re-equilibration.

Modalities: CVVH (convection only), CVVHD (diffusion only), CVVHDF (both). Anticoagulation required — regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) is preferred in bleeding-risk patients. CRRT is not superior to IHD for survival in unselected AKI — but is preferred when hemodynamic instability prevents conventional HD.

Continuous 24/7 in ICU Blood flow: 150–250 mL/min Citrate anticoagulation preferred High-volume CRRT: ≥35 mL/kg/hr PH: Available at tertiary ICUs
Hybrid Modality

SLED — Sustained Low Efficiency Dialysis

Hybrid ICU modality

SLED / SLEDD — Sustained Low-Efficiency Daily Dialysis

Extended-duration intermittent HD — the bridge between IHD and CRRT

SLED uses conventional HD equipment at lower blood flow (150–200 mL/min) and dialysate flow (150–300 mL/min) for 6–12 hours daily — providing more gentle solute and fluid removal than conventional 4-hour HD while using hospital HD machines rather than dedicated CRRT equipment. Cost-effective alternative to CRRT in hemodynamically compromised AKI patients. Allows machine and nursing staff to be shared across patients.

Increasingly used in Philippine tertiary hospitals where dedicated CRRT machines are limited. Provides equivalent AKI outcomes to CRRT in most hemodynamically unstable patients at lower cost.

Duration: 6–12 hrs/day Blood flow: 150–200 mL/min Uses standard HD machines Lower cost than CRRT PH: Available most tertiary centers

Why your nephrologist may recommend HDF over conventional HD

If you are receiving conventional hemodialysis and your center has the capacity for online HDF, ask your nephrologist about switching. The evidence for mortality benefit from high-volume OL-HDF is among the strongest in the dialysis literature. The main requirements are: a dialysis machine capable of HDF mode, online water purification infrastructure, and blood flows above 300 mL/min through your access.

Important: The optimal dialysis modality depends on individual factors — access type, cardiovascular stability, home circumstances, center capabilities, and patient preference. Discuss modality choice with your nephrologist.
