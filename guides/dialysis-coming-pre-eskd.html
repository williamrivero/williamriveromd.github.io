Why This Moment Matters

You are in the most important window of your kidney disease.

If your nephrologist has told you that you are in CKD Stage 4 or Stage 5 — meaning your eGFR is between 15 and 29 — you are standing at the most consequential point in the entire kidney-disease journey. The decisions you make in the next 12 to 24 months will shape the rest of your life with kidney disease. Not the decisions you make once dialysis has started. The ones you make before.

Six things need to happen during this window. You need to choose a treatment direction (hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or conservative management). You need to plan a vascular access if you choose hemodialysis. You need to transition to a pre-dialysis diet. You need to prepare yourself and your family emotionally. You need to enrol in PhilHealth's Z-Benefit Package. And you need to keep your kidneys protected while you do all of this.

Most Filipino patients arrive at dialysis having done none of these things. They start dialysis through the emergency room, with a temporary catheter in their neck, on a treatment they did not choose. This guide exists so that does not happen to you.

What "the window" actually means

An eGFR of 15–29 mL/min/1.73m² means your kidneys are working at roughly one quarter to one third of normal capacity. This is not a crisis — most patients in this range still feel reasonably well. But it is also not "early" disease. The ramp toward dialysis (or conservative care) is now visible. How quickly that ramp moves depends on the cause of your kidney disease, your blood pressure control, your diabetes control, and a few things partly outside your control.

📈

Typical timeline from this window

Most Filipino patients in the eGFR 15–29 range will reach dialysis within 1 to 4 years. Some progress faster (especially with poorly-controlled diabetes or significant proteinuria); some plateau for a long time. The clinical work of the next 6 months is to find out which group you are in — and to plan accordingly.

The First Decision

Three real choices: hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or conservative care.

The most important thing to understand is that there is a real choice here. Hemodialysis is what most people picture — but it is not the only option, and for many patients it is not the best one. Peritoneal dialysis can preserve much more independence. Conservative kidney management — focused care without dialysis — is a legitimate path for some patients, especially those who are very frail or elderly. None of these is a default. All of them require your active decision.

Hemodialysis (HD)

Three sessions per week, four hours each, at a dialysis centre. You sit in a chair while a machine cleans your blood through a vascular access on your arm. Most common in the Philippines. Centre-based — staff handle everything. Less daily responsibility, but rigid schedule and limited dietary freedom.

Peritoneal dialysis (PD)

Done at home, every day. A soft tube in your abdomen lets dialysis fluid wash your blood internally. Either four manual exchanges per day (CAPD) or overnight on a small machine while you sleep (APD). You keep your job and travel more freely. Higher daily self-care load — needs a clean home environment.

Conservative (no dialysis)

Active medical care focused on symptom control, quality of life, and dignity — without ever starting dialysis. For very elderly or very frail patients with multiple serious illnesses, modern evidence shows survival is often similar, with fewer hospital days and better quality of life. Not "giving up" — a different goal.

How to think about the choice

The right modality is the one that fits your life, your family, and your home — not the one that is most common. Some honest questions to think about:

⚖️

What the evidence says

For most patients under 70 who are reasonably independent, HD and PD have similar long-term survival. The difference is lifestyle, not life expectancy. PD lets you stay working and traveling; HD removes the daily responsibility but ties you to a centre schedule. Filipino kidney transplant rates are still low, but if transplant is on the horizon, both HD and PD are bridges to it. The "wrong" choice is not choosing — defaulting into dialysis through an emergency room.

The Most Time-Sensitive Step

If you choose hemodialysis, your access must be planned now.

This is the single most important sentence in this guide for HD-bound patients: an arteriovenous fistula (AVF) takes 3 to 6 months to mature before it can be used. If you wait until your eGFR is 8 or 10 to think about access, it is too late — you will start dialysis through a temporary catheter in your neck, with a much higher risk of infection and a worse first year of treatment. The right time to place an AVF is when your eGFR is around 20 to 25.

An AVF is the safest, longest-lasting hemodialysis access. A small surgery (usually in the wrist or upper arm, under local anaesthesia, as an outpatient) connects an artery to a vein. Over the next 8 to 24 weeks, the vein thickens and grows under the higher pressure — this is "maturation." Once mature, it provides reliable, low-infection-risk access for years.

The alternatives — AV grafts (synthetic tube) and tunneled catheters — exist for situations where an AVF is not possible, but they have higher infection rates and shorter lifespans. Plan for an AVF first, fall back to alternatives only if necessary.

The access planning timeline

eGFR 25–29 (Stage 4)
Vein-mapping referral. An ultrasound of your arm veins to see which one is best for an AVF. Ask your nephrologist for a referral to a vascular surgeon for vein mapping. Start protecting the non-dominant arm: no IV lines, no blood draws, no BP cuffs on that arm from now on.
eGFR 20–25 (late Stage 4)
AVF placement. The optimal window for surgery. Outpatient procedure under local anaesthesia, typically 1–2 hours. You go home the same day. Start gentle hand-grip exercises (squeezing a soft ball) the next day to encourage maturation.
eGFR 15–20 (early Stage 5)
AVF maturation and surveillance. Your AVF is "growing in." Monthly checks by your vascular surgeon or nephrologist to confirm it is maturing. If it is not maturing well, intervention now (angioplasty or revision) gives time to fix it.
eGFR 10–15 (Stage 5)
AVF should be ready. A mature AVF can be cannulated. Dialysis can start through your AVF the moment it is needed — no neck catheter, no infection risk, no rushed surgery.
eGFR <10 (or symptoms)
Dialysis initiation. Through your mature AVF, on a planned schedule, at a centre you have already chosen. This is what success looks like.

If you choose peritoneal dialysis, the timing is different

The PD catheter (a Tenckhoff catheter) is placed in your abdomen 2 to 4 weeks before you actually need to start dialysis — much later than an AVF. The "break-in" period is brief. But you still need to choose PD now so your home can be prepared, the catheter surgery can be scheduled, and PhilHealth coverage can be arranged. More on PD catheter care here.

⚠️

The number-one access mistake in the Philippines

Starting hemodialysis through a temporary internal jugular (neck) catheter because the AVF was placed too late or never placed. This happens to roughly half of new Filipino HD patients. The temporary catheter causes a 10-fold higher infection rate in the first year, doubles your hospitalisation rate, and worsens long-term mortality. The fix is simple: plan AVF placement at eGFR 20–25, not at the moment of crisis.

Daily Decisions

The pre-dialysis diet: different from the dialysis diet.

One of the most confusing things about CKD Stage 4–5 is that the dietary advice now is not the same as the dietary advice once you are on dialysis. The pre-dialysis diet is designed to slow kidney damage, control blood pressure, and prevent uremic symptoms — typically lower-protein and very low-sodium. The dialysis diet is designed to replace what dialysis cannot fully clean and is often higher in protein.

Sodium

Aggressive reduction. <2 g sodium/day (~5 g salt). No patis, no toyo, no Maggi cubes, no instant noodles, no processed meats. This is the single most modifiable factor in slowing kidney decline. Full Filipino-food sodium guide.

Protein

Moderate restriction. 0.6–0.8 g/kg/day in pre-dialysis CKD (this is roughly one palm-sized serving of fish or chicken per day for a 60-kg adult). Once you start dialysis, this jumps to 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day. Discuss any protein change with your nephrologist or renal dietitian.

Potassium & phosphorus

Limit if your blood levels are rising. Foods to watch: buko juice, bananas, oranges, tomato sauce, kamote tops, dried fish, processed cheese, soft drinks (cola). Your nephrologist will tell you how strict to be based on your monthly bloodwork.

Fluid: usually still free at this stage

Unlike dialysis patients, most pre-dialysis patients can still drink to thirst as long as they are urinating normally. Fluid restriction starts when urine output drops or you develop swelling — typically after dialysis begins. Do not restrict fluids on your own — ask your nephrologist when (and if) it should start.

🥗

One realistic pre-dialysis day on a Filipino plate

  • Breakfast: 1 cup of rice, 1 small piece of grilled fish (no patis), 1 boiled egg, slices of cucumber and tomato.
  • Lunch: 1 cup of rice, half cup ginisang sayote (no Magic Sarap), 1 piece adobong manok (small thigh), pinakbet without bagoong.
  • Dinner: 1 cup rice, sinigang na isda made with sampalok concentrate (no instant mix), ½ cup sautéed kalabasa.
  • Avoid: instant noodles, hotdogs, tocino, longganisa, canned sardines/corned beef, salted eggs, dried fish, soft drinks, store-bought sauces.
The Inner Work

Preparing your mind, your family, and your work.

Almost every patient remembers two moments in their CKD journey: the first time they heard the word "kidney failure," and the day dialysis actually began. The space between those moments — the months you are in right now — is when the emotional work either happens, or doesn't. The patients who do this work cope better; the ones who don't tend to crash hard at the start of dialysis.

What patients actually feel

  • Anger: at doctors who didn't catch it earlier, at family members who don't understand, at God or fate.
  • Grief: over the body that worked, the future that is changing.
  • Bargaining: "if I follow the diet perfectly, will my kidneys come back?" (They will not — but you can slow things down a lot.)
  • Fear: of the needles, of dependency, of being a burden, of dying.
  • Eventually — acceptance, and a different kind of normal.

What helps, in real life

  • Visit a dialysis centre while you're still well. Demystify it. Talk to a patient who has been on dialysis for a few years — most will say the first few months were the hardest.
  • Tell your immediate family early. Don't make them piece it together later. Bring your spouse or adult child to your nephrology visits.
  • Plan your work. If you have a flexible job, tell your employer once you have a treatment plan. PD patients often keep working full-time.
  • Talk to a counsellor or your priest, pastor, or imam. Faith and structured emotional support both help — and they help differently.
  • Don't wait for depression to declare itself. If you have lost interest in food, family, or sleep for more than 2 weeks, ask your nephrologist for a referral.
👨‍👩‍👧

For the family

Filipino families are extraordinary at showing up — but the most helpful thing in the pre-dialysis window is not heroic acts. It is presence at clinic visits, willingness to learn the diet alongside the patient, honesty about what the household can and cannot afford, and a clear conversation about who will be the back-up support if dialysis becomes home-based (PD). Start these conversations now, not the week before treatment begins.

The Other Path

Conservative kidney management: not the same as giving up.

For some patients — especially those who are very elderly, very frail, or living with multiple serious illnesses — dialysis may not extend life meaningfully and may worsen the time that is left. Conservative kidney management (sometimes called "conservative care" or "supportive care") is the path of active medical care without ever starting dialysis. It is a legitimate, evidence-based choice. It is not abandonment.

What conservative care actually involves: excellent symptom management (treating itching, nausea, breathlessness, pain), careful blood pressure and anaemia control, dietary support for comfort rather than restriction, fluid balance through diuretics, and home-centred care with hospice or palliative-care involvement. The goal is not to extend life as long as possible — it is to make the time that remains as comfortable, present, and meaningful as possible.

For patients over 80 with multiple serious illnesses, modern evidence shows that conservative care often produces similar survival to dialysis, with fewer hospitalisations and better quality of life. For younger or healthier patients, dialysis usually extends life meaningfully. The decision is personal, and your nephrologist should be willing to discuss it openly.

If conservative management is the right path for you or your family member, see the dedicated advance care planning guide for the full conversation about goals of care, surrogate decision-makers, and family discussions.

The Practical Step Most Patients Skip

PhilHealth Z-Benefit Package: enrol now, before you need it.

PhilHealth's Z-Benefit Package for End-Stage Renal Disease covers a substantial portion of dialysis costs in accredited facilities. But it requires paperwork, accreditation, and several visits to set up. The patients who arrive at dialysis with their PhilHealth Z-Benefit already in place have far less financial chaos in their first year. The ones who don't can spend the first three months of dialysis trying to navigate the system while also adapting to a new treatment.

The PhilHealth pre-ESKD enrolment checklist

  • Ensure your PhilHealth membership is active — verify in person at a PhilHealth office or via the Member Portal. Pay any back-contributions if you are a self-employed/voluntary member.
  • Add your spouse and dependents if not already declared — this matters if a family caregiver will be the principal member during your treatment.
  • Get the Z-Benefit application form from your nephrologist or accredited dialysis centre. Required documents typically include the MD certification, a recent eGFR, and an ICD-10 diagnosis code.
  • Choose an accredited dialysis facility — confirm in writing that the centre is currently PhilHealth-Z-accredited, not just PhilHealth-accredited.
  • Coordinate with the centre's PhilHealth liaison — every accredited centre has one. Bring your application package and your nephrologist's referral letter.
  • For Indigent or Senior-Citizen members: bring your Senior Citizen ID, MSWDO certificate, or 4Ps documentation as applicable — these affect your premium status and waiting periods.
  • Keep a folder with all original receipts, certifications, and forms — both physical and a phone-photo backup. The system runs on paper.
💰

The realistic cost picture

Even with PhilHealth Z-Benefit fully active, most Filipino dialysis patients have out-of-pocket costs of PHP 8,000–25,000 per month for things PhilHealth does not fully cover (EPO, iron, transport, food, missed sessions, occasional hospitalisations). Discuss this honestly with your family now. Some patients access additional support through PCSO, DSWD's Medical Assistance Program (MAP), or local government LGU programs — your social worker can help navigate these.

Bring This to Your Next Visit

Questions to ask your nephrologist this month.

The most useful clinic visits are the ones where you arrive with questions written down. Here are the questions that matter at your stage. Bring them. Print them. Ask all of them.

About your trajectory

  • What is my eGFR today, and how has it changed over the last year?
  • Based on the cause of my kidney disease and my current control, when do you think I am likely to need dialysis — within a year, within 2–3 years, or longer?
  • What is my UPCR or albumin/creatinine ratio? Is my proteinuria controlled?
  • Are there any medications you can adjust to slow progression further?

About modality

  • Have I been formally counselled on HD vs PD vs conservative care? If not, can I have that visit scheduled?
  • Given my home, my work, and my family situation, what would you recommend, and why?
  • Is conservative management something we should discuss, given my age and health?

About access

  • If HD is the plan, when should I be referred for vein mapping?
  • Which arm should I protect from now — and what does "protection" mean in practice?
  • Who is the vascular surgeon you would refer me to for AVF placement?
  • If PD is the plan, when should the catheter be scheduled?

About PhilHealth and cost

  • Which dialysis centres near me are PhilHealth Z-accredited?
  • Can your office help me start the Z-Benefit application?
  • Roughly what should we plan for in out-of-pocket cost per month, with PhilHealth?
For Clinicians · The Pre-ESKD Window

The eGFR 15–29 window: highest-yield intervention point in nephrology.

The pre-ESKD window represents the highest-leverage period in the chronic kidney disease care continuum. The decisions made between eGFR 29 and dialysis initiation drive first-year mortality, first-year hospitalisation, vascular access patency, modality satisfaction, and out-of-pocket cost — far more than any decision made after dialysis begins.

Identifying the window in clinic

The simplest operational definition: any patient with eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m² should trigger structured pre-ESKD planning at the next clinic visit. In high-risk subgroups — type 2 diabetics with persistent UPCR >1 g, polycystic kidney disease with ESKD already present in first-degree relatives, glomerulonephritis with active immunosuppression failing — pre-ESKD planning should begin at eGFR <45.

📊

Risk prediction

The Tangri 4-variable Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE: age, sex, eGFR, UPCR) is well-validated in Asian cohorts and remains the most useful bedside tool. A 2-year KFRE >10% should prompt formal modality counselling; >20% should trigger access planning. In Filipino diabetic CKD, observed rates often exceed predicted; clinical judgment should err toward earlier preparation.

For Clinicians · Modality Education

Structured modality counselling: when, by whom, and what.

The single most consistent finding in pre-ESKD outcomes research is that structured modality education early in Stage 4 increases home-dialysis uptake, reduces unplanned starts, and improves long-term satisfaction. Despite this, the Philippines remains under-served — formal modality education programmes are concentrated in tertiary urban centres, and most Stage 4 patients in private practice never receive a structured visit.

A workable Filipino-practice modality education protocol
1
Trigger: eGFR <30 (or <45 if KFRE 2-yr >20%). Schedule a dedicated 30–45 min counselling visit, separate from routine nephrology follow-up.
2
Cover three options explicitly: in-centre HD, peritoneal dialysis (CAPD/APD), and conservative kidney management. Do not present HD as the default. Do not omit conservative care for elderly/frail patients.
3
Use the patient's actual life as the framing: work schedule, home environment, distance to centre, family caregivers, financial reality. Generic pamphlets do not move decisions; matched scenarios do.
4
Include a peer-patient encounter. Ten minutes with an experienced PD or HD patient changes more decisions than thirty minutes with a physician.
5
Document the decision in the chart with the date, the modality chosen, and the rationale. A chart-documented decision is the trigger for downstream access referral and PhilHealth planning.
6
Revisit at every follow-up. Modality decisions can and do change as patients learn more. Document changes the same way.

Filipino practice gaps

  • PD under-utilisation. National PD share remains <10% despite favourable evidence and PhilHealth coverage. Drivers: physician comfort, training pipeline, and home-environment concerns. The fix is structural — increased PD-trained nephrologists and a PD-first messaging at the modality visit.
  • Conservative care under-discussed. Only a minority of nephrologists in Filipino practice formally discuss conservative kidney management even when it is the better option. The cultural framing of dialysis as "non-negotiable" leaves patients and families without the language to choose otherwise.
  • Dietitian access. Renal dietitian visits are not consistently available in mid-tier centres; the modality visit often becomes the only nutrition counselling opportunity. Use it.
For Clinicians · Vascular Access

Vascular access planning algorithm.

The KDOQI Vascular Access Guidelines (2019, revised 2024) and Filipino consensus recommendations align on the central principle: AVF first, place early, surveil obsessively. Operationalising this in Filipino practice requires that the nephrologist take ownership of timing — vein mapping referrals are routinely delayed by 6–12 months, and placement happens too close to dialysis initiation.

Pre-ESKD access pathway
1
Modality decision documented as HD → immediate referral for upper-extremity vein mapping (duplex ultrasound). Do not wait for symptoms.
2
Arm protection education at the same visit: non-dominant arm preferred. Patient counselled to refuse all peripheral IVs, BP cuffs, and venipuncture on that arm. Provide a physical reminder (wristband or chart sticker).
3
Vascular surgery referral at eGFR 25: for AVF placement at eGFR 20–25. Outpatient, regional anaesthesia, day-of-surgery discharge in routine cases.
4
Monthly maturation surveillance after placement. Physical exam (thrill, bruit, augmentation) plus duplex at 6–8 weeks. Early intervention (angioplasty or revision) for non-maturing AVF.
5
Cannulation testing by experienced HD nurse before first dialysis use. Document successful cannulation in chart.
6
Backup plans: if AVF maturation fails, AV graft is preferred over tunneled catheter. Tunneled catheter is acceptable bridge only when both AVF and graft are not feasible (severely compromised vasculature, urgent dialysis need, transient access for transplant work-up).

For PD-bound patients

PD catheter placement timing is more flexible: 2–4 weeks before anticipated dialysis need. Use this lead time to: (1) confirm home environment suitability, (2) train the patient and family, (3) coordinate with the PD centre on supply delivery logistics. Avoid simultaneous AVF and PD catheter placement unless the patient is uncertain about modality and a backup is genuinely needed — usually the answer is to make the modality decision more decisively, not to hedge with two access procedures.

🩺

The Filipino access realities you cannot ignore

  • Vascular surgery wait times in public tertiary centres can extend 3–6 months. Build that into your timing — refer at eGFR 25 minimum, earlier if your local surgical queue is long.
  • Peripheral vasculature in long-standing diabetics may not support a wrist AVF. Do not delay vein mapping waiting for "the right moment" — get the surgeon's assessment early.
  • Patient-driven delays are common (fear of surgery, desire to avoid "marking" the arm). The fix is the modality visit done well, plus a brief in-clinic conversation with a patient who already has a working AVF.
For Clinicians · Conservative Management

Conservative kidney management as a structured alternative.

Conservative kidney management (CKM) is supportive, comprehensive, non-dialytic care for patients who, for medical or value-based reasons, will not initiate dialysis. Modern evidence (UK, Canadian, Australian cohorts) shows that for patients aged ≥80 with significant comorbidity, dialysis often does not extend life meaningfully and frequently worsens quality of life and time at home. Filipino practice has been slow to adopt structured CKM, partly because of cultural framing and partly because palliative care infrastructure is concentrated in a few tertiary centres.

Identifying patients who may benefit from CKM discussion
1
Age ≥80 with multiple comorbidities (especially advanced cardiac disease, advanced dementia, active malignancy with limited prognosis).
2
Surprise question: "Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?" If no — open the conservative care conversation.
3
Functional status: Karnofsky <40, dependent for ADLs, recent decline in mobility — predictors of poor dialysis outcomes.
4
Patient preference: some younger, less-comorbid patients also choose CKM for value-based reasons. Honour this with structured care, not abandonment.

Components of structured CKM

  • Symptom-focused care: uremic pruritus, restless legs, fatigue, breathlessness, anorexia, nausea — all of which have specific therapies.
  • BP and volume management with diuretics and selective antihypertensives; goal is comfort, not numerical perfection.
  • Anaemia management with ESA and iron, calibrated to symptoms (target Hb ~10 g/dL for symptomatic relief).
  • Liberalised diet for quality of life — strict low-sodium, low-potassium diets that worsen anorexia in a frail patient are counterproductive.
  • Advance care planning conversations (see the dedicated ACP guide) — surrogate decision-maker, code status, hospitalisation preferences, location-of-death preference.
  • Co-management with palliative care when available; primary-care-led CKM is feasible in regions without specialist palliative services.
For Clinicians · PhilHealth Pathway

PhilHealth Z-Benefit and pre-ESKD financial planning.

The PhilHealth Z-Benefit Package for ESRD provides bundled coverage for HD or PD initiation in accredited centres. The package is generous by Filipino standards but underutilised — many patients begin dialysis without the application processed, then spend the first 90 days both adapting to dialysis and chasing paperwork. The pre-ESKD window is the right time to set this up.

Pre-ESKD financial planning workflow
1
At modality decision visit: verify PhilHealth membership status and dependent enrolment. Voluntary/self-employed members in arrears should be advised to clear back-contributions early.
2
Identify a PhilHealth-Z-accredited dialysis facility in the patient's geographic catchment. Z-accreditation is a separate credential from standard PhilHealth accreditation — confirm both with the centre.
3
Initiate Z-Benefit application: physician certification (eGFR, ICD-10 N18.5/N18.6), referral letter, supporting labs. Process typically takes 4–8 weeks.
4
Document additional support pathways: PCSO Medical Assistance, DSWD MAP, LGU social welfare, and (for indigent) PhilHealth Indigent Program. Refer to medical social worker if available.
5
Set realistic expectations: even with full Z-Benefit, monthly out-of-pocket for HD typically PHP 8,000–25,000 (EPO, iron, transport, food, occasional admissions). PD out-of-pocket may be lower or higher depending on home logistics.
📋

Practical pearls

  • Many accredited centres have a dedicated PhilHealth liaison — refer to that person directly rather than sending the patient through general admissions.
  • Senior Citizens Act and PWD discounts apply additionally to non-PhilHealth-covered items (laboratory, pharmacy) — ensure documentation is in place.
  • For patients living far from a Z-accredited centre, transport itself can be the dominant cost; this should be part of the modality decision (PD avoids 3×/week transport).
For Clinicians · Pre-ESKD Checklist

The pre-ESKD bundle: a clinic-ready checklist.

A consolidated checklist for use at every Stage 4–5 nephrology visit. Designed for Filipino outpatient practice and adaptable to either tertiary-centre or community settings.

At eGFR <30 (or KFRE 2-yr >10%)

  • KFRE calculated and documented in chart.
  • Structured modality counselling visit scheduled or completed (HD, PD, conservative — all three).
  • Vein-mapping ultrasound referred (if HD-leaning).
  • Non-dominant arm protection counselled and documented; physical reminder placed.
  • PhilHealth membership verified; Z-accredited centre identified.
  • NSAIDs, nephrotoxic herbals, and contrast use reviewed and minimised.
  • Renal dietitian referral if available; sodium and protein targets discussed.
  • Hepatitis B status confirmed (vaccinate if non-immune).
  • BP target reviewed (<130/80 with RAAS blockade per indication).

At eGFR 20–25

  • Vascular surgery referral for AVF placement (if HD chosen).
  • Modality decision re-confirmed and documented.
  • Z-Benefit application initiated.
  • Family meeting recommended; primary caregiver identified.
  • Anaemia work-up (Hb, ferritin, TSAT); ESA initiation if indicated.
  • CKD-MBD labs (Ca, P, iPTH, vit D) reviewed and managed.
  • Bicarbonate checked; supplementation if <22.

At eGFR 15–20

  • AVF maturation surveillance ongoing (or PD home preparation underway).
  • Z-Benefit fully approved and on file at chosen centre.
  • Volume status re-evaluated at every visit; uremic symptom screen.
  • Hyperkalemia management plan in place (dietary, RAASi adjustments, K-binder if indicated).
  • Transplant work-up considered if patient is candidate (age, comorbidity, family donor).
  • Advance care planning conversation initiated, especially if frail or elderly.

At eGFR <15 or symptomatic

  • Dialysis initiation through planned access (mature AVF or PD catheter), not a temporary line.
  • Centre admission process confirmed; first session date set.
  • Transport logistics confirmed (3×/week is a sustained logistical burden — re-verify).
  • Transition diet counselling (pre-dialysis → dialysis-specific).
  • First Z-Benefit cycle activated; out-of-pocket budget confirmed with family.
