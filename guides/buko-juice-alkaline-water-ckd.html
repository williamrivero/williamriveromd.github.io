Buko juice — the clear liquid from young green coconuts — is one of the most beloved Filipino beverages and is widely marketed as a "natural health drink," a "kidney cleanser," and an "electrolyte replenisher." This is the most widespread misconception. The kidney is a filtration organ — it does not require a "cleanser." Buko juice has diuretic properties in healthy people because the healthy kidney excretes its potassium, sodium, and fluid load. In CKD patients, these minerals cannot be excreted — they accumulate. There is no clinical evidence that buko juice improves kidney function, removes uremic toxins, or slows CKD progression. The only things that do this are evidence-based medications (ACE inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, finerenone), blood pressure control, and blood sugar management.Ito ang pinaka-laganap na maling kaalaman. There is very limited evidence that buko juice might slightly increase urine citrate — a compound that inhibits calcium oxalate stone formation — in healthy individuals. Partially True — Context Matters

Use Caution — Stage Dependent

In CKD Stage 1–2 (eGFR >60), occasional small amounts of buko juice are unlikely to cause acute harm — the kidneys can still excrete potassium, though less efficiently. However, as CKD progresses (Stage 3+), the margin of safety narrows significantly. For Stage 3b (eGFR 30–44), limit to occasional very small servings only. For Stage 4–5 and dialysis — avoid entirely. Always check your serum potassium level before making decisions about high-potassium foods.
