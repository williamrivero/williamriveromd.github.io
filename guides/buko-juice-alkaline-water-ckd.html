Part 1 — Buko Juice

Buko Juice (Young Coconut Water) — What Is It Really?

Buko juice — the clear liquid from young green coconuts — is one of the most beloved Filipino beverages and is widely marketed as a "natural health drink," a "kidney cleanser," and an "electrolyte replenisher." For healthy people, many of these claims have merit. For CKD patients, buko juice can be dangerous — and in dialysis patients, potentially fatal. The reason comes down to one mineral: potassium.

A single 240 mL glass of fresh buko juice contains approximately 600 mg of potassium. For a dialysis patient on a 1,500 mg daily potassium restriction, one glass uses up 40% of the entire day's allowance — before any food is eaten.

The Core Problem

Why Potassium Is Dangerous in CKD

Healthy kidneys excrete excess potassium continuously in the urine. CKD kidneys cannot — potassium accumulates in the blood. The heart is exquisitely sensitive to serum potassium levels. Hyperkalemia (high blood potassium) causes life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias — often without warning symptoms.

Unlike blood sugar which has gradual effects, hyperkalemia can cause sudden cardiac arrest without warning. Dialysis removes potassium during sessions, but between sessions the kidneys cannot excrete it — making diet the only protection.

⚡

Cardiac arrest from buko juice — this has actually happened

Multiple documented cases exist in nephrology literature — and in Philippine clinical practice — of dialysis patients dying from hyperkalemia-induced cardiac arrest after drinking buko juice or coconut water. One or two large glasses can raise serum potassium by 1–2 mEq/L in a fluid-restricted dialysis patient. Combined with a missed dialysis session or a high-potassium meal, this can be fatal. This is not theoretical.

Separating Fact from Fiction

Buko Juice Claims — Myth-Busting for CKD Patients

Busted
"Buko juice cleanses the kidneys and flushes out toxins."
This is the most widespread misconception. The kidney is a filtration organ — it does not require a "cleanser." Buko juice has diuretic properties in healthy people because the healthy kidney excretes its potassium, sodium, and fluid load. In CKD patients, these minerals cannot be excreted — they accumulate. There is no clinical evidence that buko juice improves kidney function, removes uremic toxins, or slows CKD progression. The only things that do this are evidence-based medications (ACE inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, finerenone), blood pressure control, and blood sugar management.
Busted
"Buko juice is natural, so it's safe for my kidneys."
"Natural" is not a synonym for "safe" — especially in CKD. Potassium is entirely natural. Arsenic is natural. The kidney's job in a healthy person is to continuously excrete natural electrolytes that would otherwise accumulate to toxic levels. When that job is impaired, natural electrolytes like potassium become lethal. Star fruit (balimbing) is also natural — and it causes acute kidney failure and death in CKD patients. The physiological impact matters — not the source.
Busted
"Buko juice is good for kidney stones."
There is very limited evidence that buko juice might slightly increase urine citrate — a compound that inhibits calcium oxalate stone formation — in healthy individuals. However, the evidence is weak and based on very small studies. For CKD patients, the potassium risk far outweighs any theoretical stone benefit. For kidney stone prevention in CKD, the evidence-based approach is: adequate fluid intake (plain water), dietary oxalate restriction, adequate dietary calcium, and potassium citrate supplementation if prescribed. Buko juice is not a substitute.
Partially True — Context Matters
"Buko juice is a good electrolyte drink for hydration."
For healthy athletes and active people — yes. Buko juice is an excellent natural electrolyte beverage after exercise-induced sweating. For people who can freely excrete potassium, it is a healthy alternative to commercial sports drinks. For CKD Stage 3+, dialysis patients, or anyone with hyperkalemia risk — no. The potassium that makes it a good hydration drink for healthy people is exactly what makes it dangerous in impaired kidney function. Sports drinks (Gatorade) are also high in sodium and potassium — equally problematic in CKD.
Use Caution — Stage Dependent
"I only have Stage 2 CKD so a little buko juice is fine."
In CKD Stage 1–2 (eGFR >60), occasional small amounts of buko juice are unlikely to cause acute harm — the kidneys can still excrete potassium, though less efficiently. However, as CKD progresses (Stage 3+), the margin of safety narrows significantly. For Stage 3b (eGFR 30–44), limit to occasional very small servings only. For Stage 4–5 and dialysis — avoid entirely. Always check your serum potassium level before making decisions about high-potassium foods. If potassium is already above 5.0 mEq/L, there is no safe amount.

💡

What about "buko sarap" or flavored coconut drinks?

Processed coconut water drinks (Zesto Buko, Coco Pandan, flavored buko drinks) contain added sugar and preservatives in addition to coconut water. They have the same potassium content as fresh buko juice, plus additional sodium from preservatives and phosphate from additives. These are not safer alternatives — they are equally or more problematic for CKD patients.

Part 2 — Alkaline Water

Alkaline Water — What Are the Claims?

Alkaline water (pH 8–9.5) is heavily marketed in the Philippines through multilevel marketing networks, water refilling stations, and social media. It is sold with claims ranging from anti-aging to cancer prevention to kidney protection. Here is what the claims are — and what the evidence actually shows.

What "alkaline water" actually is

Water with a pH above 7.0 — typically achieved through electrolysis (ionizers), mineral addition (calcium, magnesium bicarbonate), or bicarbonate tablets. Regular tap water is pH 6.5–8.5. The stomach immediately acidifies anything you drink to pH ~2.0 — so the alkalinity of the water is neutralized before it reaches the bloodstream.

Common marketing claims

"Neutralizes acid in the body" · "Prevents cancer" · "Anti-aging antioxidant" · "Cleanses kidneys" · "Balances body pH" · "Improves hydration better than regular water" · "Reverses CKD" · "Reduces creatinine." These claims are circulated through Facebook groups, YouTube, and water delivery marketing.

The business model matters

Many alkaline water products in the Philippines are sold through multi-level marketing (MLM) or franchise refilling stations. Financial incentives to sell — not evidence of efficacy — drive the aggressive marketing. The fact that something is expensive and heavily promoted does not mean it has health benefits. Ask: "Where are the published clinical trials?"

What the Evidence Actually Shows

Alkaline Water — Applying the Science

The human body maintains blood pH between 7.35–7.45 with extraordinary precision through the respiratory and renal buffering systems. A deviation of just 0.1 pH units causes serious symptoms. The pH of what you drink has no measurable effect on blood pH in a healthy person.

Busted
"Alkaline water neutralizes the acid in your body."
The body regulates blood pH through the bicarbonate buffer system, respiratory CO₂ excretion, and renal acid excretion — not through what you drink. Drinking alkaline water does not change blood pH. If it did, you would require hospitalization for metabolic alkalosis. The concept of "acidic body" being dangerous and needing dietary correction is not how human physiology works. No peer-reviewed clinical trial has demonstrated that alkaline water changes blood pH in healthy individuals.
Busted
"Alkaline water cleanses the kidneys and reduces creatinine."
No clinical evidence supports this claim. Creatinine is a waste product of muscle metabolism — its serum level reflects kidney filtration capacity, not urine pH or hydration fluid type. Drinking any type of water — alkaline, regular, or flavored — does not lower serum creatinine. What lowers creatinine is treating the underlying cause of CKD (blood pressure control, glucose control, RAAS blockade, SGLT2 inhibitors). Marketing that claims alkaline water reduces creatinine is medically false and potentially dangerous if patients replace proven treatments with water.
Busted
"Alkaline water prevents or cures cancer."
This claim is based on a misunderstanding of tumor microenvironment research. Cancer cells do create a locally acidic microenvironment — but this is a consequence of tumor metabolism, not a cause of cancer, and it is not influenced by the pH of drinking water. The WHO, American Cancer Society, and Philippine Society of Medical Oncology have not endorsed alkaline water for cancer prevention or treatment. No randomized controlled trial exists supporting this claim.
Partially True — Very Specific Situations
"Alkaline water is better for hydration than regular water."
One small study found slightly improved hydration markers in athletes after intense exercise with alkaline water versus plain water. This is an extremely specific context and the effect size was minimal. For the general population and CKD patients, plain water hydrates identically to alkaline water. The kidney regulates osmolality regardless of water source. The premium price of alkaline water (₱15–40/liter versus ₱1–5 for purified water) is not justified by the evidence.
Alkaline Water and CKD

Alkaline Water in CKD — Specific Concerns

The one scenario where alkaline water has a basis — metabolic acidosis

CKD Stage 3–5 patients frequently develop metabolic acidosis — a real reduction in blood bicarbonate from impaired renal acid excretion. This IS a genuine concern that requires treatment. However, the treatment is sodium bicarbonate tablets (prescribed by your nephrologist) or increased intake of dietary bases (fruits and vegetables) — not alkaline water. Alkaline water at pH 8–9 contains negligible buffering capacity compared to pharmaceutical bicarbonate.

Potential harms from alkaline water in CKD

Some ionized alkaline water machines add potassium to raise pH — directly dangerous in CKD. Some mineral-enriched alkaline waters contain extra calcium or phosphorus — problematic for CKD-MBD management. The greatest harm is not chemical — it is financial and behavioral: spending ₱3,000–₱10,000/month on water instead of medications, and believing the water is treating their CKD while the actual disease progresses untreated.

⚠

The biggest danger of alkaline water in CKD is not the water itself

Patients who believe alkaline water is treating their CKD may stop or reduce their medications, skip nephrology follow-ups, or feel falsely reassured about their progression. We have seen patients reach dialysis who delayed seeking proper treatment because they were "managing" with alkaline water. False hope delays real treatment — and with kidney disease, delay is permanently damaging.

Evidence-Based Guidance

What Should CKD Patients Actually Drink?

Plain purified or filtered water

The best beverage for most CKD patients. Purified or filtered tap water (Baguio, distilled, or well-filtered municipal water) hydrates identically to alkaline water at a fraction of the cost. For CKD Stage 1–3 without fluid restriction — aim for pale yellow urine as a hydration guide. Amount depends on your CKD stage and fluid restriction — ask your nephrologist.

For metabolic acidosis — bicarbonate, not water

If your bicarbonate is low (<22 mEq/L), your nephrologist will prescribe sodium bicarbonate tablets. Increasing vegetable and fruit intake (within potassium limits for your CKD stage) also provides dietary bases. These interventions have actual clinical trial evidence for slowing CKD progression — alkaline water does not.

Low-potassium beverages that are safe

Filtered tap water or purified water · Brewed hot coffee (moderate amounts, avoid with advanced CKD) · Brewed tea (unsweetened, moderate amounts) · Apple juice (lower potassium than orange or buko juice) · Diluted calamansi juice (small amounts) · Lemonade with controlled sugar. Always discuss your fluid and beverage choices with your nephrologist as restrictions vary by CKD stage.

✅

What actually protects kidneys — the evidence-based list

  • Blood pressure control to <130/80 mmHg with ACE inhibitors or ARBs
  • Blood sugar control — HbA1c 7–8% in diabetic CKD (KDIGO 2024)
  • SGLT2 inhibitors (Catania/Rhea dapagliflozin) — proven to slow CKD progression
  • Finerenone (Firialta) for diabetic CKD with proteinuria
  • Low-sodium diet (<2,000 mg/day) — reduces proteinuria and blood pressure
  • Protein restriction (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day) in advanced pre-dialysis CKD
  • Smoking cessation — smoking doubles CKD progression rate
  • Regular exercise — 150 min/week moderate activity (KDIGO 2024)
  • Regular nephrology follow-up — monitoring eGFR, proteinuria, electrolytes
Important: If you are currently drinking alkaline water and taking it in place of or alongside prescribed medications, discuss this openly with your nephrologist. There is no judgment — many patients do this based on information from family and social media. What matters is that your nephrologist knows everything you are consuming so they can give you accurate guidance on what is safe at your specific stage of kidney disease.
