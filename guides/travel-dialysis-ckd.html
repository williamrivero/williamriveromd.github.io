Before You Go

Start Planning at Least 6–8 Weeks Ahead

Travel is possible for most CKD and dialysis patients — but it requires significantly more lead time than for healthy travelers. Dialysis centers abroad must be booked weeks in advance, medications must be secured in sufficient supply, and your nephrologist must clear you to travel based on your current clinical stability.

The most common mistake: booking flights and hotels before confirming dialysis availability. Always confirm the dialysis center first — not all centers accept visiting patients, and available slots are limited.

Who should NOT travel without special precautions

Patients with recent hospitalization (<4 weeks), uncontrolled blood pressure, recent change in dialysis prescription, active infection, severe anemia (Hgb <80 g/L), severe fluid overload, recent cardiac events, or those on peritoneal dialysis with active peritonitis. Always get explicit clearance from your nephrologist.

Documents to prepare before departure

Medical summary letter from nephrologist (in English and destination language if possible), current medication list with generic names and doses, dialysis prescription sheet, recent labs (within 4 weeks), copy of PhilHealth and health insurance cards, emergency contacts including home dialysis center phone number.

For Hemodialysis Patients

Arranging Dialysis at Your Destination

⚠

Book dialysis first — everything else second

Dialysis slots at tourist destinations fill up months in advance. Do not assume you can arrange dialysis upon arrival. Confirm your slot in writing, with the center's name, address, phone number, and the name of the nephrologist who will supervise your sessions.

How to find a dialysis center abroad

Use the Global Dialysis Directory (globaldialysis.com) — free, lists centers in 140+ countries searchable by city. The International Society of Nephrology (ISN) also maintains a patient travel assistance network. Ask your nephrologist's network for referrals — personal connections between nephrologists facilitate smoother bookings.

What to send the receiving center

Your dialysis prescription (machine settings, blood flow rate, dialysate composition, UF goal, session duration), recent labs (Hgb, ferritin, PTH, K+, phosphorus, albumin), hepatitis B and C serology results (required by all centers), HIV status, access type and recent access notes, any special instructions from your nephrologist.

Hepatitis serology is mandatory

All dialysis centers worldwide require current HBsAg, anti-HCV, and anti-HIV results — typically within 3–6 months. Without these results, you will be refused treatment or placed in an isolation room. Get these tests done before your trip and carry the paper results.

Missing dialysis is far more dangerous than missing any other medical treatment. If your dialysis center falls through, go to the nearest hospital emergency department immediately — do not wait to see if you feel okay.

Medications During Travel

Managing Your Medications Away from Home

How much to bring

Bring enough medication for your entire trip plus a 50% buffer for delays, lost luggage, or extended stays. Always carry medications in your hand luggage — never in checked bags. Carry a letter from your nephrologist listing all medications with generic names, doses, and medical necessity — required for customs in many countries for controlled drugs and injectables (ESA, insulin).

Temperature-sensitive medications

Erythropoietin (EPO/ESA) and insulin must be kept between 2–8°C — use an insulated medication travel pouch (Frio wallet or similar). Airlines allow these with a doctor's letter. Notify the cabin crew so they do not freeze your medications. Do not store in overhead bin during very cold cabin conditions.

💡

Phosphate binders — the most commonly forgotten medication

Phosphate binders must be taken with every meal regardless of where you are eating. They cannot be skipped because "the food looks low in phosphorus." Bring more than you think you need — they are bulky but essential. Calcitriol and cinacalcet are also often unavailable in smaller destination pharmacies.

Diet & Fluid

Eating and Drinking Safely While Traveling

Fluid restrictions do not change

Your fluid limit is the same wherever you are. Heat, humidity, and increased physical activity will make you feel thirstier — but exceeding your fluid allowance causes pulmonary edema, not just swelling. Use ice chips instead of full cups of water. Track your intake as strictly as at home.

High-risk foods to avoid everywhere

Restaurant food is almost universally high in sodium — assume all restaurant meals have 2–3× more salt than home-cooked food. Street food and buffets are particularly dangerous for potassium and phosphorus. Dark colas, sports drinks, coconut water, and fresh fruit juices are high in potassium and phosphorus — avoid entirely.

Safer choices when eating out

Grilled or steamed protein with plain rice. Request no sauce, no seasoning, no added salt. Avoid soups and broths — extremely high sodium. Plain bread, plain pasta, egg whites, simple salads with dressing on the side. In Japan, udon and soba in plain broth (no soup) — noodles only. McDonald's plain chicken sandwich (no sauce) is often a safe emergency meal globally.

Air Travel Specifics

Flying with Kidney Disease

Cabin pressure and fluid shifts

Aircraft cabins are pressurized to 6,000–8,000 feet elevation. This causes mild fluid redistribution and increased water loss through breathing. Dialysis patients with tight fluid restrictions may notice more leg swelling during long flights — compression stockings help. Do not try to compensate by drinking more fluid.

At the airport security

Carry a letter from your nephrologist for all injectable medications (EPO, insulin), needles, and dialysis supplies. Fistula patients should inform security that their arm contains a dialysis access and request a manual check — do not allow blood pressure cuffs or tight bands on the fistula arm. Request wheelchair assistance if fatigued.

🚫

Long-haul flights and dialysis timing

Never schedule a long-haul flight (>6 hours) on the day before or after dialysis without discussing with your nephrologist. The ideal schedule is dialysis → 1 rest day → travel. Flying immediately post-dialysis when you are at your driest and most fatigued increases the risk of hypotension and cramping during the flight.

Peritoneal Dialysis Patients

Traveling on PD — Special Considerations

Advantages of PD for travel

PD patients have a significant advantage over HD patients for travel — no need to find and book a dialysis center. CAPD exchanges can be performed anywhere with a clean surface and proper technique. Cycler patients can travel with a portable cycler (Baxter HomeChoice Pro or Fresenius Sleep Safe are airline-approved). PD supplies can often be shipped directly to your hotel by your supplier.

Sterile technique is non-negotiable

Peritonitis risk increases dramatically with lapses in aseptic technique — and foreign hospital treatment of peritonitis is complicated and expensive. Always perform exchanges in a clean, enclosed room away from pets, open windows, and air conditioning vents. Use a mask. Never rush an exchange. Carry extra betadine and supplies for emergencies.

Supply logistics for PD travel

CAPD bags are heavy and bulky — 4 bags/day = approximately 20 kg of supplies per week. Most airlines allow medical supplies as additional baggage free of charge with a doctor's letter. Alternatively, contact Baxter or Fresenius in your destination country — they can often arrange supply delivery directly to your hotel or rental. Arrange this at least 4–6 weeks before travel.

Non-Dialysis CKD Patients

Travel for CKD Stages 1–4

Generally safe with precautions

Non-dialysis CKD patients can travel freely in most cases. The key risks are: dehydration (worsens kidney function acutely), missed medications, dietary lapses, and exposure to nephrotoxic drugs or contrast agents. Carry a medical summary in English listing your CKD stage and eGFR — this is critical if you need medical care abroad.

Hydration during travel

Unlike dialysis patients, CKD Stage 1–3 patients should actually drink slightly more than usual during travel — dehydration is a major cause of acute-on-chronic kidney injury. Target pale yellow urine. In hot climates or with diarrheal illness, contact your nephrologist early — AKI can be prevented with prompt oral rehydration.

Avoid in all CKD patients

NSAIDs (ibuprofen, mefenamic acid — common in travel medicine kits for pain/fever). Contrast dye procedures without pre-hydration protocol. Traditional or herbal remedies sold as local medicines. Dehydration from alcohol or heat without fluid replacement. Salt-loading from excessive restaurant eating without dose adjustment of diuretics.

Emergency Preparedness

What to Do in a Medical Emergency Abroad

🆘

Carry this information at all times — on your phone AND on paper

  • Your name, blood type, and known allergies
  • Your nephrologist's name and phone number (with country code for international calls)
  • Your home dialysis center name and emergency number
  • Your current medication list with doses
  • Your dialysis prescription (blood flow, UF goal, session duration, dialysate composition)
  • Your hepatitis B/C and HIV serology results
  • Your travel insurance policy number and 24-hour emergency helpline
  • Name and address of the nearest hospital to your destination

Travel insurance is mandatory for dialysis patients

Standard travel insurance excludes pre-existing conditions. You need a policy that explicitly covers: emergency dialysis, hospitalization for kidney-related complications, medical repatriation if needed, and prescription medications. Declare your kidney disease fully — failure to disclose voids the policy. Specialist providers for dialysis patients include Global Dialysis Insurance and AXA Medical.

Philippine Embassy / Consulate

Save the contact number of the Philippine Embassy or nearest consulate at your destination. They can assist with emergency repatriation, connecting you with Filipino healthcare workers in the area, and providing consular assistance if hospitalized. Many Filipino nurses and doctors work in hospitals worldwide and can serve as advocates.

Pre-Travel Checklist

Complete This Before You Travel

📋 Medical preparation

  • Nephrology clearance obtained
  • Medical summary letter in English prepared
  • Dialysis center booked and confirmed in writing (HD patients)
  • Hepatitis B, C, HIV serology results obtained (within 6 months)
  • Current labs within 4 weeks (Hgb, K+, phosphorus, albumin)
  • All medications prepared with 50% extra supply
  • Insulated pouch for ESA/insulin
  • Phosphate binders packed — more than enough
  • Medication list in English with generic names and doses
  • Doctor's letter for controlled drugs and injectables

📋 Logistics & Emergency

  • Travel insurance with CKD/dialysis cover confirmed
  • Emergency card prepared (laminated) with all key info
  • Nearest hospital to destination identified
  • Philippine Embassy contact saved
  • Home nephrologist reachable by international call
  • PD supply delivery arranged at destination (PD patients)
  • Compression stockings packed (long-haul flights)
  • Dietary plan discussed for destination cuisine
  • Fluid allowance confirmed and strategy planned
  • Blood pressure monitor packed
Important: This guide is for general education. Every patient's travel clearance depends on individual clinical stability. Always obtain explicit written clearance from your nephrologist before booking any travel.
