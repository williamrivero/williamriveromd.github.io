Advance Care Planning for Dialysis Patients

Understanding your options, making your wishes known, and ensuring your values guide your care — even if you cannot speak for yourself.

Updated: May 2026 KDIGO 2024 • Philippine Context W. G. M. Rivero, MD, FPCP, DPSN

W. G. M. Rivero, MD, FPCP, DPSN

Specialist in Internal Medicine, Nephrology & Clinical Nutrition. This guide is for educational purposes and does not replace a personal consultation.

Section 1

What Is Advance Care Planning?

Advance Care Planning (ACP) is a process of thinking, talking, and writing down your wishes about future medical care — before a crisis happens. It is not a single document. It is a conversation: with yourself, your family, and your doctor.

For people on dialysis, ACP is especially important. Dialysis extends life, but it does not cure kidney failure. Over time, many patients face decisions about whether to continue, adjust, or stop dialysis. Planning ahead ensures that your values — not just your disease — guide those decisions.

“The goal of ACP is not to give up hope. It is to make sure your hope is heard.”
Section 2

Why ACP Matters for Kidney Patients

Kidney failure is one of the few chronic conditions where life-sustaining treatment (dialysis) requires active participation three times a week. This makes the question of 'how much treatment is right for me?' unusually urgent and personal.

High Symptom Burden

Fatigue, pain, pruritus, and poor sleep affect quality of life even with adequate dialysis. ACP helps align treatment intensity with your daily reality.

High Mortality Risk

Annual mortality on dialysis is 15–20%. Patients with diabetes, age >75, or cardiovascular disease face even higher risk. Planning while well is far easier than in crisis.

Sudden Incapacity

Stroke, cardiac arrest, or sudden neurological events can occur without warning. Without a documented plan, life-or-death decisions fall entirely to family members who may be unprepared.

Family Protection

Families who have had ACP conversations report less guilt, less conflict, and less grief after a loved one's death. Your plan is a gift to those who love you.

Section 3

The Goals-of-Care Conversation

A goals-of-care (GOC) conversation explores what matters most to you — not just medically, but as a person. It is not about choosing between living and dying. It is about choosing how to live.

Your doctor may ask questions like these. Think about your answers in advance.

Question Your Doctor May Ask Why It Matters
“What do you understand about your illness?” Helps correct misconceptions and build shared understanding of prognosis.
“What are your biggest worries right now?” Opens space to discuss fear of pain, being a burden, dying alone, loss of dignity.
“What makes your life meaningful?” Anchors care decisions to your actual values — family, faith, independence, contribution.
“What would be unacceptable to you — what is a fate worse than death?” Helps define limits of care: permanent unconsciousness, machine dependence, loss of all function.
“If your heart stopped, would you want us to try to restart it?” Directly addresses CPR preference — a core ACP decision for high-risk patients.
Section 4

Key Decisions and How to Document Them

ACP is not just a conversation — it should be written down so it can be found and honored when needed. These are the four decisions that matter most for dialysis patients.

  1. Surrogate Decision-Maker (Healthcare Proxy)

    Choose someone you trust to make medical decisions for you if you cannot speak for yourself. This person must understand your values deeply — not just love you. In the Philippines, a written designation is advisable; discuss this with your doctor and have it witnessed.

  2. CPR Preference (Do-Not-Resuscitate Order)

    CPR on dialysis patients has a survival-to-discharge rate of only 3–17%, far lower than the public imagines. A DNR order does NOT mean no treatment — it means no chest compressions or electric shocks if the heart stops. All other treatments continue unless separately specified.

  3. ICU / Ventilator / Artificial Nutrition Preferences

    Specify whether you would want: admission to ICU, placement on a breathing machine (ventilator), tube feeding or intravenous nutrition if you cannot eat, and for how long you would want these if there is no sign of improvement. Context matters — your wishes may differ if the cause is treatable (e.g., pneumonia) versus irreversible (e.g., massive stroke).

  4. Dialysis Continuation, Adjustment, or Withdrawal

    This is the most complex and important decision for dialysis patients. It is addressed in detail in Section 6. You may specify under what conditions you would want dialysis stopped, or that you wish to transition to comfort-focused care.

Section 5

Conservative Kidney Management: Dialysis Is Not the Only Choice

Conservative kidney management (CKM) is a comprehensive, person-centered approach to advanced CKD and kidney failure that prioritizes symptom control, quality of life, and comfort — without initiating dialysis. It is a valid and ethical choice, not a giving up.

ⓘ

Who May Benefit Most from CKM?

  • Age >75 with multiple serious comorbidities (advanced heart failure, dementia, active malignancy)
  • Severe frailty with Clinical Frailty Scale score of 7–9 (severely/very severely/terminally frail)
  • Poor functional status: dependent in most activities of daily living, bed- or chair-bound
  • Patient's stated wish to avoid the burden of thrice-weekly dialysis treatments

CKM does not mean abandonment. It means active, compassionate management of fluid overload, pain, breathlessness, itching, nausea, depression, and other symptoms — without machine-dependent treatment. Median survival without dialysis in carefully selected older patients may be comparable to dialysis, with better quality of life.

Section 6

Dialysis Withdrawal: When Enough Is Enough

Dialysis withdrawal — the decision to stop dialysis — is one of the most common ways that patients on dialysis die. In Western data, approximately 20–25% of dialysis deaths follow planned withdrawal. In the Philippines, this conversation remains culturally difficult but ethically clear: stopping a treatment that no longer serves a patient's goals is not equivalent to causing death.

⚠

Common Situations That Prompt This Conversation

  • Severe, uncontrollable symptoms despite optimal dialysis (pain, cachexia, persistent hypotension)
  • New catastrophic event: large stroke, advanced dementia, terminal malignancy
  • Complete loss of meaningful function or activities the patient once valued
  • Patient's autonomous, clearly expressed and repeated wish to stop

When dialysis is stopped, death typically follows within days to about two weeks, due to fluid and potassium accumulation. The focus of care shifts entirely to comfort: symptom control, spiritual care, presence of family, and dying with dignity.

✓

What Comfort Care Looks Like

  • Pain and breathlessness managed with opioids (oral morphine is the standard)
  • Agitation and restlessness treated with midazolam or haloperidol as needed
  • Mouth care and skin care to prevent distress from dryness
  • Family present, pastoral or spiritual support available, no unnecessary procedures
  • Medications for comfort continued; all non-comfort medications discontinued
Section 7

The Philippine Context: Culture, Faith, and Family

In the Philippines, death remains a topic that many families avoid until a crisis forces the conversation. Strong Catholic and family-centered values create unique dynamics around end-of-life decisions. Understanding these dynamics helps — it does not mean accepting paralysis.

The Role of the Family

Filipino culture is deeply communal. Decisions are rarely made alone. ACP works best when the patient, family, and doctor share a conversation — not just sign forms. Encourage family to be present at the goals-of-care discussion.

Faith and the Will of God

Many Filipinos believe that dying is in God's hands. ACP does not contradict this belief — it aligns human choices with God-given values of dignity, love, and compassion. Stopping a machine is not the same as taking a life.

Financial Reality

Dialysis costs PHP 3,000–5,000+ per session out-of-pocket even with PhilHealth. For families already stretched thin, ACP conversations may include honest discussion about financial sustainability and what level of care is realistic.

Legal Landscape in the Philippines

The Philippines does not yet have a formal advance directive law akin to the US POLST system. However, written ACP documents are ethically and clinically honored by most hospitals. Discuss the format with your doctor. The Philippine College of Physicians supports ACP as standard practice.

Section 8

A Guide for Families and Caregivers

If you are reading this as a family member or caregiver of a dialysis patient, this section is for you. Caring for someone with kidney failure is a profound act of love. It also places enormous weight on you.

  • Ask your loved one while they are still well: “What do you want if you get too sick to speak for yourself?” Write down what they say.
  • Attend a goals-of-care conversation with the nephrologist. You can be present without taking control.
  • If your loved one is incapacitated: ask “What would they want?” not “What do I want for them?” — this is a critical distinction.
  • Understand that agreeing to comfort care or dialysis withdrawal is not giving up — it is honoring the patient's dignity.
  • Ask the medical team: “What would a natural death look like for my family member?” and “What can we do to make them comfortable?”
  • Take care of yourself. Caregiver burnout is real. Accepting help is not weakness — it is wisdom.
ⓘ

When to Contact the Nephrology Team

  • Your loved one has expressed a wish to stop dialysis or says life is no longer worth living
  • There is conflict within the family about the goals of treatment
  • A new serious illness has changed the situation (e.g., stroke, cancer, severe dementia)
  • You feel overwhelmed and need support in making decisions
