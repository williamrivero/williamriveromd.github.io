Hope After Allograft Failure

Understanding why transplanted kidneys decline, what comes next, and how to pursue a second chance — with a clear plan and renewed purpose.

The Basics

Your Transplant & How It Works

A kidney transplant places a donor kidney — the allograft — into your lower abdomen, where it connects to your blood vessels and bladder. Because it comes from another person, your immune system recognizes it as foreign. Immunosuppressive medications must be taken for the entire life of the transplant to prevent your body from attacking it. Your native kidneys are usually left in place.

What "Allograft" Means

Any organ transplanted from one human to another. In nephrology, allograft specifically means your transplanted kidney — distinct from your native (original) kidneys.

Immunosuppression

Usually tacrolimus + mycophenolate + prednisone — taken every day, as prescribed, for as long as the kidney functions. Missing doses is the most common preventable cause of rejection.

Graft Survival

Living-donor kidneys last ~20 years on average; deceased-donor ~14–15 years. Many function for 25–30+ years with excellent adherence and monitoring.

Your Personal Baseline Is the Reference Point

A creatinine of 1.6 mg/dL is normal for you if it has been stable for years. What matters is change from your personal baseline — even a small, gradual rise over months signals a problem that needs evaluation before it becomes irreversible.

Early Warning Signs

What Is Allograft Dysfunction?

Allograft dysfunction means the transplanted kidney is no longer working as well as it should. It exists on a spectrum — from mild and reversible to irreversible failure. The earlier it is caught, the more options you have.

Rising Creatinine

The earliest lab signal. A 20–25% rise above your personal baseline warrants same-week evaluation — do not wait for your next scheduled appointment.

Increased Proteinuria

New or worsening protein in the urine signals damage to the allograft's glomerular filtration barrier. Rising UACR requires urgent workup.

Worsening Hypertension

New or difficult-to-control high BP may reflect renal artery stenosis, calcineurin inhibitor toxicity, or chronic rejection — all require evaluation.

🚨

Urgent Symptoms — Contact Your Transplant Team or ER Immediately

  • Significant decrease or sudden absence of urine output
  • Fever ≥38°C / 100.4°F — may signal rejection OR serious infection
  • Pain or tenderness over the transplant site (lower abdomen / iliac fossa)
  • New or sudden swelling of the legs, face, or abdomen
  • Severe fatigue, confusion, metallic taste, nausea, or generalized itching — signs of uremia
The Timeline of Failure

Why Allografts Fail — A Timeline

Different problems arise at different time points after transplant. Understanding this timeline helps your doctor identify the most likely cause quickly and choose the right treatment.

Days 0–7 · Delayed Graft Function (DGF)
The kidney recovers slowly from preservation and ischemia injury. Usually temporary — the kidney "wakes up" over days to weeks. May require temporary dialysis. Does not predict long-term failure if managed appropriately.
Days 7–90 · Acute T-Cell (Cellular) Rejection
The immune system mounts a cellular attack via T-lymphocytes invading the kidney. Treated aggressively with high-dose steroids ± anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG). Highly reversible when caught early — the key reason why frequent early monitoring is essential.
Months 1–12 · BK Polyomavirus Nephropathy
BK virus reactivates when immunosuppression is too potent, causing interstitial nephritis and tubular damage. Treatment requires a deliberate, careful reduction of immunosuppression. A delicate balance — reduce too little and rejection risks increase; reduce too much and BK progresses.
Months 3–24 · Acute Antibody-Mediated Rejection (AMR)
Donor-specific antibodies (DSA) form and attack the allograft's vascular endothelium. More difficult to treat than cellular rejection — requires IVIG, plasmapheresis, and/or rituximab. Risk is higher in sensitized and re-transplant patients.
Years 2–10+ · Chronic Allograft Nephropathy (CAN)
The most common cause of late graft loss. A slow, multifactorial process combining chronic immune injury, calcineurin inhibitor toxicity, hypertension, proteinuria, and progressive fibrosis. Often silent until eGFR is already significantly reduced. Progression can be slowed but not reversed.
Anytime · Recurrent Native Kidney Disease
Some kidney diseases recur in the transplant — particularly FSGS, IgA nephropathy, MPGN/C3GN, and primary hyperoxaluria. Recurrence risk and timing vary by disease type and must be explicitly discussed when evaluating for re-transplant candidacy.

Rejection is classified by the underlying mechanism — which determines both the treatment and the prognosis. The gold standard for diagnosis is a percutaneous allograft biopsy classified by the Banff criteria.

🔬

The Allograft Biopsy

When rejection is suspected, a percutaneous allograft biopsy is performed under ultrasound guidance — a thin needle takes a small tissue sample of the transplanted kidney. The Banff classification system categorizes the findings into specific rejection patterns, which directly guides targeted treatment selection. It is a safe, outpatient procedure performed at your transplant center.

When the Allograft Fails

What Happens If the Kidney Fails: Your Options

If the allograft reaches end-stage, you face a treatment transition — but with important advantages. You have prior transplant experience, an established team, and you may be eligible for re-transplant sooner than expected.

Hemodialysis (HD)

3× weekly in-center or home HD. The failed transplant kidney is usually left in place. Can sometimes be initiated through a functioning AV fistula from your original transplant workup.

Peritoneal Dialysis (PD)

Daily home-based option. Can begin at eGFR 8–10 mL/min. Requires PD catheter placement — plan ahead before complete allograft failure.

Re-Transplantation

The optimal therapy for eligible patients. Second transplant outcomes are comparable to first transplants. Early re-listing at eGFR 15–20 maximizes the chance of a pre-emptive transplant.

Conservative Care

Supportive management without dialysis — a valid, dignified choice for those who prioritize comfort-focused care, coordinated with palliative medicine.

⏰

Timing Your Transition — Do Not Wait for Emergency

The ideal window to plan dialysis access and begin re-transplant workup is when eGFR falls to 15–20 mL/min. Waiting until complete failure forces emergency dialysis initiation — higher complication rates, worse outcomes. At NKTI, Philippines, re-listing can begin at eGFR approaching 20 mL/min.

What Happens to the Failed Transplant Kidney?

In most cases, the failed allograft is left in place. The small, scarred kidney rarely causes problems. However, transplant nephrectomy (surgical removal) may be recommended if:

1

Recurrent infections or persistent fever

The failed kidney can harbor chronic infection that does not resolve while in place, even with antibiotics.

2

Chronic graft site pain or hematuria

Persistent bleeding or pain from the failed graft site may require surgical removal for adequate symptom control.

3

Selected cases of high sensitization for re-transplant

In some patients, removal may reduce ongoing antigenic stimulus and lower DSA levels, potentially improving re-transplant candidacy. Discussed case-by-case with your transplant team.

💊

Immunosuppression Withdrawal — Follow the Taper Plan

When the allograft definitively fails and dialysis resumes, immunosuppressants are tapered gradually over 3–6 months under physician guidance. Abrupt cessation can trigger rejection nephritis in the failed graft — causing fever, graft pain, and hematuria. Always follow your transplant team's specific tapering protocol.

Second Chance

Re-Transplantation: Is It Possible?

For most patients who experience allograft failure, re-transplantation is a realistic and worthwhile goal. Approximately 15–20% of kidney transplants performed each year worldwide are re-transplants — and many patients have received 3 or even 4 successful kidneys over a lifetime of care.

Pre-Emptive Re-Transplant: The Gold Standard Outcome

A pre-emptive re-transplant — performed before you need to return to dialysis — is the best possible outcome of allograft failure. It avoids all risks of dialysis and produces the best long-term graft and patient survival of any transition pathway. It requires early re-listing: ideally when eGFR is still 15–20 mL/min. At NKTI, Philippines, the re-listing process can be initiated at this threshold.

Immunology · Antibodies

Sensitization & the PRA/cPRA Score

Your first transplant exposed your immune system to the donor's HLA (human leukocyte antigen) proteins. After graft failure, your immune system may have formed Donor-Specific Antibodies (DSA) — antibodies that recognize HLA antigens that differ from your own. The more HLA mismatches you've been exposed to, the more sensitized you become.

Desensitization Protocols

IVIG

High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin modulates antibody production and complement activation. Given over multiple monthly sessions.

Plasmapheresis

Physically removes circulating DSA from the blood, rapidly lowering antibody titers. Most effective in combination with IVIG.

Rituximab & Beyond

Anti-CD20 agent depletes B-cells (antibody-producing cells). Newer agents — belatacept, eculizumab, imlifidase — used in specialized protocols at experienced centers.

💻

Virtual Crossmatch

Modern transplant programs compare your antibody profile against a potential donor's HLA typing computationally — identifying compatible donors within hours, even for highly sensitized recipients. This has significantly expanded transplant access for previously "untransplantable" patients.

The Best Option

Living Donor Re-Transplant

A living donor transplant — from a compatible family member, spouse, or close friend — is the single best option for most patients facing allograft failure. It eliminates the waiting list entirely and enables a pre-emptive transplant before dialysis begins.

Best Long-Term Outcomes

Living-donor kidneys have an average functional lifespan of 20+ years. Recipients of living-donor re-transplants have better graft and patient survival than those who wait years on dialysis for a deceased-donor kidney.

No Waiting List — Scheduled Electively

A living donor transplant is planned as elective surgery — with full preparation, optimized recipient conditioning, and ideal timing. This eliminates emergency scenarios and allows pre-emptive transplant before dialysis.

HLA Matching Advantage

Related donors often share more HLA antigens, reducing the antibody formation that leads to sensitization and rejection. Sibling or parent donors frequently provide excellent HLA matches.

Paired Kidney Exchange

If your donor is blood-type or HLA incompatible with you, a kidney exchange program matches them with another recipient — and you receive a compatible kidney in return. NKTI operates a paired exchange program.

🤝

Who Can Be a Living Donor?

  • Age 18–65 (evaluated case-by-case up to 70)
  • Normal kidney function with two healthy kidneys
  • No significant diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, kidney disease, or active malignancy
  • Blood-type compatible — or compatible via desensitization / paired exchange
  • Fully informed and voluntarily consenting — donors are never coerced
  • Under Republic Act 9208 and NKTI protocols, living donor workup is performed at no cost to the donor in the Philippines
Emotional Health

Hope & Emotional Wellbeing After Allograft Failure

Losing a transplanted kidney is one of the most emotionally difficult experiences a kidney patient can face. The grief is real — you have lost an organ that gave you back your freedom, energy, and daily life. This grief is valid, and it deserves the same attention as any physical symptom.

What the Evidence Tells Us About Hope After Allograft Failure

Patients who pursue re-transplantation after graft failure have significantly better long-term survival than those who remain on dialysis. The human body — and spirit — shows remarkable capacity for resilience. Here are facts to hold onto:

  • Most eligible patients who are re-listed do receive a second kidney transplant
  • Second transplant outcomes are comparable to first transplants in the majority of published studies
  • Many patients around the world have lived decades with 2, 3, or even 4 kidney transplants
  • A living-donor re-transplant can occur within months of failure — not years — especially with a willing donor already identified
  • Advances in desensitization have opened re-transplant pathways for highly sensitized patients who were previously considered untransplantable

Common Emotional Responses — All Are Normal

Grief & Loss

Mourning the kidney — especially a living donor's gift — is a natural and legitimate response that should be acknowledged, not suppressed.

Anger

"Why me again?" — anger at the disease or perceived unfairness is a healthy part of processing significant loss. It is valid and expected.

Fear & Uncertainty

Fear of dialysis, of not finding a new donor, or of "what comes next" — all very common, and worth discussing openly with your medical team.

Renewed Determination

Many patients channel their experience into living donation advocacy, peer support for other recipients, or renewed commitment to their own health goals.

🫂

Practical Steps for Emotional Support

  • Request a referral to the transplant social worker or psychologist — standard of care, not a sign of weakness
  • Connect with peer support groups for transplant recipients — NKTI maintains active patient support networks
  • Keep close family members informed and involved in your re-transplant planning — family support strongly improves outcomes
  • Consider disclosing your situation to a trusted employer or school — legal protections exist under the PWD Act and CSC policies in the Philippines
  • Spiritual care — chaplaincy, faith community, or personal practice — is a validated component of resilience in chronic illness
Your Next Appointment

Questions to Ask Your Transplant Nephrologist

Being prepared with specific questions makes the most of your consultation time — and ensures nothing important is left unaddressed.

My Questions for My Doctor

  • What is my current eGFR trend — how fast is my allograft declining per year?
  • Has a biopsy been done? What is the Banff classification, and does it change treatment?
  • Are there still-reversible causes we have not fully addressed — drug levels, blood pressure, occult infection?
  • When should I be re-listed for kidney transplant? Can it be pre-emptive?
  • What is my current cPRA / sensitization level, and how does it affect estimated waiting time?
  • Should I be evaluated for a desensitization protocol? Is this available at our center?
  • Is there a family member or friend who should be evaluated as a living donor now?
  • What dialysis access should I prepare — AV fistula or peritoneal dialysis catheter?
  • When should we begin tapering immunosuppression, and what is the exact taper plan?
  • Should the failed allograft be surgically removed, or is it safe to leave in place?
  • Can I meet with the transplant social worker or psychologist?
  • Realistically — given my cPRA, cause of failure, and overall health — what is the expected timeline for a second transplant?
