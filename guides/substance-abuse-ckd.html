Section 1

Clinical Overview

Substance use is a substantially under-recognized contributor to both acute kidney injury (AKI) and AKI-on-CKD across nephrology and internal-medicine practice. Stimulants (methamphetamine, cocaine), opioids, chronic analgesic/NSAID use, inhalants, and injection drug use each generate distinct renal syndromes, yet exposure is frequently absent from the chart because patients fear judgment, legal consequences, or loss of transplant candidacy. In the Philippine context — where amphetamine-type stimulant ("shabu") use is prevalent and a punitive policy environment heightens fear of disclosure — the gap between true exposure and documented exposure is especially wide. The practical implication is unambiguous: a substance history obtained only when the clinician "suspects" it will systematically miss cases. Screening must be standardized, routine, and non-judgmental for every patient, not triggered by appearance or assumption.

Why it's missed

  • Stigma & fear — anticipated judgment, criminalization (acute in the Philippines), and worry about being denied transplant or dialysis suppress disclosure.
  • Selective screening — clinicians ask only "high-risk-looking" patients, embedding bias and missing professionals, elderly analgesic users, and AAS-using athletes.
  • Atypical presentations — AKI attributed to "dehydration," "sepsis," or "contrast" without probing the precipitant.
  • Toxicology blind spots — many agents (synthetic cannabinoids, novel stimulants, designer opioids) are not detected on standard immunoassay panels.
  • Time pressure — substance history is the first thing dropped in a rushed clerking.

Mechanistic themes

  • Vasoconstriction & ischemia — sympathomimetic surge, renal artery vasospasm, malignant hypertension, thrombotic microangiopathy.
  • Direct tubular toxicity — toxins, adulterants, and metabolites injuring tubular epithelium (ATN).
  • Rhabdomyolysis — agitation, hyperthermia, seizures, immobilization, and direct myotoxicity → pigment nephropathy.
  • Volume depletion — anorexia, vomiting, diaphoresis, hyperthermia compounding pre-renal physiology.
  • Infection-related GN — HIV, HBV, HCV, endocarditis, and AA amyloidosis in people who inject drugs (PWID).
  • Adulterants — levamisole (ANCA vasculitis/TMA), talc/fillers, melamine, and unknown cutting agents.
🩺 Clinical Pearl

Frame substance screening as a universal vital-sign question, scripted identically for the executive with hypertensive AKI and the patient who is visibly unwell: "I ask everyone this — in the last year have you used any recreational or non-prescribed drugs?" Universal, normalized inquiry both reduces stigma and removes the diagnostic anchoring that causes stimulant nephropathy to be misfiled as "essential malignant hypertension."

Section 2

Screening & Brief Intervention (SBIRT)

SBIRT — Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment — is the evidence-based, public-health framework for integrating substance use detection into routine care. It is feasible in a busy nephrology clinic or dialysis unit: a 1–2 item universal screen for all patients, a validated full instrument for positive screens, a 5–10 minute brief motivational intervention, and a warm hand-off to addiction services for those with probable disorder.

Validated instruments

  • Single-item screens — NIDA single-question ("How many times in the past year have you used an illegal drug or prescription medication for non-medical reasons?"); a single drink-quantity question for alcohol. High sensitivity, ideal universal first step.
  • AUDIT-C — 3-item alcohol screen; rapid, well-validated across CKD populations.
  • DAST-10 — 10-item Drug Abuse Screening Test for non-alcohol drug use severity.
  • CAGE-AID — CAGE adapted to include drugs; brief, memorable, lower sensitivity for early/moderate use.
  • NIDA Quick Screen / NM-ASSIST — tiered tool covering tobacco, alcohol, prescription and illicit drugs with substance-specific involvement scoring.

Language, documentation & confidentiality

Use person-first, non-stigmatizing language: "person who uses drugs," "use," and "substance use disorder" — never "abuser," "addict," "clean/dirty urine," or "drug habit." Stigmatizing terminology measurably reduces disclosure and degrades the therapeutic alliance. Document objectively (substance, route, frequency, last use) and explain the limits of confidentiality up front, particularly given the Philippine legal environment; reassure patients that clinical information is used to protect their kidneys, not to report them.

🩺 Clinical Pearl

The brief intervention is motivational, not confrontational. Use OARS micro-skills — Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening, Summaries — and roll with resistance rather than arguing. A single empathic statement linking the substance to the kidney finding ("Your kidney numbers and this drug are connected, and stopping can let them recover") leverages the "teachable moment" of an acute renal event, when motivation to change is highest.

Stimulant nephrotoxicity is the dominant pattern in Filipino practice. Methamphetamine and cocaine converge on a final common pathway of catecholamine excess: intense renal vasoconstriction and malignant-range hypertension drive ischemic ATN, thrombotic microangiopathy, and accelerated nephrosclerosis, while hyperthermia, agitation, and seizures precipitate rhabdomyolysis. Chronic stimulant exposure produces FSGS and hypertensive CKD that mimic — and are routinely misdiagnosed as — primary essential hypertension.

The "historical" HAN literature warrants caution: much of the original collapsing-FSGS signal reflected adulterants and undiagnosed HIV rather than heroin per se, and incidence fell sharply with cleaner supply and ART. Today, in a PWID with collapsing FSGS, exclude HIVAN first.

Adulterants are a frequent hidden cause. Levamisole-contaminated cocaine produces ANCA-associated vasculitis, retiform purpura, and pauci-immune GN; talc and fillers cause granulomatous disease; unknown cutting agents account for many "idiopathic" toxin-associated ATNs, especially with synthetic cannabinoids.

⚠️ Pitfall

Do not equate a negative urine drug screen with absence of substance-related injury. Synthetic cannabinoids, designer stimulants, toluene, and many novel opioids are invisible to standard immunoassay panels, and adulterant-driven syndromes (levamisole vasculitis) occur with the parent drug already metabolized. A clinically compatible syndrome with negative routine tox should prompt expanded testing and a careful exposure history — not exclusion of the diagnosis.

Section 4

AKI Workup in Suspected Substance Use

When AKI is suspected to be substance-related, the workup must simultaneously characterize the renal lesion and screen for the specific complications each agent produces — pigment nephropathy, toxic-alcohol ingestion, electrolyte/acid–base derangement, and bloodborne infection. The urinalysis with manual microscopy is the highest-yield single test and should never be skipped.

Essential labs

  • UA with microscopy/sediment — muddy-brown granular casts (ATN), heme-positive dipstick without RBCs (myoglobinuria), WBCs/eosinophils & WBC casts (AIN), dysmorphic RBCs/RBC casts (GN).
  • Creatine kinase (CK) — screen all stimulant/opioid-overdose/agitated patients for rhabdomyolysis.
  • CMP — creatinine trend, K, Ca, PO4, HCO3, transaminases.
  • Urine electrolytes & FeNa (or FeUrea if on diuretics) — pre-renal vs intrinsic distinction.
  • Urine toxicology — confirm exposure, but interpret with full knowledge of panel limits.
  • CBC — anemia, thrombocytopenia (TMA, hypersplenism), leukocytosis (sepsis, levamisole agranulocytosis).

When to expand workup

  • Anion & osmolar gaps — suspected toxic alcohols (ethylene glycol, methanol); elevated osmolar gap with high anion-gap acidosis is the screening combination.
  • Urine for calcium oxalate / fluorescence — ethylene glycol.
  • HIV, HBsAg, anti-HCV — all PWID; complement, cryoglobulins, ANCA, ANA where GN suspected.
  • Levamisole-cocaine work-up — ANCA (often dual MPO/PR3), skin biopsy for retiform purpura.
  • Blood cultures & echocardiography — PWID with fever/AKI (endocarditis-associated GN).
  • Renal biopsy — unexplained GN, nephrotic-range proteinuria, or suspected drug-induced AIN/FSGS not resolving.

Diagnostic sequence

  1. Stabilize, assess volume status, and resuscitate as needed before extensive testing.
  2. Send UA with manual microscopy, CK, CMP, CBC, and paired urine/serum electrolytes for FeNa.
  3. If heme-positive dipstick without RBCs or CK markedly elevated → treat as rhabdomyolysis pathway.
  4. If high anion-gap acidosis → calculate osmolar gap; consider toxic alcohols and obtain levels/empiric therapy.
  5. Send urine toxicology and a careful exposure history; expand to drug-specific tests for novel/undetected agents.
  6. Screen all PWID for HIV/HBV/HCV; add immunologic serologies, cultures, and echo as the picture dictates.
  7. Biopsy when the lesion is unexplained, nephrotic, or fails expected recovery.
⚠️ Pitfall

Urine immunoassay toxicology is a screening test, not a gold standard. False negatives are common (synthetic agents, low-cross-reactivity opioids such as fentanyl/tramadol/methadone on a generic "opiate" assay, rapid clearance), and false positives occur (certain antibiotics, decongestants). Never override a clinically compatible syndrome on the basis of a negative immunoassay, and confirm management-altering results with GC-MS/LC-MS where available.

Section 5

Rhabdomyolysis Management

Rhabdomyolysis is the unifying complication across stimulants, opioid overdose, inhalants, and synthetic cannabinoids, and is a leading cause of substance-related AKI. Pigment nephropathy results from a triad of renal vasoconstriction, direct heme-protein tubular toxicity, and intratubular cast obstruction — all aggravated by volume depletion. Early, aggressive volume repletion is the single most effective intervention.

Management bundle

  1. Recognize / risk-stratify by CK — diagnosis at CK >5× ULN (~1,000 U/L); AKI risk rises markedly above ~5,000 U/L and is high >15,000–20,000 U/L. Trend CK serially.
  2. Aggressive isotonic fluid resuscitation — early IV isotonic crystalloid (e.g., 0.9% saline or balanced solution), often starting ~1–1.5 L/h then titrated; lactated Ringer's is acceptable and avoids hyperchloremic acidosis. Begin before AKI is established.
  3. Target urine output — aim ~200–300 mL/h (≈3 mL/kg/h) until CK is clearly falling and pigment cleared; use UOP, not a fixed fluid volume, to guide titration.
  4. Monitor electrolytes closely — hyperkalemia (life-threatening, from muscle release plus AKI), hyperphosphatemia, and early hypocalcemia; avoid calcium repletion unless symptomatic/severe (late rebound hypercalcemia is common).
  5. Bicarbonate — limited/controversial — urinary alkalinization (target urine pH >6.5) is not proven superior to aggressive saline alone; reserve for selected cases, monitor for worsening hypocalcemia and metabolic alkalosis, and do not delay or substitute for volume.
  6. Avoid nephrotoxins & nephrotoxic adjuncts — hold NSAIDs and other nephrotoxins; mannitol is not routinely recommended.
  7. Escalate to RRT for standard indications (below) — RRT does not efficiently clear myoglobin and is used to manage AKI complications, not the rhabdomyolysis itself.

Indications for renal replacement therapy are the conventional AKI triggers: refractory hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis unresponsive to medical therapy, oligoanuric AKI, and diuretic-resistant volume overload, plus uremic complications.

🩺 Clinical Pearl

Resuscitate to urine output, not to a number on the fluid order. In a young stimulant user with crush/exertional rhabdomyolysis and preserved cardiac function, under-resuscitation — not over-resuscitation — is the usual error; aggressive early isotonic fluid titrated to ~3 mL/kg/h urine output prevents the cast nephropathy that drives the AKI. Reassess volume status frequently once UOP is established, and watch for the predictable late rebound hypercalcemia during the recovery phase.

Section 6

Stimulant Hypertensive & Vascular Emergencies

Cocaine and methamphetamine produce hypertensive emergencies, coronary vasospasm/chest pain, accelerated-malignant hypertension with renal involvement, and thrombotic microangiopathy. Because the underlying driver is catecholamine excess with α- and β-adrenergic stimulation, the cornerstone of management is benzodiazepine-first therapy, which reduces central sympathetic outflow and treats agitation, tachycardia, and hypertension simultaneously. The central pharmacologic hazard is unopposed α-stimulation from non-selective β-blockade.

✅ Do

  • Benzodiazepines first-line — titrated IV (e.g., diazepam/lorazepam) for agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, and chest pain.
  • Phentolamine — α-blocker; directly counters α-mediated vasoconstriction and coronary vasospasm.
  • Nitrates / nitroprusside — for ongoing chest pain and afterload reduction.
  • Calcium-channel blockers — reasonable adjuncts for blood pressure control.
  • Active cooling for hyperthermia; aggressive supportive care.
  • Assess end-organ injury — renal function, sediment, troponin, neuro-imaging; evaluate for TMA (smear, LDH, platelets, haptoglobin).

⛔ Avoid

  • Unopposed non-selective β-blockade (e.g., propranolol) in cocaine toxicity — leaves α-mediated vasoconstriction unchecked → worsening hypertension and coronary vasospasm.
  • β-blocker monotherapy before adequate benzodiazepine/α-coverage.
  • Reflexive thrombolysis for stimulant chest pain without confirmed STEMI — vasospasm and aortic dissection must be excluded.
  • Over-rapid BP lowering that risks renal/cerebral hypoperfusion in chronic accelerated HTN.
  • Nephrotoxic contrast loads without weighing AKI risk in the actively injured kidney.

Accelerated-malignant hypertension from chronic stimulant use frequently presents with AKI, hematuria/proteinuria, and a thrombotic microangiopathy picture (schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, LDH elevation) that overlaps with primary TMA. Distinguishing stimulant-driven hypertensive emergency from a primary thrombotic microangiopathy (e.g., aHUS/TTP) is essential, as it changes therapy; controlling blood pressure and removing the stimulant often resolves the hematologic picture in the former.

🩺 Clinical Pearl

Reach for lorazepam or diazepam before any antihypertensive in cocaine/methamphetamine toxicity. The "avoid unopposed beta-blockade" teaching is most clinically relevant for non-selective agents; even where mixed α/β agents (labetalol) are debated, benzodiazepine-first plus a pure α-blocker (phentolamine) and nitrates is the safest, mechanism-aligned sequence — and it simultaneously treats the agitation, tachycardia, and hyperthermia that drive concurrent rhabdomyolysis.

Section 7

Infection-Related Nephropathies in PWID

People who inject drugs carry a high burden of bloodborne infection, each linked to a characteristic glomerular lesion. Recognizing these patterns directs both renal and antimicrobial/antiviral therapy, and underscores the central role of screening, vaccination, and harm reduction in nephroprotection.

Screening is mandatory: test every PWID for HIV, HBsAg, and anti-HCV, and vaccinate non-immune patients against hepatitis B (and ensure routine immunizations). Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) deserves specific vigilance in CKD — it causes proximal tubular toxicity and Fanconi-type injury; favor tenofovir alafenamide or alternative agents and dose-adjust all antiretrovirals and antivirals to GFR.

🩺 Clinical Pearl

Harm reduction is nephroprotection. Counseling on sterile injection equipment, never sharing supplies, and access to clean needles directly reduces the incidence of HIVAN, cryoglobulinemic MPGN, endocarditis-associated GN, and AA amyloidosis. Even where formal needle-exchange programs are constrained — as in much of the Philippines — clinicians can deliver individual harm-reduction counseling, vaccinate, link patients to HIV/HCV treatment, and treat soft-tissue infections early to interrupt the chronic-inflammation pathway to amyloidosis.

Section 8

Dialysis Patients & Drug Dosing

Patients with ESKD on maintenance hemodialysis represent a high-risk intersection of substance use, polypharmacy, and altered pharmacokinetics. Loss of renal clearance, hypoalbuminemia, altered volume of distribution, and intermittent dialytic removal complicate dosing of both substances of abuse and the medications used to treat substance use disorders (SUD). The clinician must reason separately about renal clearance (lost in ESKD) and dialyzability (whether the molecule is removed across the membrane), as these are independent properties.

Dialyzability & ESKD dosing of common agents

Dialyzability is governed chiefly by molecular weight, protein binding, and volume of distribution. Highly protein-bound, lipophilic, large-Vd molecules are poorly removed by conventional dialysis regardless of membrane flux. The table below summarizes practical behavior of agents commonly encountered in the dialysis unit.

Medication-assisted treatment (MAT) in ESKD

Buprenorphine — preferred in ESKD

  • Predominantly hepatic metabolism (CYP3A4) with minimal renal contribution; no renal dose adjustment required.
  • Ceiling effect on respiratory depression confers a wider safety margin than full agonists in a frail, polypharmacy population.
  • Buprenorphine/naloxone combination acceptable — the naloxone component is poorly bioavailable sublingually and not a renal concern.
  • Minimal QTc effect compared with methadone; favored when ECG monitoring access is limited.
  • Initiate when in mild–moderate withdrawal (avoid precipitated withdrawal); coordinate with the dialysis schedule for observed dosing if needed.

Methadone & naltrexone considerations

  • Methadone: effective but carries dose-dependent QTc prolongation — high relevance in HD patients with electrolyte shifts (hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia). Baseline and periodic ECG; review co-prescribed QT-prolonging agents (ondansetron, azithromycin, antipsychotics).
  • Not significantly dialyzed → no post-HD supplement; watch interdialytic accumulation and sedation.
  • Naltrexone: opioid-free pathway useful for alcohol or opioid relapse prevention, but the active metabolite accumulates in ESKD; requires opioid abstinence before induction and precludes opioid analgesia if surgery/trauma occurs.
  • Extended-release injectable naltrexone data in ESKD are limited; individualize.

Access protection, adherence, and intradialytic safety

  • Vascular access & infection in PWID: people who inject drugs (PWID) are at elevated risk of access-site infection, bacteremia, endocarditis, and graft/catheter loss. Prefer arteriovenous fistula over catheter where feasible; counsel never to self-cannulate or inject into the access; maintain a low threshold for blood cultures, echocardiography, and infectious-disease involvement with any fever or new murmur.
  • Adherence & missed sessions: active use predicts missed dialysis, interdialytic weight gain, hyperkalemia, and emergent presentations. Use shortened-but-completed sessions over walkouts, flexible scheduling, and integration of MAT dosing into the dialysis visit to leverage the thrice-weekly contact as a care anchor.
  • Intradialytic agitation / intoxication: prioritize staff and patient safety. Assess for hypoxia, hypoglycemia, hypotension, and uremia before attributing behavior to intoxication. De-escalate verbally; if pharmacologic control is unavoidable, use the lowest effective dose with continuous monitoring, recognizing benzodiazepine accumulation risk. Do not abruptly terminate dialysis in a volume-overloaded patient — manage to safe completion when possible.
🩺 Clinical Pearl

In the HD population, "renally dosed" and "removed by dialysis" are different questions. Buprenorphine needs neither renal adjustment nor a post-HD supplement (hepatic, protein-bound), whereas gabapentin needs both a renal reduction and a supplemental post-HD dose. Anchoring MAT to the thrice-weekly dialysis encounter — observed buprenorphine, embedded counseling, scheduled labs — converts a missed-session liability into the most reliable touchpoint in the patient's week.

Section 9

Comorbid Pain & Mental Health

Untreated pain and psychiatric illness are among the strongest drivers of substance use and relapse in CKD. Effective opioid-sparing analgesia and concurrent treatment of mood and anxiety disorders are therefore core components of SUD management — not adjuncts. The challenge in CKD is that many first-line agents (NSAIDs) are nephrotoxic and many alternatives (gabapentinoids, opioid metabolites) accumulate.

Analgesic ladder in CKD

  • Step 1 — Acetaminophen (paracetamol): first-line systemic analgesic in CKD/ESKD; no meaningful renal dose adjustment for standard doses. Respect hepatic limits.
  • Topical agents: topical NSAIDs (low systemic absorption), topical lidocaine, and capsaicin for localized musculoskeletal/neuropathic pain — favored to spare systemic exposure.
  • Gabapentinoids: effective for neuropathic pain but renally cleared — require dose reduction and post-HD supplementation; start low, titrate slowly, monitor sedation/falls.
  • Avoid NSAIDs systemically in CKD: risk of AKI, accelerated CKD progression, hyperkalemia, hypertension, and fluid retention. This includes mefenamic acid, a widely used OTC NSAID in the Philippines.
  • Opioids when needed: prefer agents without active renally-cleared metabolites — buprenorphine (also dual-purpose for OUD) and judicious fentanyl/hydromorphone; avoid morphine, codeine, tramadol in advanced CKD.
  • Use multimodal, non-pharmacologic strategies (physiotherapy, CBT, exercise, sleep optimization) throughout.

Mental health comorbidity

  • Screen routinely for depression and anxiety (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7); both are highly prevalent in CKD/ESKD and drive self-medication with alcohol, stimulants, and sedatives.
  • SSRIs are generally first-line and reasonably tolerated in CKD; sertraline and escitalopram are commonly preferred. Consider QTc with citalopram/escitalopram at higher doses, especially alongside methadone.
  • Be cautious with sedating agents and benzodiazepines (accumulation, falls, dependence); prefer non-benzodiazepine strategies for anxiety where possible.
  • Treat insomnia behaviorally first; many hypnotics accumulate or worsen daytime sedation in ESKD.
  • Integrated care: co-locate or tightly coordinate nephrology, addiction, and mental-health services. Treating the underlying psychiatric driver improves both adherence and SUD outcomes.

For deeper protocols, cross-reference the site's Pain Management in CKD guide (analgesic selection, renal dosing tables) and the CKD, Mental Health & Sleep guide (screening tools, antidepressant selection, behavioral insomnia management). These complement the SUD-specific guidance here.

⚠️ Clinical Pitfall

Gabapentinoid accumulation is a frequently missed cause of altered mental status, myoclonus, ataxia, and falls in CKD and ESKD. Pregabalin and gabapentin are renally cleared and are dialyzable, so they require both renal dose reduction and a supplemental post-HD dose. Prescribing them at standard doses — or failing to recognize toxicity when a patient on these agents presents confused or sedated — is a common, avoidable error. Always reconcile the dose against eGFR and dialysis schedule.

Section 10

Referral Pathways & Medication-Assisted Treatment

Nephrologists and internists are well positioned to identify SUD and to initiate or co-manage treatment, but specialist addiction medicine input improves outcomes for moderate-to-severe disorders. A co-management model — where nephrology retains the renal and dialysis relationship while addiction services lead pharmacotherapy and psychosocial treatment — is generally most effective, leveraging the frequent contact dialysis affords.

Referral triggers

  1. Moderate-to-severe SUD by DSM-5 / ASAM criteria, or failure of brief intervention in primary care.
  2. Need for medication-assisted treatment (buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone) where the treating clinician is not licensed or experienced to initiate.
  3. Withdrawal requiring medical management (alcohol with prior seizures/DTs, severe opioid withdrawal, polysubstance withdrawal).
  4. Significant psychiatric comorbidity — suicidality, psychosis, severe depression — warranting integrated dual-diagnosis care.
  5. Repeated substance-related medical complications (recurrent AKI, missed dialysis, access infection, endocarditis in PWID).
  6. Pregnancy with SUD, or social/legal circumstances requiring structured rehabilitation pathways.
  7. Patient request for treatment — a relapse window that should never be deferred.

The Philippine treatment system

Navigating PH pathways

  • DOH-accredited Treatment & Rehabilitation Centers (TRCs): residential and outpatient facilities providing detoxification, rehabilitation, and aftercare; the backbone of structured treatment nationwide.
  • Dangerous Drugs Board (DDB): the policy-making and coordinating body; sets standards and oversees the national framework, including accreditation and the drug-dependency examination process.
  • Community-Based Drug Rehabilitation (CBDR): LGU- and barangay-anchored programs for individuals with mild-to-moderate use, integrating health, psychosocial support, and reintegration close to home — often the most accessible option.
  • Voluntary submission vs court-ordered: patients may enter treatment voluntarily (confidential, treatment-oriented) or via compulsory/court-mandated pathways under the Comprehensive Dangerous Drugs Act. Voluntary entry is preferred clinically; understand the local legal context and protect confidentiality wherever lawful.
  • Integration with nephrology: coordinate so that dialysis schedules, MAT dosing, and rehabilitation attendance do not conflict; supply the treatment facility with renal dosing constraints (e.g., avoid morphine/NSAIDs, gabapentinoid limits).
🩺 Clinical Pearl

The single most important referral skill is the warm handoff — directly connecting the patient to the addiction clinician or facility during the same encounter, rather than handing over a phone number. Motivation to engage is highest at the moment of disclosure and erodes quickly; a same-day, in-person or telephone introduction dramatically improves the odds the patient actually arrives. In the dialysis setting, schedule the first addiction contact to coincide with a dialysis day.

Section 11

Clinical Algorithms

Three operational algorithms for high-frequency, high-stakes scenarios. Each is decision-branching; adapt thresholds to local resources.

(A) Suspected drug-induced AKI — diagnostic workup

  1. Stabilize & screen: assess volume status, blood pressure, urine output; obtain creatinine/eGFR trend, electrolytes, urinalysis with microscopy, and CK. Take a focused substance history and consider a urine drug screen.
  2. Pattern recognition:
    • Markedly elevated CK + pigmented (heme-positive, RBC-negative) urine → rhabdomyolysis (stimulants, immobilization).
    • Sterile pyuria, white-cell casts, eosinophilia, recent drug exposure → acute interstitial nephritis.
    • Bland sediment + ischemic/hypotensive context → ATN / prerenal.
    • Hematuria, proteinuria, hypertension, low complement → consider glomerular / vasculitic / cryoglobulinemic process (HCV in PWID).
  3. Remove the offender: stop nephrotoxins (NSAIDs/mefenamic acid, contrast where avoidable), discontinue the suspected substance, correct hypovolemia.
  4. Targeted therapy: aggressive IV fluids for rhabdomyolysis; treat hyperkalemia; consider steroids for biopsy- or clinically-supported AIN; manage hypertensive injury.
  5. Escalate: nephrology referral; consider renal biopsy if diagnosis unclear or glomerular disease suspected; dialysis for refractory hyperkalemia, acidosis, volume overload, or uremia.
  6. Prevent recurrence: SBIRT, MAT referral, deprescribe nephrotoxins, document and educate.

(B) Stimulant chest pain / hypertensive crisis — sequenced management

  1. Immediate: ECG, continuous monitoring, IV access, troponin, vitals; ABCs.
  2. First-line agent — benzodiazepine: titrated IV benzodiazepine reduces sympathetic drive, lowers BP and heart rate, and relieves agitation and chest pain; often the single most effective intervention.
  3. Avoid unopposed beta-blockade in acute cocaine/methamphetamine toxicity (theoretical unopposed alpha vasoconstriction). Prefer benzodiazepines first.
  4. Persistent hypertension/ischemia: add nitroglycerin and/or a vasodilator (e.g., phentolamine, nicardipine) titrated to effect; aspirin for suspected ACS.
  5. Hyperthermia/agitation: active cooling, more benzodiazepine, fluids; check CK for rhabdomyolysis and monitor renal function.
  6. Disposition: serial troponins/ECG to exclude MI; treat arrhythmia per ACLS; admit for ongoing ischemia, arrhythmia, AKI, or rhabdomyolysis. Arrange SUD follow-up before discharge.

(C) SBIRT — Screen, Brief Intervention, Referral to Treatment

  1. Screen: apply a validated tool (e.g., AUDIT-C for alcohol, single-question drug screen, or NIDA-style screen) to every CKD/dialysis patient at intake and periodically.
  2. Stratify risk:
    • Negative → reinforce, rescreen at interval.
    • Low–moderate risk → proceed to brief intervention.
    • High risk / probable SUD → brief intervention plus referral.
  3. Brief intervention: 5–10 minute motivational, non-judgmental conversation; provide personalized feedback linking use to renal harm (AKI, progression, missed dialysis), elicit the patient's own goals, and negotiate a concrete next step.
  4. Referral to treatment: for moderate-severe disorders, initiate a warm handoff to addiction medicine / PH treatment pathway and consider MAT initiation or co-management.
  5. Document & follow up: record screen result, intervention, plan, and referral; revisit at the next dialysis or clinic contact to sustain engagement.
Section 12

Case Snapshots

Three condensed teaching cases illustrating recurrent substance-related renal presentations.

Case 1 — Methamphetamine + rhabdomyolysis AKI

A 28-year-old man presents agitated and hyperthermic after a multi-day "shabu" (methamphetamine) binge. He is tachycardic and hypertensive with diffuse muscle tenderness. Labs show CK >30,000 U/L, creatinine 4.2 mg/dL (baseline normal), hyperkalemia, and tea-colored urine that is heme-positive but red-cell-negative on microscopy.

  • Teaching points: Stimulant-induced rhabdomyolysis (from hyperthermia, agitation, vasoconstriction, and direct myotoxicity) is a classic cause of pigment-mediated AKI.
  • Cornerstone therapy is early, aggressive IV crystalloid to maintain brisk urine output; treat hyperkalemia urgently.
  • Control agitation/hyperthermia with benzodiazepines and active cooling — avoid unopposed beta-blockade.
  • Most cases recover renal function with supportive care; dialysis is reserved for refractory hyperkalemia, acidosis, or volume overload. Link to SBIRT and MAT before discharge.

Case 2 — Analgesic nephropathy with papillary necrosis

A 55-year-old woman with chronic headaches and dysmenorrhea has taken OTC mefenamic acid and combination analgesics daily for over a decade. She presents with CKD (eGFR 32), sterile pyuria, mild proteinuria, and an episode of gross hematuria with flank pain; imaging shows small, scarred kidneys with calcifications and a sloughed papilla.

  • Teaching points: Chronic, high-cumulative-dose NSAID/analgesic use causes chronic tubulointerstitial disease and renal papillary necrosis — a common, under-recognized exposure in the Philippines where mefenamic acid is freely available.
  • Hallmarks: bland-to-sterile pyuria, modest proteinuria, papillary necrosis (flank pain, hematuria, possible obstruction from sloughed papillae), and small scarred kidneys.
  • Management is cessation of all NSAIDs/offending analgesics, conservative CKD care, and substitution with acetaminophen and topical/non-nephrotoxic strategies.
  • Screen for the behavioral pattern of analgesic overuse and address the underlying pain and any analgesic-use disorder.

Case 3 — HCV cryoglobulinemic MPGN in a person who injects drugs

A 40-year-old man with a history of injection drug use presents with edema, hypertension, hematuria, and nephrotic-range proteinuria. He has palpable purpura and arthralgia. Workup reveals chronic hepatitis C, cryoglobulinemia, low C4, positive rheumatoid factor, and a biopsy showing membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN).

  • Teaching points: HCV (acquired through injection drug use) is a leading cause of cryoglobulinemic MPGN — suspect it in any PWID with a nephritic/nephrotic picture, purpura, and low complement.
  • Workup: HCV serology and RNA, cryoglobulins, complements (low C4), RF; biopsy confirms MPGN pattern.
  • Cornerstone treatment is direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy for HCV; severe/rapidly progressive cryoglobulinemic disease may require immunosuppression (e.g., rituximab) and, in fulminant cases, plasma exchange.
  • Integrate harm reduction, OUD treatment (MAT), and infectious-disease/hepatology co-management; this case ties SUD, viral hepatitis, and glomerular disease together.
Section 13

Evidence & Guidelines

Management of substance use disorders in CKD draws on nephrology guidelines for AKI and CKD, addiction-medicine standards for SUD treatment, the Philippine national drug-control framework, and a scattered but consistent body of nephrology literature on substance-related kidney injury.

Guidelines

  • KDIGO 2024 — AKI & AKD: updated guidance on definition, staging, risk stratification, nephrotoxin stewardship, and supportive management — directly applicable to drug-induced AKI (rhabdomyolysis, ATN, AIN).
  • KDIGO 2024 — CKD Evaluation & Management: emphasizes eGFR/albuminuria staging, progression risk, blood-pressure and metabolic targets, and avoidance of nephrotoxins (notably NSAIDs) — central to analgesic-nephropathy prevention.
  • ASAM 2024 — Treatment of Substance Use Disorders: evidence base for screening, the place of medication-assisted treatment (buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone), withdrawal management, and levels of care — adaptable to the renal/dialysis population.
  • DOH / Dangerous Drugs Board (Philippines): the national framework for accredited Treatment & Rehabilitation Centers, community-based drug rehabilitation, and voluntary vs compulsory treatment pathways under the Comprehensive Dangerous Drugs Act.

Key evidence themes

  • Stimulant nephropathy: consistent reports linking cocaine and methamphetamine to rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI, malignant hypertension, ischemic injury, and accelerated CKD.
  • Heroin / opioid-associated nephropathy: historical association with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and, in PWID, secondary processes (AA amyloidosis from chronic infection, HCV/HIV-related glomerular disease).
  • Analgesic nephropathy: robust evidence tying chronic high-dose NSAID/combination-analgesic use to chronic tubulointerstitial disease and papillary necrosis — highly relevant given OTC mefenamic-acid availability locally.
  • Synthetic cannabinoid AKI: case-series evidence of acute kidney injury (often ATN/AIN pattern) following "spice"/synthetic cannabinoid exposure.
  • MAT pharmacokinetics in ESKD: buprenorphine's hepatic clearance and favorable safety profile support its preference over methadone in dialysis patients.

Evidence gaps: high-quality, prospective data specific to SUD management in CKD and dialysis populations remain limited. Most renal-substance associations derive from case series, registries, and extrapolation rather than randomized trials, and dedicated pharmacokinetic/safety studies of MAT agents in ESKD are sparse. Synthetic and novel psychoactive substances are evolving faster than the literature. Until stronger CKD-specific evidence emerges, practice should integrate general SUD treatment standards (ASAM), renal protection principles (KDIGO), nephrotoxin stewardship, and the local Philippine treatment infrastructure, individualized to each patient.
