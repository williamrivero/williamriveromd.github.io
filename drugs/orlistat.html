Dosing: Adult

Dosage and administration (US label) US FDA label

2 DOSAGE AND ADMINISTRATION The recommended dosage of XENICAL is one 120 mg capsule taken orally three times a day with each main meal containing fat (during or up to 1 hour after the meal). (2) Take XENICAL with a nutritionally balanced, reduced-calorie diet that contains approximately 30% of calories from fat. (2) Distribute the daily intake of fat, carbohydrate, and protein over three main meals.

(2) Skip the dose of XENICAL if a meal is missed or contains no fat. Resume XENICAL at the next scheduled main meal containing fat. (2) Administer a multivitamin containing fat-soluble vitamins once daily at least 2 hours before or 2 hours after administration of XENICAL, or at bedtime. Dosing: Pediatric

Pediatric use (US label) US FDA label

8.4 Pediatric Use The safety and eﬀectiveness of XENICAL in combination with a reduced calorie diet and increased physical activity to reduce excess body weight and maintain weight reduction long term have been established in pediatric patients aged 12 years and older with obesity. Use of XENICAL in this indication is supported by a 54-week randomized, placebo-controlled trial in 539 pediatric patients aged 12 years and older with obesity (deﬁned as a baseline BMI that was 2 units greater than the US weighted mean for the 95 th percentile based on age and sex) [see Clinical Pharmacology (12.2), Clinical Studies (14.2)] and from trials in adult patients with obesity [see Clinical Studies (14.1)].

Adverse reactions with XENICAL treatment in pediatric patients aged 12 years and older were generally similar to those reported in adults [see Adverse Reactions (6.1)]. The safety and eﬀectiveness of XENICAL have not been established in pediatric patients less than 12 years of age.

5.1 Loss of Eﬀect of Certain Medications and Dietary Supplements with Coadministration

5.1 Loss of Eﬀect of Certain Medications and Dietary Supplements with Coadministration of XENICAL Serious outcomes due to the loss of eﬀect of cyclosporine, levothyroxine, warfarin, amiodarone, antiepileptic drugs, antiretroviral drugs, certain fat-soluble vitamins, and beta carotene have been reported with coadministration of XENICAL. Do not coadminister XENICAL simultaneously with cyclosporine or levothyroxine [see Drug Interactions (7)].

Administer cyclosporine 3 hours after the administration of XENICAL. Administer levothyroxine and XENICAL at least 4 hours apart. Administer a multivitamin supplement at least 2 hours before or 2 hours after the administration of XENICAL, or at bedtime. Monitor the eﬀectiveness of cyclosporine, levothyroxine, warfarin, amiodarone, antiepileptic drugs, antiretroviral drugs, certain fat-soluble vitamins, and beta carotene, as clinically indicated [see Drug Interactions (7) and Clinical Pharmacology (12.3)].

5.2 Liver Injury

5.2 Liver Injury There have been rare postmarketing reports of severe liver injury with hepatocellular necrosis or acute hepatic failure in patients treated with XENICAL, with some of these cases resulting in liver transplant or death [see Adverse Reactions (6.2)]. Instruct patients to report any symptoms of hepatic dysfunction (anorexia, pruritus, jaundice, dark urine, light-colored stools, or right upper quadrant pain) while taking XENICAL.

When these symptoms occur, immediately discontinue XENICAL and other suspected medications and obtain liver function tests, including ALT and AST levels.

5.3 Oxalate Nephrolithiasis

5.3 Oxalate Nephrolithiasis and Oxalate Nephropathy with Renal Failure XENICAL may increase levels of urinary oxalate. Cases of oxalate nephrolithiasis and oxalate nephropathy with renal failure have been reported [see Adverse Reactions (6.2)]. Monitor renal function when prescribing XENICAL to patients at increased risk for oxalate nephropathy, including patients with renal impairment and in those with a history of hyperoxaluria or calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis.

Discontinue XENICAL in patients who develop oxalate nephropathy.

5.4 Acute Gallbladder Disease

5.4 Acute Gallbladder Disease Acute events of gallbladder disease such as cholelithiasis, have been reported in clinical trials and during the postmarketing experience with XENICAL. In a 4-year clinical trial of XENICAL, cholelithiasis was reported in 2.9% (47/1649) of XENICAL-treated patients versus 1.8% (30/1655) of placebo-treated patients.

If cholelithiasis is suspected, gallbladder studies and appropriate clinical follow-up are indicated.

5.5 Gastrointestinal Events

5.5 Gastrointestinal Events Gastrointestinal events may increase when XENICAL is taken with a diet high in fat (>30% total daily calories from fat) [see Adverse Reactions (6.1)]. Advise patients receiving XENICAL to adhere to dietary guidelines [see Dosage and Administration (2)]. The daily intake of fat should be distributed over three main meals.

If XENICAL is taken with any one meal very high in fat, the possibility of gastrointestinal eﬀects increases.

6.1 Clinical Trials Experience

6.1 Clinical Trials Experience Because clinical trials are conducted under widely varying conditions, adverse reaction rates observed in the clinical trials of a drug cannot be directly compared to rates in the clinical trials of another drug and may not reflect the rates observed in patients. Most Commonly Observed Adverse Reactions during 1 Year of Treatment XENICAL was evaluated for safety in adults with obesity or overweight with at least one comorbid condition in seven multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials of 1- to 2-years duration, and in a 4-year trial.

The seven trials included 1,466 patients treated with placebo, 1,913 patients treated with XENICAL 120 mg orally three times a day, and 809 patients treated with unapproved dosages of XENICAL. Age of the patients ranged from 17 to 78 years; 80% were female, 91% White, 6% Black or African American, 2% were of Hispanic or Latino ethnicity, and 1% of other races.

Six of the trials enrolled patients without type 2 diabetes mellitus (Trials 1-6) and one of the trials enrolled a population of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (Trial 7). The 4-year trial (Trial 8) included 3,304 patients, age range 30-58 years; 55% were female; 99% were White and 1% other races. This trial included a subset of patients with abnormal glucose at baseline [see Clinical Studies (14.1)].

In placebo-controlled clinical trials, 9% of patients treated with XENICAL discontinued treatment due to adverse reactions, compared with 5% of placebo-treated patients. The most common adverse reactions that led to discontinuation of XENICAL treatment were gastrointestinal in nature. Gastrointestinal symptoms, as shown in Table 1, were the most commonly observed adverse reactions associated with the use of XENICAL in the ﬁrst year of seven placebo-controlled clinical trials.

6.2 Postmarketing Experience

6.2 Postmarketing Experience The following adverse reactions have been identiﬁed during postapproval use of XENICAL. Because these reactions are reported voluntarily from a population of uncertain size, it is not always possible to reliably estimate their frequency or establish a causal relationship to XENICAL exposure.

Immune System: Rare cases of hypersensitivity. Signs and symptoms have included pruritus, rash, urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm and anaphylaxis. Very rare cases of bullous eruption. Gastrointestinal: Pancreatitis; lower gastrointestinal bleeding. Hepatobiliary: Hepatitis that may be serious, and hepatic failure, with some of these cases resulting in liver transplant or death [see Warnings and Precautions (5.2)]; cholelithiasis [see Warnings and Precautions (5.4)]; increases in transaminases and alkaline phosphatase.

Skin and Subcutaneous Tissue: Leukocytoclastic vasculitis. Clinical signs include palpable purpura, maculopapular lesions, or bullous eruption. Renal and Urinary: Acute oxalate nephropathy and hyperoxaluria in patients with or at risk for renal disease.

Pregnancy considerations

Pregnancy (US label) US FDA label

8.1 Pregnancy Risk Summary Available data from published prospective and retrospective cohort studies over decades of use with XENICAL during pregnancy have not identiﬁed an increased risk of major birth defects, miscarriage or other adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. However, weight loss oﬀers no beneﬁt to a pregnant patient and may result in a small for gestational age infant.

When a pregnancy is recognized, advise the pregnant patient of the risk to a fetus and discontinue XENICAL (see Clinical Considerations). Oral administration of orlistat to pregnant rats and rabbits during the period of organogenesis did not show any adverse embryofetal eﬀects at doses 23 times and 47 times, respectively, the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) based on body surface area (mg/m 2) (see Data).

The background risk of major birth defects and miscarriage for the indicated population is unknown. All pregnancies have a background risk of birth defect, loss, or other adverse outcomes. In the U.S. general population, the estimated background risk of major birth defects and miscarriage in clinically recognized pregnancies is 2 to 4% and 15 to 20%, respectively.

Clinical Considerations Disease-associated maternal and/or embryo/fetal risk Appropriate weight gain based on pre-pregnancy weight is currently recommended for all pregnant patients, including those who have overweight or obesity, because of the obligatory weight gain that occurs in maternal tissues during pregnancy. Mechanism of action

12.1 Mechanism of Action Orlistat is a reversible inhibitor of gastrointestinal lipases. It exerts its therapeutic activity in the lumen of the stomach and small intestine by forming a covalent bond with the active serine residue site of gastric and pancreatic lipases. The inactivated enzymes are thus unavailable to hydrolyze dietary fat in the form of triglycerides into absorbable free fatty acids and monoglycerides. Undigested triglycerides are not absorbed, resulting in a caloric deﬁcit.

Figure 1 Dose-Response Relationship for Orlistat in Adult Human Volunteers At the recommended therapeutic dose of 120 mg three times a day, orlistat inhibits dietary fat absorption by approximately 30% in adult patients. Orlistat-treated pediatric patients excreted more fecal fat daily (27% of dietary intake) than placebo-treated pediatric patients (7% of dietary intake).

The magnitude of fecal fat excretion was consistent with that in adults (approximately 30%). Ethanol does not aﬀect orlistat’s eﬀect on preventing the absorption of fat. Based on fecal fat measurements, the eﬀect of orlistat is seen as soon as 24 to 48 hours after dosing. Upon discontinuation of therapy, fecal fat content usually returns to pretreatment levels within 48 to 72 hours.

Other Short-term Studies Adult Patients In several studies of up to 6-weeks duration, the eﬀects of therapeutic doses of orlistat on gastrointestinal and systemic physiological processes were assessed in subjects with normal weight and with obesity. Postprandial cholecystokinin plasma concentrations were lowered after multiple doses of orlistat in two studies but not signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from placebo in two other experiments.

There were no clinically signiﬁcant changes observed in gallbladder motility, bile composition or lithogenicity, or colonic cell proliferation rate, and no clinically signiﬁcant reduction of gastric emptying time or gastric acidity. In addition, no eﬀects on plasma triglyceride levels or systemic lipases were observed with the administration of orlistat in these studies.

In a 3-week study of 28 healthy male volunteers, orlistat (120 mg three times a day) did not signiﬁcantly aﬀect the balance of calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, zinc, copper, and iron. Half-life and elimination (verbatim, US label)

The primary metabolite M1 had a short half-life (approximately 3 hours) whereas the secondary metabolite M3 eliminated at a slower rate (half-life approximately 13.5 hours).

12.3 Pharmacokinetics Absorption Systemic exposure to orlistat is minimal. Following oral dosing with 360 mg 14 C-orlistat, plasma radioactivity peaked at approximately 8 hours; plasma concentrations of intact orlistat were near the limits of detection (<5 ng/mL). In therapeutic studies involving monitoring of plasma samples, detection of intact orlistat in plasma was sporadic and concentrations were low (<10 ng/mL or 0.02 µM), without evidence of accumulation, and consistent with minimal absorption.

Distribution In vitro orlistat was >99% bound to plasma proteins (lipoproteins and albumin were major binding proteins). Orlistat minimally partitioned into erythrocytes. Elimination Metabolism Based on an oral 14 C-orlistat mass balance study in patients with obesity, two metabolites, M1 (the hydrolyzed ß-lactone ring product of orlistat) and M3 (sequential metabolite after M1’s cleavage of the N-formyl leucine side-chain), accounted for approximately 42% of total radioactivity in plasma.

M1 and M3 have an open ß-lactone ring and extremely weak lipase inhibitory activity (1000- and 2500-fold less than orlistat, respectively). In view of this low inhibitory activity and the low plasma levels at the therapeutic dose (average of 26 ng/mL and 108 ng/mL for M1 and M3, respectively, 2 to 4 hours after a dose), these metabolites are considered pharmacologically inconsequential.

The primary metabolite M1 had a short half-life (approximately 3 hours) whereas the secondary metabolite M3 eliminated at a slower rate (half-life approximately 13.5 hours). Plasma concentrations of orlistat and its metabolites M1 and M3 were similar in pediatric patients to those found in adults at the same dose level.

Excretion Following a single oral dose of 360 mg 14 C-orlistat in subjects with normal weight and with obesity, fecal excretion of the unabsorbed drug was found to be the major route of elimination. Orlistat and its M1 and M3 metabolites were also subject to biliary excretion. Approximately 97% of the administered radioactivity was excreted in feces; 83% of that was found to be unchanged orlistat.

The cumulative renal excretion of total radioactivity was <2% of the given dose of 360 mg 14 C-orlistat. The time to reach complete excretion (fecal plus urinary) was 3 to 5 days. The disposition of orlistat appeared to be similar between subjects with normal weight and subjects with obesity. Based on limited data, the half-life of the absorbed orlistat is in the range of 1 to 2 hours.

Speciﬁc Populations No pharmacokinetic study was conducted for speciﬁc populations such as geriatric patients, patients of diﬀerent races, and patients with renal or hepatic impairment. Drug Interaction Studies Alcohol In a multiple-dose study in 30 normal-weight subjects, coadministration of orlistat and 40 grams of alcohol (e.g., approximately 3 glasses of wine) did not result in alteration of alcohol pharmacokinetics, orlistat pharmacodynamics (fecal fat excretion), or systemic exposure to orlistat.

Amiodarone In a pharmacokinetic study conducted in healthy volunteers who received 120 mg orlistat three times daily for 13 days and a single dose of 120 mg orlistat on the morning of Day 14 co-administered with a single dose of 1,200 mg amiodarone on Day 4, a 23 to 27% reduction in the systemic exposure to amiodarone and desethylamiodarone was observed [see Drug Interactions (7)]. The eﬀect of commencing orlistat treatment in patients on stable amiodarone therapy has not been studied.

Cyclosporine In a multiple-dose study, coadministration of 50 mg cyclosporine twice daily with 120 mg orlistat three times daily decreased cyclosporine AUC and C max by 31% and 25%, respectively. In the same study, administration of 50 mg cyclosporine twice daily three hours after the administration of 120 mg orlistat three times daily decreased cyclosporine AUC and C max by 17% and 4%, respectively.

Digoxin In 12 normal-weight subjects receiving orlistat 120 mg three times a day for 6 days, orlistat did not alter the pharmacokinetics of a single dose of digoxin. Fat-soluble Vitamin Supplements and Analogues A pharmacokinetic interaction study showed a 30% reduction in beta-carotene supplement absorption when concomitantly administered with orlistat.

Orlistat inhibited absorption of a vitamin E acetate supplement by approximately 60%. The eﬀect of orlistat on the absorption of supplemental vitamin D, vitamin A, and nutritionally-derived vitamin K is not known. Glyburide In 12 normal-weight subjects receiving orlistat 80 mg three times a day for 5 days, orlistat did not alter the pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics (blood glucose-lowering) of glyburide.

Nifedipine (extended-release tablets) In 17 normal-weight subjects receiving orlistat 120 mg three times a day for 6 days, orlistat did not alter the bioavailability of nifedipine (extended-release tablets). Oral Contraceptives In 20 normal-weight female subjects, the treatment of orlistat 120 mg three times a day for 23 days resulted in no changes in the ovulation-suppressing action of oral contraceptives.

Phenytoin In 12 normal-weight subjects receiving orlistat 120 mg three times a day for 7 days, orlistat did not alter the pharmacokinetics of a single 300-mg dose of phenytoin. Pravastatin In a 2-way crossover study of 24 normal-weight, mildly hypercholesterolemic patients receiving orlistat 120 mg three times a day for 6 days, orlistat did not aﬀect the pharmacokinetics of pravastatin.

Warfarin In 12 normal-weight subjects, administration of orlistat 120 mg three times a day for 16 days did not result in any change in either warfarin pharmacokinetics (both R- and S-enantiomers) or pharmacodynamics (prothrombin time and serum Factor VII). Although undercarboxylated osteocalcin, a marker of vitamin K nutritional status, was unaltered with orlistat administration, vitamin K levels tended to decline in subjects taking orlistat [see Drug Interactions (7)].
