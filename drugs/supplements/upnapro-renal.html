Kidney notes

UPNAPRO RENAL is a renal nutrition formula (AMBICA INTERNATIONAL CORPORATION) registered with the Philippine FDA as a food for special medical purposes (FSMP), not as a drug or a food supplement. It comes in a lower-protein (LP) version for CKD not on dialysis and a higher-protein (HP) version for dialysis. Its job is to help a patient meet energy and protein targets when food alone falls short.

What each variant is for

  • UPNAPRO RENAL HP: higher-protein version: for patients on dialysis, to help reach 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day when food alone falls short. Registered "for the dietary management of patients prone to malnutrition associated with chronic kidney disease vanilla flavor".
  • UPNAPRO RENAL LP: lower-protein version: for CKD not on dialysis; fit the formula's protein within a low-protein prescription (0.55 to 0.6 g/kg/day, or 0.6 to 0.8 g/kg/day with diabetes) and use it mainly to cover energy. Registered "for the dietary management of patients prone to malnutrition associated with chronic kidney disease vanilla flavor".
  • Some UPNAPRO RENAL registrations do not name the variant; check the label (LP or HP) before dispensing.

How it fits the KDOQI 2020 nutrition guideline

  • Chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages 3 to 5, not on dialysis, metabolically stable, without diabetes: KDOQI 2020 recommends a low-protein diet of 0.55 to 0.6 g/kg body weight per day, or a very low-protein diet of 0.28 to 0.43 g/kg/day with ketoacid analogues, under close dietitian supervision. With diabetes the target is 0.6 to 0.8 g/kg/day.
  • Maintenance hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, metabolically stable: 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg body weight per day.
  • CKD stages 3 to 5 or on dialysis, at risk of or with protein-energy wasting (PEW): KDOQI suggests a minimum 3-month trial of oral nutritional supplements when dietary counseling alone does not reach energy and protein needs.
  • A renal formula is a tool for reaching these targets. The lower-protein (LP) version has to fit inside the daily protein allowance of a patient not on dialysis; the higher-protein (HP) version helps a dialysis patient reach the higher target. Neither replaces individualized counseling by a renal dietitian.

Evidence

  • No clinical study of UPNAPRO RENAL itself in kidney disease was found in a PubMed brand-name search (November 17, 2026); the evidence below is for oral nutritional supplements as a class.
  • Cochrane review (Mah 2020): 22 randomized trials, 1,278 adults on dialysis. Oral protein-based supplements probably raise serum albumin (mean difference 0.19 g/dL, moderate certainty), more so on hemodialysis and in malnourished patients. Deaths were not reported as an outcome in any trial, so the effect on survival and other clinical outcomes is uncertain.
  • Meta-analysis (Liu 2023): 22 randomized trials, 1,185 patients on dialysis. Supplements raised serum albumin (1.26 g/L), body mass index and handgrip strength, with no significant change in serum phosphorus, potassium or C-reactive protein.
  • Renal-specific supplement trial (Fouque 2008): 86 hemodialysis patients with low intake randomized to a renal-specific oral supplement (another brand) for 3 months or standard care. Intake rose in the per-protocol analysis (not clearly in intention-to-treat), phosphate and binder use did not increase, and subjective global assessment and quality of life improved.
  • Observational data: in a large matched cohort of hypoalbuminemic hemodialysis patients, intradialytic supplements were associated with lower mortality (adjusted intention-to-treat hazard ratio 0.91, 95% CI 0.85 to 0.98), with residual confounding possible (Lacson 2012); a smaller disease-management cohort found fewer hospitalizations but no significant mortality difference (Cheu 2013).
  • Evidence for supplements in CKD not on dialysis is thinner: the trials above are in dialysis patients. In non-dialysis CKD, the formula's role is to cover energy needs while staying within a prescribed low-protein diet.

Cautions in kidney disease

  • Potassium, phosphorus and sodium: renal formulas are marketed as lower in these than standard formulas, but amounts differ by brand, variant and reformulation. Check the product label and count every serving in the day's potassium and phosphorus; monitor serum levels after starting.
  • Fluid: a ready-to-drink serving, or the water used to mix a powder, counts toward the interdialytic fluid allowance.
  • Variant mismatch: an HP (dialysis) version taken by a patient on a low-protein diet before dialysis adds protein beyond the prescription; an LP version on dialysis may not deliver enough protein.
  • Diabetes: these formulas contain carbohydrate. Check glucose after starting, especially with insulin or sulfonylureas, and use the diabetic protein target (0.6 to 0.8 g/kg/day) before dialysis.
  • Cost: these are paid out of pocket in most settings. Reserve them for patients with, or at risk of, protein-energy wasting, set a goal (intake, weight, albumin, subjective global assessment), and reassess after about 3 months.
  • A food for special medical purposes is meant for use under medical supervision; it supplements meals and does not replace them.

Bottom line

A reasonable choice for a patient with, or at risk of, protein-energy wasting whose intake falls short after dietitian counseling: match the variant to the stage (LP before dialysis, HP on dialysis), check the label for potassium, phosphorus and fluid, and reassess after about 3 months. Supplements raise albumin and intake; effects on survival are not proven by trials.
