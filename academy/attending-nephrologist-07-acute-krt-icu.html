Training track for attending nephrologists in adult practice

This module is study material for attending nephrologists and senior fellows in adult practice. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol. Doses and thresholds must be checked against current product labels, guidelines, and your institution’s approved protocols. Regulatory and benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Start acute kidney replacement therapy (KRT) for indications and trajectory, not for a creatinine number.
  • Match modality to hemodynamics, brain risk, local capability and transfer feasibility.
  • Write a plan that includes daily recovery review and stopping criteria.

The big picture, in plain words

Your duty · Section 1.2, 2024 hemodialysis guidelinesUnder the Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) guidelines, you coordinate during emergencies (b8), and you endorse by letter or call when your patient is under another nephrologist’s care in hospital (b12).1
In plain wordsDialysis in the intensive care unit (ICU) is life support for a failing organ, like a ventilator for the lungs. You put a patient on a ventilator when the lungs can no longer keep oxygen and carbon dioxide survivable, not because one blood gas looked bad.

The picture has a limit. A ventilator rests the lungs; dialysis does not rest the kidney and may slow its recovery. So the moment to start is a trade, not a threshold.

In Philippine practice you are usually the consultant, reached by phone at night by the intensivist or internist of record. Your work is to say whether there is an indication today, which modality this hospital can deliver safely, and what would change your answer.

Opening case · fictional

Case: The ICU wants your machine

A hospital 20 minutes from your unit calls you at 9 p.m. Mrs. Bautista, 66, has septic shock from a gallbladder infection. She is on norepinephrine and high-flow oxygen. Her fluid balance is now 9 liters positive, and her oxygen requirement keeps rising despite high-dose furosemide. The hospital has no continuous kidney replacement therapy (CRRT); the nearest center that does is 90 minutes away by ambulance.

The intensivist asks whether your unit’s nurses can bring a hemodialysis machine to the ICU and “pull off the fluid.” Your biomedical technician says one portable machine and a reverse-osmosis unit are free tonight. Your head nurse warns that her two nurses on call have never done a session in an ICU.

Before reading on, decide:

  • Is there an indication, or is this a number?
  • Should she be moved for continuous therapy, or treated where she is?
  • What must be agreed before your nurses connect her?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

How it works

The classic indications describe a loss of balance the body cannot buy back: potassium beyond cellular buffering, acidemia that depresses the heart and blunts vasopressors, fluid that floods the lungs, uremic pericarditis or encephalopathy, a dialyzable poison. Timing is a trade: starting earlier removes these threats sooner but exposes patients who would have recovered to catheter infection, hypotension, drug and nutrient loss, and possibly slower recovery.

Modality is a second trade. Intermittent hemodialysis (HD) works fast, which can drop blood pressure and, by lowering plasma osmolality quickly, drive water into an injured brain. Continuous therapy trades efficiency for steadiness.

On the ICU round · fictional

The intensivist points at Mr. Ramos’s creatinine: 5.2 mg/dL. “Should we start?” You look at the rest: potassium 4.6, bicarbonate 20, oxygen improving, urine output 700 mL yesterday and rising.

No balance has been lost that the body cannot buy back. You write: “No indication today. Start if potassium exceeds 6.0 despite treatment, pH falls below 7.15, or oxygen requirement rises with a positive fluid balance. Review tomorrow.”

What this shows. A written trigger lets the team act without waiting for you.

Lesson · part 2

What the evidence shows

Timing. STARRT-AKI randomized critically ill adults with severe acute kidney injury (AKI) to an accelerated start within 12 hours or a standard strategy that waited for conventional indications or AKI lasting more than 72 hours. Among 2,927 patients, 90-day mortality was 43.9% versus 43.7%. About 38% of the standard arm never needed dialysis. Among survivors, dialysis dependence was 10.4% versus 6.0% (relative risk 1.74), and adverse events 23.0% versus 16.5%.2 Early starts did not improve survival and may slow recovery.

How long to wait. AKIKI 2 randomized 278 patients who already had 72 hours of oliguria or a blood urea nitrogen above 112 mg/dL. Waiting further, until a mandatory indication or a urea nitrogen of 140 mg/dL, gave no more dialysis-free days (median 10 versus 12). In a multivariable analysis, the more-delayed strategy was associated with higher 60-day mortality (hazard ratio 1.65).3 Small trial, secondary finding, but waiting needs a ceiling.

Modality. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 guideline treats continuous and intermittent therapy as complementary (ungraded). It suggests CRRT for hemodynamically unstable patients (2B) and for acute brain injury or raised intracranial pressure (2B).4 Randomized trials have not shown a mortality difference between the two. The KDIGO 2026 draft, still in public review, revisits these questions; until it is final, quote the 2012 grades.5 The International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD) supports acute peritoneal dialysis (PD) where extracorporeal therapy is not available.6

RememberEarly starts did not save lives, and very late starts may cost them. Decide each day, against named triggers.
Lesson · part 3

What the attending does

Review daily, even by phone. Look for an absolute indication, or a trajectory toward one: potassium rising despite treatment, or fluid balance climbing while oxygenation worsens. Ask about goals of care. If you wait, write why and the triggers that end the wait, so the night resident can act without you.

Choose the modality the hospital can actually deliver. Many Philippine hospitals have no CRRT, and some ICUs depend on a dialysis unit’s nurses and a portable machine. Weigh hemodynamics, brain risk, nursing skill, water safety, and the danger of transfer; an unstable patient may be safer with slow therapy where she is.

Divide the work in writing, including the hourly fluid-removal goal, the pressure at which ultrafiltration stops, and how the team reaches you.

Look for recovery every day. Rising urine output, a creatinine that no longer climbs between sessions, and a timed urine creatinine clearance when available.

By phone, the head injury · fictional

A hospital calls about a man with a subdural hematoma and AKI. They have booked a standard four-hour hemodialysis session.

You explain that a fast session lowers plasma urea quickly, and water can then move into the injured brain. You ask for continuous therapy if it is available, or a short, gentle session with low blood flow, and you write the reason in the order.

What this shows. Choosing the modality is part of the prescription, and the brain is one of the reasons.

RememberEvery acute dialysis order needs four lines: the indication, the modality and why, who owns each task, and what would make you stop or transfer.
Stop and call
  • Potassium rising despite shifting therapy
  • Fluid overload with escalating ventilator or oxygen requirements
  • Uremic pericarditis or encephalopathy
  • Dialyzable toxin meeting Extracorporeal Treatments in Poisoning (EXTRIP) criteria
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The creatinine of 5 and nothing else

What you see
An ICU team wants to start dialysis because the creatinine reached 5.0 mg/dL. Potassium, bicarbonate, and oxygenation are stable, and urine output is rising.
What is likely going on
A number has crossed a line, but no indication is present.
What to do
Advise waiting, write the triggers that would change your mind, and review the patient daily.
Why it matters
In STARRT-AKI, accelerated starts did not save lives, and 38% of the standard group never needed dialysis.

Intermittent hemodialysis after head injury

What you see
A patient with a recent brain injury and AKI is booked for a standard four-hour session.
What is likely going on
Rapid solute removal lowers plasma osmolality and can drive water into an injured brain.
What to do
Ask for continuous or slow, low-efficiency therapy, or at least a gentle session with low blood flow and a short duration.
Why it matters
KDIGO suggests continuous therapy for acute brain injury, a graded suggestion grounded in physiology.

Waiting with no end

What you see
A patient has been “watched” for four days with rising potassium and climbing fluid balance.
What is likely going on
Watchful waiting has drifted into delay.
What to do
Decide today. AKIKI 2 suggested that delaying well beyond conventional criteria may be harmful.
Why it matters
Waiting needs a ceiling written in advance.
Field notes
  • Write why you are waiting, not only that you are waiting.
  • Choose modality by hemodynamics, brain risk, and the team at the bedside.
  • Look for recovery every day.
  • Do not transfer a stable patient only for a machine.
Case review

Back to the case: The ICU wants your machine

The indication. Fluid overload that threatens her lungs and does not respond to diuretics is a true indication. This is not a creatinine decision.

Move her or treat her there? Weigh how unstable she is to move against how gently your team can dialyze where she is. If your unit can run slow, prolonged therapy (sustained low-efficiency dialysis or slow isolated ultrafiltration), she may be safer staying; if her vasopressor dose climbs or ultrafiltration fails, transfer her. Write that trigger down.

Before connecting. Agree in writing on who is responsible for what: the intensivist runs the resuscitation, you write the prescription, and your nurses run the machine with an ICU nurse at the bedside. Set an hourly fluid-removal goal, a pressure floor, and the signed water and machine checks. If your nurses are not trained for this, say so. A safe plan may be one experienced nurse with your senior technician, or no session tonight and a transfer instead. Document the indication, the modality and why you chose it, and the goals of care discussed with her family, including relatives abroad who may call in.

Question 1 of 5What most strengthens the acute KRT indication?
Indications depend on the complication and its response to treatment.
Question 2 of 5What belongs in the acute KRT order review?
The prescription must address the indication and change with illness and recovery.
Question 3 of 5What did STARRT-AKI show?
Mortality was 43.9% versus 43.7%; dialysis dependence among survivors was 10.4% versus 6.0%.
Question 4 of 5Which patient most clearly favors CRRT over intermittent HD?
Gradual solute removal avoids rapid osmotic shifts that can worsen cerebral edema; KDIGO 2012 suggests CRRT here (2B).
