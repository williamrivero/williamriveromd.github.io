Study series for physicians on duty

This module is study material for physicians on duty in hemodialysis units. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol. At the chairside, follow your unit’s protocol and ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Doses and cut-off values are set by your unit. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Describe, with rough numbers, what healthy kidneys remove from the body each day.
  • Explain what builds up between dialysis sessions and where in the body it goes.
  • Predict what an intravenous fluid bolus does in a patient who makes no urine.
  • Explain why potassium rises, what moves it into and out of cells, and why the electrocardiogram can be falsely reassuring.
  • Explain why acid builds up and how dialysis corrects it.
  • Explain why the longest gap between sessions is the most dangerous time of the week.
Pre-testBefore you read, answer the 8 self-assessment questions and note your score. After the module, select New set of questions and compare. The difference is what you learned; the questions you still miss are what to reread.
Start here

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsPicture a house where the tap runs a little all day and the drain is closed. Nothing flows out until someone opens the drain for four hours, two or three times a week. Between those times, everything that comes in stays in. A patient without kidney function lives in that house. Water, salt, potassium, and acid from every meal and every drink wait in the body for the next dialysis session.

The picture is useful, and it has one limit. The body is not a single tank. Water, salt, and potassium sit in different compartments and move between them at different speeds. Most of this module is about those compartments, because they explain why treatment that is right for other patients can harm this one.

Figure 2 · Key terms for this module

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD)Kidney failure that needs dialysis or a transplant to sustain life.
AnuricMaking almost no urine, usually less than about 100 milliliters a day.
Residual kidney functionThe urine and waste removal that failed kidneys still manage. Many patients keep some for months or years.
Interdialytic weight gainWeight gained between two sessions. Nearly all of it is water.
Extracellular fluidWater outside the cells: in the blood and between the cells.
UltrafiltrationRemoval of water from the blood by pressure across the dialyzer membrane.
HyperkalemiaA blood potassium level above the normal range.
Metabolic acidosisA fall in blood bicarbonate because acid has built up.
Opening case · fictional

Case: One liter, fast

Mr. Dela Cruz, 58, has had no urine output for two years. He comes to the emergency room on a Sunday night with vomiting and loose stools since the morning. His blood pressure is 88/50 mm Hg and his heart rate is 110 per minute. His last dialysis was on Friday and his next is on Monday.

The emergency room physician, who rarely sees dialysis patients, orders what works for gastroenteritis in anyone else: one liter of intravenous saline, given fast, then a second liter, and a maintenance drip overnight. By morning his blood pressure is 150/95, he cannot lie flat, and his oxygen saturation is 86%. He arrives at your unit by wheelchair for his Monday session.

Before reading on, decide:

  • Was any intravenous fluid appropriate on Sunday night?
  • Where did the two liters and the overnight drip go?
  • What would you have ordered instead, and what would you watch?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

What healthy kidneys do every day

To understand what is missing, start with what is normal. Two healthy kidneys filter about 180 liters of fluid from the blood every day, roughly 125 milliliters a minute. They take back about 99% of it and send out 1 to 2 liters as urine.1 That urine carries exactly what the body does not need that day.

What comes in each dayWhat healthy kidneys doWith no kidney function
Water from drinks and foodPass more or less urine to keep body water steadyStays. Only sweat, breath, and stool remove water, about half a liter to a liter a day
Salt (sodium chloride)Excrete the day’s intakeStays, and holds water with it
Potassium from foodExcrete about 90% of the day’s intake; the gut removes the restStays. The gut removes somewhat more than before, but not enough
Acid from the breakdown of proteinExcrete the acid and rebuild bicarbonateStays, and uses up bicarbonate
Urea and other wastesFilter and excreteStay

The kidneys do this work continuously, 168 hours a week. Standard hemodialysis (HD) does it for about 12 hours a week, or 8 hours on a twice-weekly schedule.2 The body spends the rest of the week storing what it cannot remove.

The gut and the skin help a little. In kidney failure the large bowel secretes more potassium than usual, which is one reason constipation can raise the blood potassium in a dialysis patient.3 These routes cannot replace the kidneys.

RememberAsk every patient: “How much urine do you still pass in a day?” A patient who passes a liter a day and one who passes none are different patients, and the answer changes how much fluid and potassium each can handle.

Some patients keep useful urine output for months or years after starting dialysis. In a study of 6,538 patients who started hemodialysis, those who lost this residual kidney function faster during the first year were more likely to die in the years that followed.4 This was an observational study, so it shows an association and does not prove that preserving urine output prolongs life. It is still a good reason to protect what function remains. Module 3 covers how.

Lesson · part 2

Water and salt: the closed drain

Salt and water travel together. When a patient eats salt, the sodium level in the blood rises slightly, the patient becomes thirsty, and drinks until the sodium is back to normal. The body then holds that salt and water together as extra fluid. Normal body fluid contains about 9 grams of salt per liter, so 9 grams of salt, less than two teaspoons, is held with about one liter of water.1

This is why advice to “drink less” fails when it is given alone. A patient who eats salty food will be thirsty and will drink. Cutting salt reduces thirst, and less thirst means less weight gain.5

Where does the fluid go? It stays outside the cells, in the extracellular fluid. About a quarter of that is in the blood vessels and the rest lies between the cells of the tissues.

Figure 6 · What extra salt and water do

  1. Vessels fillBlood volume rises and blood pressure climbs.
  2. Heart stretchesThe heart works against more volume and more pressure.
  3. Tissues swellFluid collects in the legs, the back, and around the eyes.
  4. Lungs floodFluid enters the air spaces and the patient becomes breathless.

Edema in the legs appears only after several liters have collected, so a patient can be badly overloaded with normal-looking ankles. Breathlessness on lying flat, a raised blood pressure, and the weight gain itself are earlier signs.

Large weight gains matter beyond the day they occur. In a study of more than 34,000 patients on hemodialysis in the United States, those who gained 4 kilograms or more between sessions had a higher risk of cardiovascular death than those who gained 1.5 to 2 kilograms, after adjustment for other factors (hazard ratio 1.25).6 The study was observational. Sicker patients may gain more, so it does not prove that weight gain alone caused the deaths.

Why the fluid cannot simply be pulled off fast

Dialysis removes water from the blood, and the blood holds only about 3 liters of plasma. To remove 3 or 4 liters in one session, the tissues must refill the blood vessels as fast as the machine empties them. This is called plasma refilling. When the machine removes fluid faster than the tissues can give it back, blood volume drops and so does blood pressure.2

Fluid removal rate = fluid to remove (mL) ÷ treatment time (hours) ÷ body weight (kg)
Illustration: 3,000 mL over 4 hours in a 60-kg patient = 12.5 mL per kg per hour. The same 3,000 mL over 3 hours = 16.7 mL per kg per hour.

Faster removal is associated with harm. In an analysis of 1,846 patients from the Hemodialysis (HEMO) Study, removal rates above 13 mL per kg per hour were associated with a higher risk of death than rates up to 10 mL per kg per hour (adjusted hazard ratio 1.59).7 This was also an observational analysis. No randomized trial has tested a specific limit, and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) conference participants described fluid management as a balance between leaving fluid behind and removing it too fast.5 Your unit’s protocol sets the limit you work with.

Stop and callCall the attending nephrologist when the fluid goal cannot be reached at a safe rate in the time booked. The choices, such as a longer session, an extra session, or a smaller goal today, belong to the prescription.
Lesson · part 3

What an intravenous fluid bolus does here

In most patients, intravenous fluid is forgiving. If you give a little too much, the kidneys pass it within hours. In a patient who makes no urine, every milliliter stays until the next dialysis session. The fluid first expands the blood volume, then spreads into the tissues, and when the tissues are full it enters the lungs.

Common orderIn a patient with working kidneysIn a patient who makes no urine
One liter of saline, given fastRaises blood volume; excess is passed as urineAll of it stays. Useful only if the patient is truly volume depleted, and then in small measured amounts
Maintenance dripReplaces what the kidneys passThere is nothing to replace. A drip left running overnight can cause pulmonary edema
“Keep vein open” lineHarmlessAdds up over hours. Use a capped cannula instead
Medicines diluted in large bagsHarmlessAsk the pharmacist for the smallest safe volume
Fluids that contain potassiumThe kidneys excrete the potassiumThe potassium stays as well
Blood transfusionVolume is toleratedBest given during dialysis, so the volume and potassium can be removed

This does not mean that a dialysis patient never needs fluid. A patient with vomiting, diarrhea, bleeding, or sepsis can be truly short of volume, and a patient whose blood pressure falls during dialysis may need fluid returned. The difference is in how you give it.

Figure 7 · Giving fluid to a patient who makes no urine

  1. Confirm the needIs the patient below dry weight, with a history of losses? Or is the low pressure from the heart, infection, or bleeding?
  2. Give a small, measured amountUse the volume in your unit’s protocol, not a general ward bolus.
  3. ReassessBlood pressure, heart rate, breathing, and oxygen saturation after each amount.
  4. Stop when the pressure recoversDo not continue to a fixed total.
  5. Record the volumeThe dialysis team needs to know how much to remove.
  6. Tell the nephrologistThe next session may need to change.
RememberBefore you sign any fluid order for a dialysis patient, ask: “Where will this fluid be tomorrow?” If the answer is “still inside the patient,” give less, give it slower, and look again.
Lesson · part 4

Potassium: a small pool with large effects

Potassium is the body’s main mineral inside cells. About 98% of it is inside cells, mostly in muscle. Only about 2% is in the fluid outside cells, and that small amount is what a blood test measures.3 The steep difference between the inside and the outside sets the electrical charge across every nerve and heart muscle cell.

Two consequences follow. First, a small shift of potassium out of cells produces a large rise in the blood. Second, the blood level can change within minutes, long before the total amount in the body has changed.

A healthy person handles a meal rich in potassium in two steps. Within minutes, insulin released by the meal pushes potassium into cells, which blunts the rise in the blood. Over the next hours, the kidneys excrete the load.3 A patient without kidney function still has the first step and has lost the second. The potassium waits inside cells until dialysis.

Moves potassium into cells (lowers blood level)Moves potassium out of cells (raises blood level)
InsulinLack of insulin, as in fasting or uncontrolled diabetes
Adrenaline-like stimulation (beta-2 receptors), including salbutamolBeta-blocking medicines (a modest effect)
A rise in blood bicarbonateAcid build-up from kidney failure
—Very high blood sugar, which draws water and potassium out of cells
—Tissue breakdown: crush injury, bleeding into the gut, hemolysis

These shifts explain several things you will see. A dialysis patient who fasts for a long time can become hyperkalemic without eating any potassium, because insulin is low.3 The standard emergency treatments for a high potassium, insulin with glucose and inhaled salbutamol, work by moving potassium into cells. They buy time. They do not remove potassium from the body. In a patient without kidney function, only dialysis does that in a useful amount.2

Why the electrocardiogram cannot reassure you

A high potassium slows electrical conduction in the heart and can cause a fatal rhythm. The electrocardiogram (ECG) may show tall, peaked T waves, a wide QRS complex, or a slow rhythm. It may also show nothing. In a review of 90 hospital patients with a potassium of 6.0 mmol/L or more, the investigators found the ECG insensitive for diagnosing hyperkalemia. Changes became more likely as the potassium rose, but no T-wave measurement reliably separated dangerous levels from safe ones, and they advised against using the ECG to guide treatment in stable patients.8

RememberA normal ECG does not rule out a dangerous potassium level. An abnormal ECG in a patient who may be hyperkalemic is an emergency.

After dialysis the opposite can happen. Dialysis lowers the blood potassium quickly, and potassium then moves back out of cells over the next few hours, so the level rises again. A blood sample taken right after dialysis reads lower than the level the patient will have that evening.2

Stop and callCall the attending nephrologist at once for a patient with a high potassium result, an ECG change, or muscle weakness, and follow your unit’s hyperkalemia protocol while you call. The potassium level of the dialysis fluid is part of the prescription.
Lesson · part 5

Acid: the daily load and the buffer

Breaking down the protein in food produces acid, roughly one millimole of acid for each kilogram of body weight every day. Healthy kidneys excrete this acid and rebuild the bicarbonate that was used to neutralize it.9

Without kidney function, the acid stays. Bicarbonate in the blood neutralizes it and is used up, so the blood bicarbonate falls between sessions. When bicarbonate runs low, bone gives up some of its mineral to help neutralize the acid, and muscle protein is broken down faster.9 Over years this contributes to weak bone and loss of muscle.

Figure 9 · Acid across the week

  1. Protein is eatenThe body makes acid every day.
  2. Bicarbonate neutralizes itBlood bicarbonate falls a little each day.
  3. Bone and muscle helpThey give up mineral and protein when bicarbonate is low.
  4. Dialysis reloads the bufferThe dialysis fluid contains bicarbonate, which crosses into the blood.
  5. After dialysisBicarbonate is at its highest, then falls again.

Dialysis does not remove acid directly. It gives bicarbonate. The dialysis fluid has a higher bicarbonate level than the patient’s blood, so bicarbonate moves into the patient during the session.2 The blood bicarbonate is therefore lowest just before a session and highest just after.

Acid and potassium are linked. The kind of acid that builds up in kidney failure pushes potassium out of cells, so a patient who is more acidotic tends to have a higher blood potassium.3 A low bicarbonate before dialysis is expected. A very low bicarbonate, or a patient who is breathing deeply and fast, is not, and suggests another cause such as infection, diabetic ketoacidosis, or a missed session.

Lesson · part 6

The week on dialysis and the long gap

Put the three together and a pattern appears. Weight, potassium, acid, and urea climb between sessions and fall during each one. The peaks are highest after the longest gap.

On a three-times-a-week schedule the longest gap is two days. In a study of 32,065 patients in the United States on this schedule, the death rate on the day after the two-day gap was 22.1 per 100 person-years, compared with 18.0 on other days. Hospital admissions for heart failure, heart attack, stroke, and rhythm problems were also more frequent on that day.10 The study was observational, but the pattern fits the physiology: the first session after the long gap meets the highest fluid, potassium, and acid load of the week.

Many patients in the Philippines dialyze twice a week. Their gaps are three and four days long. The study above did not include twice-weekly patients, so its numbers cannot be applied to them directly. The same physiology applies, though, and it is reasonable to expect the longest gap to be the riskiest time for them as well.

Figure 12 · What this means on the floor

Know the scheduleFor each patient, know which session follows the long gap.
Look harder that dayExpect the largest weight gain, the highest potassium, and the lowest bicarbonate.
Ask about the gapSalt, fluid, fruit, missed medicines, and any illness since the last session.
Missed sessions multiply the riskA patient who skipped the last session has an even longer gap. See them first.
Lesson · part 7

General medicine rules that change in this patient

Much of what you learned for other patients assumes that the kidneys will correct small errors. Here they cannot. This table collects the main differences from this module. Later modules add more for medicines, tests, and the heart.

Usual ruleIn a patient without kidney functionWhy
“Give a fluid bolus for low blood pressure”Find the cause first; give small measured amounts per protocolNo urine to remove the excess
“Drink plenty of fluids”Follow the fluid allowance set by the nephrologistIntake stays until dialysis
“Start a maintenance drip”Use a capped cannulaThere are no urinary losses to replace
“Replace potassium when it is low-normal”Do not give potassium without the nephrologistA small dose can cause a large rise
“A banana and coconut water for cramps or diarrhea”Avoid; both are high in potassiumThe load cannot be excreted
“Fast before the procedure”Keep fasting short and check blood sugar and potassiumLow insulin lets potassium leave cells
“A normal ECG rules out danger”It does not rule out a high potassiumThe ECG is an insensitive test
“A low bicarbonate needs urgent work-up”A mildly low level before dialysis is expectedAcid builds up between sessions
“A creatinine this high means acute kidney injury”A high creatinine and urea are this patient’s baselineCompare with the patient’s own previous results
RememberFor any laboratory result in a dialysis patient, ask two questions before you react. When was the sample taken in relation to dialysis? What is this patient’s own usual value?

What counts as an acceptable potassium, bicarbonate, or weight gain before dialysis is set by your unit’s protocol and the attending nephrologist. Learn those values for your unit before your first shift.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The drip that came with the patient

What you see
A patient who makes no urine is sent from a ward for dialysis with a liter of saline running at a “maintenance” rate. It has been running since the night before.
What is likely going on
The ward team ordered what they order for every admitted patient.
What to do
Stop the drip and cap the cannula. Find out how much has gone in, examine the patient for overload, and tell the attending nephrologist, because today’s fluid removal target may need to change. Call the ward team and explain why.
Why it matters
Every milliliter is still inside the patient. Teaching the ward prevents the same order tomorrow.

Fasting since midnight

What you see
A diabetic dialysis patient has fasted for 14 hours for a procedure that was delayed. She feels weak. Her blood sugar is normal.
What is likely going on
Long fasting lowers insulin, and potassium moves out of cells. Weakness may be a sign of a high potassium.
What to do
Get an electrocardiogram and an urgent potassium, follow your unit’s hyperkalemia protocol if it is high, and call the attending nephrologist. Remember that a normal electrocardiogram does not rule it out.
Why it matters
Hyperkalemia can develop without the patient eating any potassium.

Normal ankles, wet lungs

What you see
A thin, elderly patient is 3 kilograms above dry weight. Her ankles are not swollen, but she slept sitting up and has crackles at both lung bases.
What is likely going on
She is overloaded. Leg edema appears late, and less in patients who have been sitting or lying.
What to do
Treat her as overloaded: sit her up, check oxygen saturation, and report the findings. Do not be reassured by the ankles.
Why it matters
Breathlessness on lying flat and weight gain are earlier and more reliable signs than ankle swelling.

The Friday result on a Monday

What you see
A potassium of 3.6 mmol/L is on the chart, and a relative asks whether the patient should eat more bananas. The sample was taken right after last Friday’s session.
What is likely going on
A sample taken right after dialysis is the lowest value of the week, and it rebounds within hours.
What to do
Explain that the result does not show a low potassium. Advise no change in diet, and check when the next pre-dialysis sample is due.
Why it matters
Acting on the timing-distorted result could cause dangerous hyperkalemia over the long gap.

Weight below target, pressure low

What you see
A patient arrives 0.8 kilograms below his dry weight after three days of poor appetite and loose stools. His blood pressure is 92/58.
What is likely going on
He may be truly short of volume, and his real body weight may also be falling.
What to do
Examine him and look for infection. Call the attending nephrologist before starting: the session may need little or no fluid removal, and the dry weight may need review.
Why it matters
Removing fluid from a patient who is already dry causes low blood pressure and can injure the heart and gut.
Field notes
  • The first question for any dialysis patient is how much urine they still pass.
  • Look at the trend of pre-dialysis weights over several weeks, not just today’s.
  • Ask what the patient ate over the gap. Dried fish, instant noodles, and sauces explain most large weight gains.
  • When another department telephones about a dialysis patient’s fluids, take the call. It is the cheapest prevention you will do all day.
  • Always note when a blood sample was taken in relation to dialysis.
Case review

Back to the case: One liter, fast

Was any fluid appropriate? Probably yes, a little. Mr. Dela Cruz had a day of vomiting and diarrhea, a low blood pressure, and a fast heart rate two days after his last session. He may truly have been short of volume. The mistake was not the decision to give fluid. It was the amount, the speed, and the maintenance drip.

Where did it go? He makes no urine, so all of it stayed. The saline first refilled his blood vessels, which is why his pressure recovered and then overshot. It then spread into his tissues. When they could hold no more, fluid entered his lungs. The overnight drip replaced urinary losses that he does not have.

What would have been better? A small measured amount of fluid, with his blood pressure, breathing, and oxygen saturation checked after each amount, stopping as soon as the pressure recovered. No maintenance drip. A check of his potassium and blood sugar, since diarrhea, vomiting, and poor intake can move both. A search for other causes of low pressure, including infection and a heart problem. And a telephone call to his nephrologist or the dialysis unit on Sunday night, so that Monday’s session could be planned.

And now? He is in pulmonary edema at your door. Sit him upright, give oxygen, examine him, and call the attending nephrologist for urgent dialysis orders. Record how much fluid he received overnight, because that volume is part of what must be removed.

The emergency room physician did what is right for most patients. Part of your role is to be the person other doctors can call about a dialysis patient before the second liter is hung.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Bedside volume assessment

Perform this assessment on three patients before their sessions while your preceptor observes. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • CriticalAsks how much urine the patient still passes in a day.
  • CriticalConfirms today’s weight, the dry weight, and the weight after the last session, and calculates the gain.
  • Asks about breathlessness, breathlessness on lying flat, and how many pillows the patient sleeps on.
  • Asks about salt and fluid intake, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, and poor appetite since the last session.
  • CriticalMeasures blood pressure and heart rate, seated, and compares them with the patient’s usual values.
  • CriticalMeasures oxygen saturation on room air.
  • Looks at the neck veins with the patient at 45 degrees.
  • CriticalListens to the lung bases for crackles.
  • Checks for edema at the ankles and, in a patient who has been in bed, over the sacrum.
  • CriticalStates a conclusion: overloaded, near dry weight, or below dry weight, with the findings that support it.
  • CriticalCalculates the fluid removal rate in mL per kg per hour for the planned session and compares it with the unit’s limit.
  • States what would be reported to the attending nephrologist.
