Role subtrack for registered nurses

This subtrack prepares Philippine registered nurses who are assigned a special portfolio in a hemodialysis unit: vascular access, infection prevention, education, quality, safety, coordination, anemia, transplant liaison, records, water and equipment liaison, or adequacy and outcomes. It teaches assessment, coordination, teaching and escalation within nursing scope. Prescriptions, dose changes, access procedures, transplant listing, water-system repair and technical release belong to the physician, the authorized team and the technical staff. All cases are synthetic.

Learning objectives · Special Dialysis Nurse Roles (SDNR)-13 · about 8 hours · week 7

After this module you should be able to

  • Integrate immediate safety, role boundaries and closed-loop coordination in a multi-problem shift.
  • Deliver a bounded portfolio project with honest outcome, process and balancing measures.
  • Demonstrate performance in four objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) stations and respond to assessor feedback.
  • Write a feasible 30-60-90-day role-development plan with protected time and measures.

Time plan. 1 h study · 2 h workshop · 5 h supervised practice. The supervised hours are arranged by your unit and recorded in its own competency system; this page records self-study only.

Intuition

The orchestra, not the solo

Each module so far has trained one instrument: access, infection, teaching, quality, safety, coordination, anemia, transplant liaison, records, water, adequacy. Real shifts are orchestral. Problems arrive together, overlap and compete, and a portfolio nurse who plays only her own part loudly can drown out what matters most.

The picture has a limit. An orchestra has a conductor and a score. In a dialysis unit the conductor is the charge nurse and the physician, but the score is written live. The capstone tests whether you can do three things at once: put immediate patient safety first, play your own portfolio part within its boundary, and hand over clearly to every other part so that nothing falls between the instruments.

In plain wordsPatients first, scope always, loops closed. Then show, with honest numbers, what your portfolio changed.
Integrated case

Work through an integrated case

Tuesday, second shift · fictional

10:45 a.m. Mr. Arturo Gonzales, 57, returned yesterday from another hospital after treatment for a catheter-related bloodstream infection. He has a tunneled catheter and a pending fistula referral. His discharge sheet says "cefazolin 3x weekly" with no dose timing relative to dialysis; the unit has not yet received a physician order for it. He has a transplant evaluation appointment tomorrow at 9 a.m.

11:20 a.m. One hour into dialysis he has rigors, temperature 38.8 °C, blood pressure 86/50.

11:35 a.m. Mrs. Perla Ignacio, on the same water loop, reports chills.

11:40 a.m. You learn that the machine at station 9, released for use this morning, has an unsigned release sheet and no documented residual disinfectant test. It is the machine Mr. Gonzales is on.

Priorities, in order.

  1. Patient safety nowCharge nurse and physician to Mr. Gonzales: stop or modify treatment per order, vital signs, blood cultures before any antibiotic as ordered. Assess Mrs. Ignacio.
  2. Stop further exposureNo new patient on the loop or machine 9 until the technical team assesses; preserve machine 9 and supplies (Modules 3 and 12).
  3. NotifyPhysician, charge nurse, head nurse, water and biomedical technicians, infection prevention.
  4. ReconcileThe antibiotic order is unclear: physician and pharmacist decide drug, dose and timing (Module 8).
  5. Coordinate tomorrowTell the transplant program about today's events so they can decide on tomorrow's evaluation (Module 10).
  6. DocumentFacts, times, exposures, decisions and owners (Module 11).
RememberSeveral portfolio nurses may be involved. Each brings her part to the charge nurse and physician, who decide. No one acts on a portfolio task in a way that delays a patient's immediate care.
Project

Choose a bounded project

The capstone project shows what your portfolio can change in a few months. Keep it bounded: one problem, one unit, one change at a time, measured honestly (Module 6).

ProblemMatters to patients, frequent enough to measure, within the unit's power.
AimWhat, for whom, how much, by when.
MeasuresOutcome, process, balancing; numerator, denominator, exclusions.
ChangeTested in Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles with predictions.
DataRun chart with baseline median and run rules.
BoundariesWhat the project does not change (prescriptions, technical systems, others' roles).

Example projects: access referral closure within tier deadlines (Module 2); weekend hand hygiene adherence (Module 3); catheter-care reassessment completion (Module 4); teach-back documentation (Module 5); corrective-action effectiveness (Module 7); post-hospital reconciliation before the next session (Module 8); anemia review closure (Module 9); documented options conversations (Module 10); delivered-minutes accuracy (Module 11); release evidence before connection (Module 12); delivered-time adherence (Module 13).

Present evidence, not completion. "We trained everyone" is activity. "Weekend hand hygiene rose from a median of 58 to 82 percent, a shift sustained for eight weeks, with no change in start delays" is evidence. If your change did not improve the outcome, present that honestly with what you learned; the method and patient safety are assessed, not only the result.

From "improve infection control" to something countable · fictional

Nurse Kris, three months into the infection prevention portfolio, proposes a capstone project to "improve infection control in the unit." Her preceptor asks her to narrow it until it has one measure, one denominator and one change she can test in six weeks.

Kris looks at her own audit data. Catheter hub disinfection before connection was observed correctly in 38 of 60 observations last month (63 percent). She writes an aim: "Raise correct hub disinfection at connection from 63 to 90 percent within six weeks, measured by 15 observed connections a week across all shifts." Her first test of change is a laminated step card clipped to each catheter tray on one shift for one week. Her balancing measure is connection time, so the fix does not quietly slow the whole shift.

What this shows. A good capstone is small enough to finish and honest enough to fail. The run chart, not the intention, is the evidence.

Assessment

Four stations and how they are marked

Each station is ten minutes, run on a simulator, unconnected machine or standardized colleague, never on a real patient. Each is scored out of 10 by a calibrated assessor. To pass the capstone: at least 32 of 40 overall, at least 7 of 10 at every station, and no critical error.

Each station is followed by immediate feedback. A learner who does not pass a station repeats it after a remediation plan (Module 4); the result is recorded by the facilitator and, for workplace competence, by the facility.

Practice

Present evidence, not effort

The fourth station asks you to present your portfolio. Examiners are not scoring how hard you worked; they are asking whether the work changed anything and whether you can tell the difference between what you did and what it achieved.

  1. The problem, with a numberWhat was happening before, with its numerator and denominator (Module 6).
  2. What you changedThe tests of change, in order, including the ones that did not work.
  3. What the data showA run chart with its median and any run-rule signal, and the balancing measure.
  4. What you cannot claimSmall numbers, a short time frame, other changes happening at the same time.
  5. What happens nextWho owns the measure after the project, and how often it will be checked.
"Zero infections" · fictional

Nurse Carlo opens his presentation with a slide reading "Zero bloodstream infections in March!" The examiner asks how many catheter patient-months the unit had in March. Carlo checks: 11. She asks how many infections the unit usually has. About one every three or four months.

Carlo revises his claim during the station: "One month with no infection in 11 catheter patient-months is consistent with our usual rate. I can't say yet that the bundle changed it. What I can show is that observed hub disinfection rose from 63 to 88 percent over six weeks, and I'll keep tracking infections per 100 catheter patient-months monthly for a year."

What this shows. A rare event in a small denominator often reads zero by chance. An honest presenter separates the process measure that clearly moved from the outcome that needs much longer to judge, and scores higher for it.

Practice case · synthetic

Case: Everything at once

Second shift, 11:20 to 11:45 a.m. As described in the integrated case: Mr. Gonzales (tunneled catheter, back from hospital, unclear cefazolin order, pending fistula referral, transplant evaluation tomorrow) develops rigors and hypotension on machine 9; Mrs. Ignacio on the same loop has chills; machine 9's release sheet is unsigned and its residual test is missing.

The infection prevention nurse, the water liaison nurse, the care coordinator and the transplant liaison are all on shift. The charge nurse asks you, the access coordinator, to "help."

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, whom you would contact and what you would say, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the debrief below.

Assess

Two patients first; then exposure control on machine 9 and the loop; then the unclear antibiotic order; then tomorrow's transplant appointment; then the pending fistula referral. Your access portfolio is the least urgent problem today.

Communicate

Ask the charge nurse what she needs from you. Offer to take patient care tasks or to coordinate notifications so portfolio colleagues can do their parts. Use SBAR, confirm receipt, record owners.

Teach

Afterward, at the debrief, show how the portfolios fitted together and where a handoff nearly dropped (for example, telling the transplant program).

Escalate

Make sure the physician, clinical head, technicians, infection prevention and transplant program have each been informed, by name and time, and that restart waits for technical release and clinical approval.
How it played out · fictional

Both patients were assessed and treated by the physician; blood cultures were drawn before antibiotics. Machine 9 was held; its release had not been completed and it was tested and released that afternoon; the loop tested within limits. The physician clarified cefazolin dosing after dialysis. The transplant program postponed the evaluation by two weeks. The access referral stayed on the tracker with a new date.

Documentation task. Write the shift debrief note: timeline, each portfolio's contribution, every notification (who, when), decisions and owners, and one system change to propose.
Second round · what changesAt the debrief, the manager proposes that the water liaison nurse sign machine releases herself 'to save time' when the technician is late.
Submit: A short written response explaining why technical release and clinical approval stay separate, and two alternatives that solve the delay.

Performance evidence for this module: Four-station OSCE passed (32 of 40, at least 7 per station, no critical error), a presented portfolio project, and a signed 30-60-90-day plan.

Critical error: Any OSCE critical error: delaying immediate care, continuing on an unreleased machine, destroying evidence, changing an order without authority, or misrepresenting project data.
Job aid · blank training formCapstone project summary (one page)
  1. Portfolio and project title; dates
  2. Problem and why it matters to patients
  3. Aim (what, for whom, how much, by when)
  4. Outcome, process and balancing measures with definitions
  5. Baseline median and run chart
  6. PDSA cycles: change, prediction, result, decision
  7. Result with counts and percentages; run-chart rule met or not
  8. Scope limits respected (what was not changed)
  9. What was learned, including what did not work
  10. Next steps and owner

Print it or copy it into your unit’s own form. Use it with fictional data here; real entries belong only in the facility’s approved records.

Portfolio measure. Project-specific outcome plus process and balancing measures; the assessment weighs the method and patient safety even if the small test of change did not improve the outcome.

Supervised practice

With your preceptor

OSCE stations use simulators and standardized colleagues; the project uses your unit's de-identified data with permission. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own protocols, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record.

  • Complete the four OSCE stations with a calibrated assessor and receive feedback.
  • Run your portfolio project for at least two PDSA cycles and present the run chart.
  • Write and sign your 30-60-90-day plan with the head nurse.
  • Lead a debrief after a real or simulated multi-problem shift.
  • Review your role charter at 90 days and record changes.

Terms

OSCE
An objective structured clinical examination: timed stations scored against observed criteria.
Critical error
An action or omission that would harm a patient, failing the station regardless of score.
Bounded project
An improvement project limited to one problem, one setting and changes within the nurse's scope.
