Nephrology Nursing specialty subtrack for peritoneal dialysis

This module is study material for registered nurses in adult peritoneal dialysis (PD). It explains the reasoning behind common nursing decisions; it is not a procedure. Exact steps follow your unit’s protocol and the device instructions; treatment parameters follow the written order. Nurses assess, verify, teach, document and escalate; prescription and medication decisions belong to the nephrologist. Every case is fictional. Pending clinical review by a PD nephrologist and a PD nurse educator.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Distinguish the roles of the Professional Regulation Commission (PRC) and Board of Nursing, the Department of Health (DOH) and its Health Facilities and Services Regulatory Bureau (HFSRB), Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth), and professional societies in a Philippine peritoneal dialysis (PD) program.
  • Support PhilHealth PD Z-benefit documentation within your scope, routing eligibility and payment decisions to authorized staff.
  • Calculate peritonitis and exit-site infection rates, months between episodes, percentage of patients peritonitis-free and culture-negative percentage, and compare them with International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD) targets.
  • Apply the 2026 ISPD framework for tracking loss from PD therapy.
  • Design a small, measurable improvement with a run chart and a balancing measure.

The big picture, in plain words

A PD program is more than a group of patients doing exchanges. It is licensed, contracted, staffed, audited and, ideally, improving. Your daily documentation is what lets the program prove eligibility, receive payment, meet standards and see its own performance.

Your work in this module is to keep records that are complete and honest, to know which authority governs which question, and to turn the numbers into one improvement at a time. Most programs do not need a grand quality plan. They need one nurse who counts carefully, looks behind each number and changes one thing.

In plain wordsThink of the program as a jeepney route with a franchise, a vehicle inspection and a fare matrix. The driver, the nurse, does the daily work, but the route survives only if the paperwork, the vehicle and the service all pass.

The picture has a limit. Patients are not passengers who can switch routes easily, and quality numbers are not just compliance. A peritonitis rate is a count of people who suffered. Treat it with that weight.

Opening case · fictional

Case: A year of numbers

The PD unit at a provincial hospital in Nueva Ecija has 32 patients. Nurse coordinator Mylene reviews last year’s records before the annual program meeting.

She counts 9 peritonitis episodes over 28.5 patient-years. Three were culture-negative. Twenty-four of the 32 patients had no peritonitis during the year. Exits from PD: 2 transplants, 4 transfers to hemodialysis and 3 deaths. The hospital director has asked for “one slide that says how we are doing.”

Before reading on, decide:

  • Is the unit within the ISPD targets?
  • Which indicator needs work first?
  • What would you change, and how would you know it did not cause harm?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Benefit amounts and coverage change

Press reports in 2024 described a PD First Z-benefit package of ₱270,000 per year covering solutions, accessories and 90 to 120 exchanges a month depending on the prescription. These are press reports, not the circular. Confirm the current amount, inclusions and annexes with the PhilHealth Regional Office before counseling a patient.

Scope

You help patients and the team assemble documentation, check chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 5 and PhilHealth Dialysis Database status as instructed, and record initiation, follow-up, infection episodes, and exit or transfer. Eligibility decisions and payment decisions remain with authorized personnel.

Example: the question at the counter

A patient’s daughter asks Nurse Mylene whether PhilHealth will “cover everything” for her father’s PD. Mylene does not quote a figure she read online. She explains, plainly, that the program is contracted for the PD benefit, that the benefits officer checks each patient’s eligibility and what is included, and that she will help assemble the documents. She walks the daughter to the benefits officer and records the referral. The daughter leaves with an appointment, not a guess.

Example: the hemodialysis ratio that does not apply

During an internal audit, a new administrator asks why the PD unit has one nurse for 18 patients when hemodialysis units staff far more tightly. The coordinator shows the self-assessment tool’s stated ratio for PD and explains that PD nursing is training, follow-up and triage, not chairside care. Importing a hemodialysis ratio into PD, in either direction, misreads the work.

RememberKnow which authority answers which question. Route eligibility and payment to the people authorized to decide.
Lesson · part 2

Measuring infection

Peritonitis rate (episodes per year at risk) = number of episodes ÷ total patient-years at risk.

Patient-years at risk = the sum of each patient’s days on PD in the period ÷ 365.25. Count time from the start of PD training or treatment. Report pre-PD peritonitis (after insertion but before PD starts) separately, per the 2022 definitions. Count relapsing peritonitis as one episode, per ISPD counting conventions.4

Months between episodes = 12 ÷ rate.

Percentage of patients peritonitis-free = patients with no episode in the period ÷ patients at risk in the period × 100.

Culture-negative percentage = culture-negative episodes ÷ all episodes × 100.

Exit-site infection rate = episodes ÷ patient-years at risk.

IndicatorISPD target
Peritonitis rate≤ 0.40 episodes per year at risk (2022)
Patients peritonitis-free per year> 80% (2022)
Culture-negative peritonitis< 15% of episodes (2022, GRADE 1C)
Exit-site infection rate≤ 0.40 episodes per year at risk (2023)

Worked example: patient-years when patients come and go

PatientDays on PD this year

1,004 ÷ 365.25 = about 2.75 patient-years at risk. Four patients, but not four years. Counting heads instead of time is the most common error in small units, and it makes the rate look better than it is.

Worked calculations

  • 6 episodes over 20 patient-years: 6 ÷ 20 = 0.30 episodes per patient-year; 12 ÷ 0.30 = 40 months between episodes. Below the 0.40 target.
  • 4 episodes over 8 patient-years: 4 ÷ 8 = 0.50 per patient-year; 12 ÷ 0.50 = 24 months between episodes. Above target.
  • 3 of 14 episodes culture-negative: 3 ÷ 14 × 100 = 21%. Above the 15% target; review sampling and laboratory methods.

Small numbers mislead

A unit with 25 patients may see its rate jump from 0.20 to 0.60 because of two extra episodes. Report confidence intervals where possible, look at trends over 12 months, and read each episode’s root cause. Do not change a whole program because of one bad quarter, and do not celebrate one good one.

International comparison

In the Peritoneal Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (PDOPPS), crude peritonitis rates were 0.28 episodes per patient-year overall, ranging from 0.26 in the United States to 0.40 in Thailand, and 10% of facilities exceeded 0.50. These are observational data from seven countries, not including the Philippines.5

RememberCount time, not heads. Then read the episodes behind the rate.
Lesson · part 3

Tracking loss from PD

The 2026 ISPD position statement notes that nearly half of people who start PD are no longer on it at two years. The most common causes are transfer to hemodialysis, death and kidney transplantation. Others include recovery of kidney function, transfer to another dialysis program and loss to follow-up. The statement replaces the inconsistently defined term “technique failure” with standardized definitions of causes of loss, recommendations on measurement, analysis and reporting, and key performance indicators for benchmarking and improvement.6

For your program: record the date and cause of every exit using the agreed definitions. Distinguish clinically different outcomes; transplant is a success, while death and hemodialysis transfer need review. Review hemodialysis transfers for preventable causes such as peritonitis, catheter dysfunction, inadequate fluid removal, burnout or caregiver loss.

Example: one word hid four stories

A unit’s registry listed every hemodialysis transfer last year as “technique failure.” When the coordinator reopened the four charts, she found four different causes: refractory peritonitis after a wet contamination that was not reported; catheter migration that was never repositioned; fluid overload in a patient whose urine had stopped; and a patient whose only caregiver went abroad. Each points to a different fix: reporting culture, access support, earlier membrane review, and assisted PD planning. None was visible under the old label.

ExitReview question
TransplantCelebrate; was the catheter plan smooth?
Transfer to hemodialysisWhich cause? Was it predictable and planned?
DeathExpected or unexpected? Any PD-related contribution?
Transfer to another programWas the handover complete?
Loss to follow-upWhat failed: contact, cost, transport?
RememberRecord the specific cause of every exit. A vague label hides the fix.
Lesson · part 4

Improving one thing at a time

  1. Pick a problem with data. For example, a culture-negative rate of 21%, or a median time from cloudy-fluid call to first antibiotic dose of 9 hours.
  2. Write an aim. “Reduce median call-to-antibiotic time from 9 hours to under 4 hours within 6 months.”
  3. Choose measures. Outcome (peritonitis cure without catheter loss), process (time stamps from call to sample to dose), balancing (after-hours staff workload, unnecessary emergency visits).
  4. Test a change. A one-page cloudy-bag pathway, a pre-labeled sample kit at home, an after-hours phone tree.
  5. Plot a run chart. Monthly values, a median line, and the date of each change.
  6. Review and adapt.

The direction is encouraging, but the numbers are small: nine calls in six months. The team keeps plotting for another six months before declaring success, and checks the balancing measure: after-hours calls to the on-call nurse rose slightly, and no unnecessary emergency visits were recorded. A single month at 3.5 hours is a hope, not a result.

RememberChange one thing, measure it monthly, and watch a balancing measure so the fix does not create a new problem.
Case review

Back to the case: A year of numbers

Mylene’s calculations. Peritonitis rate: 9 ÷ 28.5 = 0.32 episodes per patient-year, about 38 months between episodes, under the 0.40 target. Peritonitis-free: 24 ÷ 32 = 75%, under the >80% target. Culture-negative: 3 ÷ 9 = 33%, well above the 15% target.

Which indicator first. The culture-negative rate. It is furthest from target, and it is the indicator nurses control most directly.

Mylene’s plan. She reviews the three culture-negative episodes: two had antibiotics before sampling after emergency department visits, and one sample sat for 14 hours before reaching the laboratory. Her aim: culture-negative below 15% within 12 months. The change: an emergency-department alert card carried by every patient (“Send PD effluent for cell count, Gram stain and culture before antibiotics when feasible; call the PD unit”) and a courier arrangement with the laboratory.

How she will know it did no harm. Her balancing measure is time to first antibiotic dose, so that sampling never delays treatment. She plots both monthly.

The exits. She also reviews the four hemodialysis transfers using the 2026 definitions: two after refractory peritonitis, one for inadequate fluid removal, one after caregiver loss. Her slide for the director shows the three infection indicators against target, the exits by specific cause, and the one change she is testing.

Terms in plain words

Peritoneal dialysis (PD)
Dialysis that uses the lining of the abdomen as the filter, with fluid run in and out through a catheter.
CKD
Chronic kidney disease.
Exit-site infection
Purulent discharge at the exit site, with or without redness (ISPD 2023).
Peritonitis
Infection or inflammation of the peritoneum, usually shown by cloudy effluent.
Culture-negative peritonitis
Peritonitis in which no organism grows from the effluent culture.
Relapsing peritonitis
A new episode within 4 weeks of finishing therapy, same organism or one sterile episode.
Patient-years at risk
The total time all patients spent on PD in the period, in years.
Run chart
A graph of a measure over time with a median line, used to see whether a change made a difference.
Balancing measure
A measure that checks whether a change has caused harm elsewhere.
