Study series for physicians on duty

This module is study material for physicians on duty in hemodialysis units. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol. At the chairside, follow your unit’s protocol and ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Doses and cut-off values are set by your unit. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Recognize the uremic syndrome and name the findings that need the attending nephrologist the same day.
  • Explain uremic toxins by size and protein binding, and predict which ones hemodialysis removes poorly.
  • State the urgent and planned reasons to start dialysis, and explain why current guidance ties the decision to symptoms and signs rather than to an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) alone.
  • Define residual kidney function, describe how it is measured, and list what protects it and what harms it.
  • Describe incremental hemodialysis, what its trials have and have not shown, and the safety checks it requires.
Pre-testBefore you read, answer the 8 self-assessment questions and note your score. After the module, select New set of questions and compare. The difference is what you learned; the questions you still miss are what to reread.
Start here

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsPicture a house where the garbage collection has stopped. The big bags by the door are easy to see and easy to count. But the smell that makes the house hard to live in comes from many small things that nobody sees. Dialysis is a truck that comes three times a week and takes the big bags well. Some of the small things it barely touches.

The picture has a limit. Garbage sits still, and uremic solutes do not. They act on many organs. The bag we count, urea, is only mildly toxic itself. It is a marker that rises along with hundreds of other retained solutes.1 And many new dialysis patients still have kidneys that collect some of the garbage every hour of the week.

Uremic toxinA retained solute that harms the body.

Protein-bound soluteA retained solute that travels attached to albumin.
Residual kidney function (RKF)The kidney function a patient still has after dialysis starts.
Residual urea clearance (Kru)The urea clearance of the patient’s own kidneys, from a timed urine collection.
Incremental hemodialysisStarting with fewer or shorter sessions while the kidneys still help, then stepping up as their help fades.

Most patients in your chairs are stable on their prescription. A few have uremia that is coming back, or kidney function that is quietly fading. Your work is to ask the uremia questions at every session, protect the kidney function that remains, and bring clear measurements to the attending nephrologist.

Opening case · fictional

Case: She still passes a liter a day

Aling Rosario Dimaculangan, 58, is a retired public-school teacher from Malolos. She has diabetic kidney disease and started hemodialysis 7 weeks ago on a twice-weekly schedule written by her attending nephrologist.

It is 1:40 p.m., the start of the Thursday afternoon shift, after a 3-day gap. Nurse Jomar Villanueva reports. Her pre-dialysis weight is 2.9 kilograms above her target. Her blood pressure is 168/94 mm Hg and her pulse is 92 per minute. She is not breathless at rest. Both ankles are faintly swollen.

Aling Rosario says she “still passes about a liter a day.” Her daughter disagrees. She says it has been less since a computed tomography (CT) scan with contrast at another hospital 10 days ago. Her mother has also been nauseated in the mornings and itchy at night. The last timed urine collection was 6 weeks ago.

Nurse Villanueva has checked the access and sent pre-dialysis blood for the monthly panel. She asks you, “Doc, should we add an extra session this week?”

Illustrative values only, not targets.

Before reading on, decide:

  • Which of her findings point to uremia, and which point to volume, and why does the difference matter?
  • What may have happened to her residual kidney function, and what information would show it?
  • Who decides whether her schedule changes, and what exactly will you report?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

What uremia looks like at the bedside

Module 2 covered water, salt, potassium, and acid. This section covers the rest of the load. Healthy kidneys clear it in two ways. Small, water-soluble solutes pass through the glomerulus in the filtrate. Many solutes that travel attached to albumin cannot be filtered. Instead, cells of the proximal tubule take them from the blood and pass them into the urine. This is tubular secretion.1

When both routes fail, solutes accumulate and act on many organs at once. So uremia looks like many unrelated complaints.1

OrganWhat goes wrongWhat you see or hear at the chair
Brain and nervesSolutes slow brain function and injure nervesFatigue, poor concentration, broken sleep, restless legs. Later, confusion and asterixis, a flapping tremor of the hands
GutSolutes act on the gut and the brain centers for appetitePoor appetite, morning nausea or vomiting, a metallic taste
SkinUremic itch, from several causes acting togetherScratch marks, poor sleep from night itch
BloodPlatelets stick and clump poorlyEasy bruising, bleeding gums, needle sites that keep oozing
SerosaThe linings of the heart and lungs become inflamedChest pain that changes with posture or breathing, a pericardial rub
Muscle and nutritionPoor intake and increased protein breakdownWeight loss, thin arms and temples, weakness

The features in this table follow a standard review and dialysis text.1,2 They overlap with depression, anemia, and the effects of medicines, so the diagnosis is clinical.

What the evidence says

The uremic syndrome is a clinical description, not a laboratory threshold. No single solute explains it, and urea causes few of the symptoms itself.1 A blood result supports your judgment but cannot replace your questions.

Lesson · part 2

Three kinds of cargo: small, middle, and protein-bound toxins

Picture three sizes of cargo at a gate. Small boxes pass easily. Large crates pass only through a wide gate, with a push. Boxes chained to a heavy trolley barely move at all. The gate is the dialyzer membrane, and the trolley is albumin.

The European Uremic Toxin Work Group screened 857 publications and cataloged 90 retained solutes. Of these, 68 weighed less than 500 daltons. The other 22 were middle molecules, and 12 of those were larger than 12,000 daltons. Twenty-five solutes were bound to protein, and most of them were small.3 A 2012 update reviewed 621 later articles and added 56 newly reported solutes.4

A 2021 consensus conference added two dimensions to size: how dialysis removes each solute, and its links to symptoms and outcomes. This is a consensus statement, not a graded recommendation.5

GroupExamplesHow healthy kidneys clear itHow conventional hemodialysis does
Small, water-solubleUrea, creatinine, potassiumFiltrationWell, by diffusion
Middle moleculesBeta-2 microglobulinFiltration, then uptake and breakdown in the tubulePoorly with a low-flux membrane. Better with a high-flux membrane and convection
Protein-boundIndoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfateTubular secretionPoorly with any membrane, because only the free fraction crosses

Using the terms from Module 5: small solutes diffuse fast. Middle molecules diffuse slowly, so high-flux membranes and convection help. Module 7 covers the hemodiafiltration trials.

Protein-bound solutes are different. Most of each molecule sits on albumin, which stays behind the membrane. The healthy tubule works around this: transporters pull the solute off albumin and secrete it into the urine.1 Several of these solutes, such as indoxyl sulfate, come from the breakdown of dietary protein by gut bacteria.1

What the evidence does and does not show

Poor removal of protein-bound solutes by dialysis is established physiology. Their tubular secretion is one reason residual kidney function may be valuable. Whether removing more of them improves how patients feel or how long they live is not proven. Treat that link as a mechanistic hypothesis.5

What you do

Kt/V, the urea-based dose measure in Module 7, is a surrogate. It tracks urea, not the whole retained load. A patient with a good Kt/V and new uremic symptoms is still reported.

RememberUrea is the messenger, not the whole message. A good urea number does not rule out uremia.
Lesson · part 3

When dialysis begins: symptoms, not a single number

Starting dialysis is like fixing a leaking roof. You do not wait for the ceiling to fall, and you do not replace the roof because a gauge crossed an arbitrary line.

Why one number cannot mark the day

As nephrons are lost, the survivors compensate. Each filters more and excretes more potassium, phosphate, and acid. For a time the blood stays close to normal. Then the adaptations fail, at different levels of kidney function in different people. So the decision rests on the patient, not on a glomerular filtration rate (GFR).

The evidence: the IDEAL trial

The IDEAL (Initiating Dialysis Early And Late) trial randomized 828 adults with progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD) at 32 centers in Australia and New Zealand. One group was planned to start dialysis at an estimated GFR of 10 to 14 mL per minute (early start), the other at 5 to 7 (late start). GFR was estimated by the Cockcroft-Gault equation.6

Over a median of 3.59 years, 37.6% of the early group and 36.6% of the late group died. The hazard ratio for early start was 1.04 (95% confidence interval 0.83 to 1.30).6

One detail changes how you read the result. In the late group, 75.9% started above the target GFR because symptoms developed.6 So the trial shows that waiting under close follow-up, and starting when symptoms appear, is safe. It does not show that waiting for a GFR of 5 to 7 in a patient with symptoms is safe.

What the guidelines say

Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2024 Practice Point 5.4.1 says to initiate dialysis based on a composite assessment of a person’s symptoms, signs, quality of life, preferences, level of GFR, and laboratory abnormalities. Practice Point 5.4.2 says to start when one or more of these is present: symptoms or signs of kidney failure, such as uremic neurologic signs, pericarditis, anorexia, acid-base or electrolyte problems that resist medicines, or intractable itch; volume or blood pressure that cannot be controlled; and nutritional decline that resists dietary care, or cognitive impairment. It notes that this often, but not invariably, occurs at a GFR between 5 and 10 mL per minute per 1.73 m². Both are practice points, which KDIGO does not grade.7

The Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) 2015 Guideline 1.2, which is not graded, bases the decision primarily on signs and symptoms of uremia, protein-energy wasting, and whether metabolic problems and volume overload can still be managed medically.8 A KDIGO Controversies Conference called for starts that reflect each patient’s goals.9 None sets a GFR at which dialysis must begin.

What you do

A referred patient may arrive with a creatinine value and a note that says “start dialysis today.” Find out which indication is present and how urgent it is. Record the symptoms and signs, and call the attending nephrologist with them. The decision and the prescription belong to the nephrologist. The starting dialysis planner gives a structured list of indications and preparations to bring to that call.

Lesson · part 4

Residual kidney function: the small helper that works all week

Think of a small helper who works all 168 hours of the week while the big machine works about twelve. The helper is slow, but never stops.

What the remaining nephrons do

Residual kidney function (RKF) is the work the patient’s own kidneys still do. The surviving nephrons filter small solutes and water. They secrete protein-bound solutes, which dialysis removes poorly. They excrete salt, water, potassium, and phosphate between sessions. They also keep some of their hormone work, including erythropoietin and activated vitamin D.1,2

This function declines after dialysis starts, and fastest at first. In 42 patients from a pilot trial in the United States, kidney urea clearance fell by a median of 32% by week 6 and by about 61% by week 48. Urine volume fell more steadily, by a median of 47% by week 48. Urine volume correlated only moderately with urea clearance.10

What the evidence shows

These studies are observational. Each is open to the same confounder: patients who keep their kidney function are often healthier in other ways.

StudyDesign and sizeFinding
Obi and colleagues, 2016Cohort of 6,538 patients starting hemodialysis in the United StatesFaster first-year loss of residual urea clearance was associated with higher mortality. A decline of 6.0 versus 1.5 mL per minute per 1.73 m² per year carried an adjusted hazard ratio of 2.00 (1.55 to 2.59)11
Choices for Healthy Outcomes in Caring for End-Stage Renal Disease (CHOICE) Study, 2010Prospective cohort of 734 patients starting hemodialysis in 81 clinicsUrine output at year 1 was associated with lower all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 0.70, 0.52 to 0.93) and with lower erythropoietin doses.12
CANUSA (Canada-USA) reanalysis, 2001601 patients on peritoneal dialysisEach additional 250 mL of daily urine was associated with a 36% lower relative risk of death. Peritoneal clearance was not associated with survival13

None of these studies shows that preserving residual function prolongs life. The authors of the largest cohort said the benefit of preservation strategies on mortality still has to be determined.11

How residual function is measured

Urine volume is easy to record but tracks clearance loosely. The better measure is residual urea clearance (Kru), calculated from a timed collection of every void between two sessions. It uses the urine urea nitrogen, the urine volume, the collection time, and the mean of the post-dialysis and next pre-dialysis blood urea nitrogen (BUN).2

KDOQI 2015 Guideline 3.2, which is not graded, says the dose of hemodialysis may be reduced in a patient with significant residual function, provided that function is measured periodically.8

Worked example, illustrative numbers. Residual urea clearance from a timed interdialytic urine collection.2
Kru (mL per minute) = [urine urea nitrogen (mg/dL) × urine volume (mL)] ÷ [mean BUN (mg/dL) × collection time (minutes)]
Mean BUN = (post-dialysis BUN + next pre-dialysis BUN) ÷ 2
Collection over a 44-hour interval: 44 × 60 = 2,640 minutes. Urine volume 1,320 mL. Urine urea nitrogen 380 mg/dL.
Post-dialysis BUN 22 mg/dL, next pre-dialysis BUN 62 mg/dL. Mean BUN = (22 + 62) ÷ 2 = 42 mg/dL.
Kru = (380 × 1,320) ÷ (42 × 2,640) = 501,600 ÷ 110,880 = about 4.5 mL per minute.
What it does and does not tell you. A missed void lowers the result falsely. A mistimed blood sample changes it as much as the urine does. It informs the nephrologist’s prescription and does not set it.

The measured creatinine clearance calculator uses the same timed-urine arithmetic for creatinine.

What you do

Treat urine volume as a vital sign of the kidney. Ask for it at every session, write the patient’s estimate as a trend, and report a fall. The urine output and fluid balance tool helps you track it.

Arithmetic illustration, not a target. Urine as a vital sign.
A patient who passes 1,000 mL a day removes about 1,000 × 3 = 3,000 mL over a 3-day gap.
If urine falls to 300 mL a day, the same gap removes about 300 × 3 = 900 mL.
The difference, about 3,000 − 900 = 2,100 mL, now stays in the body and adds to the next session’s fluid goal.
What it does and does not tell you. It explains why weight gains rise as urine falls, not what fluid goal is safe. That belongs to the prescription (Module 8).
RememberRecord urine volume at every session as a trend. A falling trend is a finding to report, not a detail.
Lesson · part 5

What protects and what harms residual function

The helper is fragile. Low pressure, poisons, and dehydration all slow him down, and some injuries are permanent.

The surviving nephrons depend on blood flow, and there are few spares. Low pressure during dialysis, aggressive fluid removal, and vomiting or diarrhea all reduce that flow. Drugs that injure the tubule or narrow the arterioles add damage, and so does obstruction or infection of the urinary tract.

FactorWhy it mattersStrength of evidence
Low blood pressure during dialysis, aggressive fluid removalLess blood flow to the remaining nephronsInference from physiology. Module 13 covers prevention
Volume depletion from vomiting, diarrhea, or a target weight set too lowLess blood flow to the remaining nephronsInference from physiology
Iodinated contrastNarrowing of kidney vessels and direct injury to tubular cellsInference. The size of the risk in this group is not well measured
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)They block the prostaglandins that keep the arterioles of a struggling kidney openEstablished physiology, inference for this group
Aminoglycoside antibioticsDirect injury to tubular cellsEstablished physiology, inference for this group
Hemodialysis rather than peritoneal dialysisMechanism not establishedAssociation14
Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor or calcium channel blocker useMechanism not establishedAssociation with less loss. A hypothesis, not proof of protection14

What the evidence shows

Moist and colleagues studied a national random sample of new dialysis patients in the United States. Loss was defined as a urine volume below 200 mL in 24 hours at about one year. Loss was more likely with diabetes (adjusted odds ratio 1.82), heart failure (1.32), and female sex (1.45). Patients on peritoneal dialysis were less likely to lose function than those on hemodialysis (0.35). Use of an ACE inhibitor (0.68) or a calcium channel blocker (0.77) was associated with less loss.14

The authors called these findings hypotheses. Patients who received these drugs may have differed in other ways, so the study does not show that either drug protects the kidney. The harms in the table are inference from physiology, not trial results.

What you do

Before you agree to iodinated contrast, a nonsteroidal drug, or an aminoglycoside for a patient who still passes urine, ask whether the attending nephrologist wants an alternative. Report recurrent low pressure (Module 13) in such a patient, because each episode may cost kidney function as well as comfort.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist when a patient reports or shows a fall in urine output, or when a contrast study, a nephrotoxic drug, or surgery is planned for a patient who still passes urine. Do not change the prescription. Bring the dates, the urine volumes, and the planned procedure.
RememberA patient who still passes urine has something to lose. Protect it before every scan, every new medicine, and every session.
Lesson · part 6

Incremental dialysis: starting with less, then stepping up

Think of a dimmer switch rather than an on-off switch. Dialysis starts low while the kidneys still help, and it is turned up as their help fades.

The physiology is addition. The total clearance a patient receives is kidney clearance plus dialysis clearance. When residual urea clearance is substantial, fewer sessions can reach a similar total. The plan is safe only if residual function is measured and the prescription steps up when it falls.

The trials are small. None of the completed trials had a clinical primary outcome such as death or hospital admission. TwoPlus does. The completed trials show feasibility and suggest reasonable safety in selected patients watched closely. They disagree on whether residual function is protected. The British and Spanish trials found no difference. The two smaller trials suggest benefit, with wide uncertainty.

The observational data

Obi and colleagues compared 351 patients who started twice weekly with 8,068 matched patients who started three times weekly. The incremental group kept 16% more residual urea clearance. Incremental starts were associated with higher mortality only in patients whose baseline clearance was 3.0 mL per minute per 1.73 m² or less (hazard ratio 1.61, 1.07 to 2.44). Above that level there was no difference (0.99, 0.76 to 1.28).20 Doctors chose who started twice weekly, and all had survived the first year, so bias is likely.

A meta-analysis of 22 cohort studies of incremental hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis (75,292 participants) found no clear mortality difference (risk ratio 1.14, 0.85 to 1.52), with wide disagreement between studies (heterogeneity 86%). Full-dose dialysis began after about 12 months on average.21

What the guidelines say

KDOQI Guideline 3.2 permits a reduced dose with measured residual function, as above.8 The KDIGO Controversies Conference called for better evidence on individualized approaches.9 Neither sets a threshold.

Decision walk-through. The opening case against four entry rules.
Vilar 2022: urea clearance 3 mL per minute per 1.73 m² or more.16
Murea 2022: urine 500 mL a day or more and estimated GFR 5 or more.17
Fernández Lucas 2025: kidney urea clearance 2.5 mL per minute or more.15
TwoPlus: kidney urea clearance 3.5 mL per minute per 1.73 m² or more and urine 500 mL in 24 hours or more.19
Aling Rosario’s last clearance is 6 weeks old, and her urine may have fallen since the scan. You cannot say which rule she would meet today.
What this does and does not tell you. These are study eligibility rules, not unit thresholds, and none is a validated cut-off. The nephrologist and your unit’s protocol set the criteria. You bring current measurements.

Figure 8 · Stepping up from fewer sessions to full dialysis

  1. StartThe attending nephrologist orders fewer sessions because measured residual function is substantial.
  2. MeasureUrine volume every session. Timed collections as ordered.
  3. Watch after the long gapPotassium, fluid gain, blood pressure, bicarbonate, and uremic symptoms.
  4. Recognize a triggerA fall in urine or clearance, rising weight gains, high potassium, low bicarbonate, or new symptoms.

What you do

A patient on a twice-weekly schedule is not missing a session. Watch closely after the long gap. The trial in the United Kingdom found lower bicarbonate with incremental starts.16 Report any fall in urine, rising weight gains, or new uremic symptoms. Each may prompt a step up.

RememberIncremental dialysis is a plan that depends on measured kidney function. When the urine falls, the plan must change, and the nephrologist changes it.

Putting it together at the chair

Three questions cover every patient. They take less than a minute once they become habit.

Figure 9 · Three questions at every chair

Is this uremia?Ask the five uremia questions. Compare the answers with the patient’s own pattern.
Is there kidney function to protect?Ask about urine volume. Check for planned contrast, new drugs, or recent low pressure.
Is the schedule still right?Check the order, the weight gain after the long gap, and the date of the last timed collection.

The timed collection

A timed collection is only as good as its handling. At the start time the patient empties the bladder and discards that urine, then keeps every void until the stop time. The two blood samples must bracket the collection: one at the end of the previous session, one at the start of the next. Follow your unit’s protocol and the skills checklist below.

When you call, use the situation, background, assessment, and recommendation (SBAR) format from Module 1. Include the schedule, the last clearance and its date, and recent events.

RememberBring measurements, not impressions: a urine volume, a weight trend, and the date of the last collection.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The good Kt/V and the itch

What you see
Mang Danilo, 61, has dialyzed three times a week for four years. His monthly Kt/V is above the unit’s target. He tells you he scratches all night, has lost his appetite, and feels sick each morning.
What is likely going on
These are uremic symptoms. A good urea-based dose does not show that middle and protein-bound solutes are controlled, and his own kidneys stopped helping long ago.
What to do
Ask the five uremia questions in full and examine for a rub and asterixis. Check that his sessions have been delivered in full. Report the symptoms to the attending nephrologist the same day. Do not change the prescription yourself.
Why it matters
Kt/V is a surrogate. A patient can meet the target and still feel uremic.

The referral that says “start today”

What you see
A man of 70 arrives from a provincial hospital with a creatinine result and a note asking for dialysis today. He walks in unaided, eats well, and has no edema. His potassium from yesterday is normal.
What is likely going on
No urgent indication is evident. The request rests on a laboratory value, not on symptoms or signs.
What to do
Examine him, record the symptoms and signs you looked for, and call the attending nephrologist. Report clearly that you found no urgent indication, and let the nephrologist decide on the timing and plan.
Why it matters
Dialysis is started for symptoms and signs. A careful record protects the patient from both a rushed start and a missed one.

The rub before connection

What you see
Aling Corazon, 66, missed two sessions during a typhoon. Before connection she has chest pain that eases when she sits forward, and you hear a scratchy sound over the heart. Her pressure is normal.
What is likely going on
Uremic pericarditis is likely after missed dialysis. It may change the anticoagulation plan for the session, and an effusion can follow.
What to do
Do not connect until you have called the attending nephrologist. Follow your unit’s protocol. Recheck the pressure often and watch for distended neck veins and falling pressure, which need emergency action.
Why it matters
A rub is a same-day call. With a falling pressure it becomes an emergency.

Less urine after the scan

What you see
A woman on twice-weekly dialysis says her urine has dropped by half since an abdominal CT scan with contrast last week. Her weight gain is the largest it has been.
What is likely going on
Her residual function may have fallen after the contrast. Her twice-weekly schedule depended on that function.
What to do
Ask for a measured urine volume, check the date of her last timed collection, and call the attending nephrologist today. Report the scan, the urine change, and the weight gain. The schedule and today’s fluid goal are the nephrologist’s decision.
Why it matters
An incremental schedule is safe only while the kidney function it relies on is still there.

The pain reliever from the pharmacy

What you see
A young man who still passes about a liter a day mentions that he has been taking an over-the-counter pain reliever for a sprained ankle for a week.
What is likely going on
He may be taking a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), which can reduce blood flow to his remaining nephrons.
What to do
Ask him to show you the box and check the generic name. Tell the attending nephrologist, who can advise on a safer alternative. Record it in his medication list.
Why it matters
Residual function is easy to lose and hard to regain. One question can protect it.
Field notes
  • Ask “How much urine do you pass in a day?” at every session and write the answer, even when it is “none.”
  • Ask the family about thinking and sleep. They notice slowing before the patient does.
  • Check the date of the last timed urine collection whenever a patient on an incremental schedule has a larger weight gain.
  • When a patient mentions a scan, a new antibiotic, or a pain reliever, ask whether the attending nephrologist knows.
  • Read a twice-weekly schedule as a deliberate plan, not a missed session, until the order says otherwise.
Case review

Back to the case: She still passes a liter a day

Uremia or volume? Two findings point to uremia: morning nausea and night itch. Both are new. Three findings point to volume: a weight 2.9 kilograms above target after the long gap, a blood pressure of 168/94 mm Hg, and ankle edema. She is not breathless at rest. The two problems have different fixes. Extra fluid removal treats volume but does not replace the protein-bound and middle-sized solutes her kidneys may have been clearing. You ask the five questions in full, examine for a rub and asterixis, and find neither.

What happened to her residual function? Her story fits a fall in residual function after the contrast scan. The timing fits, and less urine, new symptoms, and a larger weight gain are what lost kidney work would produce. Diet, medicines, or another cause of nausea remain possible. Three pieces of information would settle it: a measured urine volume, a repeat timed collection to compare with 6 weeks ago, and today’s potassium and bicarbonate. Ask too whether anyone gave her an NSAID or other new medicine at the other hospital.

Who decides, and what do you report? The attending nephrologist decides whether her schedule changes. An extra session and today’s fluid goal are both part of the prescription. You call before connection. In SBAR form: Aling Rosario, 7 weeks on twice-weekly hemodialysis, is 2.9 kilograms above target with ankle edema and new nausea and itch. Her family reports less urine since a contrast CT scan 10 days ago, and her last timed collection was 6 weeks ago. You request a review of her schedule and an order for a repeat timed collection, and you ask how the nephrologist wants today’s fluid goal handled. You record the advice and the time, and tell Nurse Villanueva and the family the plan.

Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 8 questions at random from this module’s 15-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 7 of 8 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 15Why is urea described as a marker of uremia rather than its main cause?
Urea is easy to measure and moves with the retained load, but it causes few of the symptoms itself.
Question 2 of 15A man has a Kt/V above target but reports new night itch, poor appetite, and morning nausea. What is the best interpretation?
Kt/V is a surrogate that tracks urea, not middle or protein-bound solutes.
Question 3 of 15Why does conventional hemodialysis remove indoxyl sulfate poorly?
The healthy tubule secretes protein-bound solutes. Dialysis cannot.
Question 4 of 15What did the European Uremic Toxin Work Group find about the 90 retained solutes it cataloged?
Of 90 solutes, 68 were under 500 daltons, 22 were middle molecules, and 25 were protein-bound.
Question 5 of 15What did the IDEAL trial show?
Deaths were 37.6% with early start and 36.6% with late start.
Question 6 of 15Why must the IDEAL result be read with care?
It tested waiting with close follow-up, not waiting in a symptomatic patient.
Question 7 of 15A referred woman with an estimated GFR of 9 has a pericardial rub, vomiting, and asterixis. What is the best next step?
A pericardial rub and asterixis are urgent uremic indications whatever the GFR, and the vomiting adds to them.
Question 8 of 15How does KDIGO 2024 grade its practice points on when to start dialysis?
Practice points are ungraded. Do not borrow another system’s grades.
Question 9 of 15A resident calculates an estimated GFR from the creatinine of a patient on dialysis for a year. What is wrong?
Dialysis removes creatinine, so the blood level no longer reflects kidney function.
Question 10 of 15What did the CHOICE cohort find about urine output at one year on hemodialysis?
The hazard ratio was 0.70 in an observational cohort, so confounding is possible.
Question 11 of 15A timed collection gives a much lower clearance than before. The patient forgot to collect two voids. What is the best interpretation?
An incomplete collection lowers the calculated clearance. Record it honestly.
Question 12 of 15A man who passes a liter of urine a day is scheduled for a non-urgent contrast CT scan. What should you do?
Residual function is worth protecting, and the decision belongs to the nephrologist.
Question 13 of 15What did Moist and colleagues find about ACE inhibitor use and loss of residual function?
The adjusted odds ratio was 0.68 in an observational cohort, which does not prove protection.
Question 14 of 15A woman on twice-weekly dialysis has rising weight gains and says her urine has fallen. What do you do?
Rising gains and falling urine are triggers to step up. The schedule and the fluid goal belong to the nephrologist.
Question 15 of 15What have the completed randomized trials of incremental dialysis shown so far?
The British and Spanish trials found no protection. Two smaller trials suggested benefit. TwoPlus is in progress.

Terms in plain words

Uremic syndrome
The illness caused by retained solutes in kidney failure, affecting the brain, gut, skin, blood, serosa, and muscle.
Uremic retention solute
A substance that builds up in the blood when the kidneys fail. It is called a uremic toxin when it harms the body.
Middle molecule
A retained solute larger than about 500 daltons. High-flux membranes and convection remove it better than low-flux membranes.
Protein-bound solute
A retained solute that travels attached to albumin. The kidney secretes it, and dialysis removes it poorly.
Tubular secretion
Active transfer of a substance from the blood into the urine by the cells of the kidney tubule.
Asterixis
A flapping tremor of the outstretched hands, seen in uremic and other metabolic encephalopathies.
Residual kidney function
The kidney function a patient still has after dialysis starts.
Residual urea clearance (Kru)
The volume of blood the kidneys clear of urea each minute, calculated from a timed interdialytic urine collection.
Incremental hemodialysis
Starting with fewer or shorter sessions while residual function is substantial, then increasing as it falls.
Practice point
A KDIGO statement of expert guidance that is not graded for strength or evidence.
