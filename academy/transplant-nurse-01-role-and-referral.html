Specialty subtrack for registered nurses

This subtrack prepares Philippine registered nurses for supervised kidney transplant ward and clinic work. It teaches assessment, communication and escalation within nursing scope. Candidacy, listing, organ allocation, compatibility decisions, dose changes and benefit approval belong to the authorized transplant team, the histocompatibility laboratory, the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) and the facility. All cases are synthetic.

Learning objectives · about 2 hours · week 1

After this module you should be able to

  • Explain, in physiologic terms, why transplant usually outperforms dialysis and why the first weeks carry extra risk.
  • State when a potentially suitable patient should be referred and why waiting for dialysis to start loses options.
  • Describe what each member of the transplant team decides and where the nurse's authority begins and ends.
  • Compose a complete Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (SBAR) referral and close the loop with a named owner and a follow-up date.
  • Identify the practical barriers (distance, work, cost, family expectations) that most often stall a referral.
Intuition

A relay race, not a referral slip

Think of the transplant pathway as a relay race with many runners: the clinic nurse, the referring physician, the transplant coordinator, the surgeon, the laboratory, the donor team, the benefits office, and the patient and family. A baton dropped between any two runners costs months. Nobody loses the race by running slowly. They lose it in the handoffs, when each runner assumes the next one already has the baton.

The picture has a limit. In a real relay every runner can see the baton. In transplant care the baton is often invisible: a referral letter sitting unread, a laboratory result filed without review, a patient who stopped answering because the bus fare to the center is half a week's income. Your job on this pathway is to keep the baton visible. You do that by writing referrals that carry everything the next person needs, by confirming that someone actually received them, and by writing down who owns the next step and when you will check.

In plain wordsThe nurse rarely decides who gets a kidney. The nurse very often decides whether the patient's file keeps moving.

Throughout this subtrack you will work alongside the same small team. Nurse Carla is your preceptor, a transplant nurse with many years on the ward and in the clinic. Joy is the recipient coordinator. Dr. Santos is the transplant nephrologist. The patients change from module to module. All of them, staff and patients, are fictional, and their stories are built from situations transplant nurses meet every week.

Physiology and evidence

Why transplant, and why the clock starts early

What dialysis replaces, and what it does not. A healthy kidney never stops working. It filters about 180 liters of plasma a day. It adjusts sodium, potassium, acid and water minute by minute. It makes erythropoietin and active vitamin D. It clears middle-sized uremic molecules that dialysis membranes remove poorly.

Thrice-weekly hemodialysis squeezes a week of that work into about 12 hours. Between sessions, water, potassium and acid build up. During sessions they are pulled off quickly. The heart and vessels take the swings, which is why left ventricular hypertrophy, vascular calcification and sudden cardiac death are the main causes of death on dialysis. A working transplant restores continuous filtration and the kidney's hormones, so many of those stresses simply stop.

The survival curve has a dip before it rises. In a national cohort of more than 228,000 people on dialysis in the United States, those who received a first deceased-donor transplant had a higher risk of death in the first two weeks than comparable patients still waiting on the list (relative risk about 2.8), the cost of major surgery and induction immunosuppression. The curves crossed within months. By 18 months the relative risk had fallen to 0.32, and long-term mortality among recipients was 48 to 82 percent lower than among wait-listed patients who stayed on dialysis: about 3.8 deaths per 100 patient-years after transplant, against 6.3 per 100 patient-years on the waiting list.1 A later systematic review of observational studies found the same direction for mortality, cardiovascular events and quality of life across most subgroups.2 These are observational data, so selection still matters; but because the comparison group was already judged fit enough to be listed, much of the healthy-candidate bias is removed.

RememberThe first weeks after transplant are the riskiest weeks; the years after are the reward. That early dip is why careful evaluation, prepared families and alert postoperative nursing matter so much.

Why referral is a chronic kidney disease (CKD)-clinic task, not a dialysis-unit task. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2020 candidate guideline recommends that every patient with chronic kidney disease (CKD) G4 to G5 who is expected to reach kidney failure be informed about, educated about and considered for transplant, regardless of socioeconomic status, sex, gender identity or ethnicity (1D: a strong recommendation built on very low-certainty evidence). It advises referral for evaluation at least 6 to 12 months before dialysis is anticipated, so that a preemptive transplant (one done before dialysis starts) remains possible, and referral of patients already on dialysis once they are medically stable; both of these are ungraded statements. It recommends a preemptive transplant from a living donor as the preferred treatment for eligible patients (1A).3 Evaluation takes months: cardiac testing, cancer screening, dental and infection clearance, donor work-up, and paperwork. A referral made on the day dialysis starts guarantees the patient a period of dialysis that might have been avoided.

In practice, start thinking about referral when the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is falling through the low 20s, or when a validated risk tool predicts a meaningful chance of kidney failure within a year or two. Age alone, diabetes alone, or being on dialysis for a long time do not exclude a patient; the team weighs the whole person.

In the CKD clinic · fictional

Nurse Carla, your preceptor, is reviewing the morning list with you. Mrs. Abad, 61, has an eGFR of 22, down from 31 a year ago. Her chart says "transplant: not discussed." You assume it is too early, since she is nowhere near dialysis.

Carla shakes her head. "Evaluation here takes six months on a good day. Count backward from when she will need dialysis, not forward from today." She asks you to raise transplant at this visit and to flag the chart for the nephrologist.

What this shows. The referral clock runs backward from the expected start of dialysis. The nurse who notices the trend is often the one who starts it.

Evidence

Time on dialysis is a risk you can shorten

Most risk factors for a poor transplant result belong to the patient or the donor. Age, diabetes and the quality of the donor kidney cannot be changed by the time the patient reaches the clinic. Time spent on dialysis before transplant is different. The system controls it, and the referral is where it starts.

Why time on dialysis might matter. Each year on dialysis adds to the cardiovascular load described above. The left ventricle thickens. Arteries calcify. Nutrition and muscle mass fall. Infections and access procedures accumulate. A recipient who arrives at surgery after years of this brings a more worn body to the operation and to the first year of immunosuppression. This is a plausible mechanism, not a proven one, so look at what the data show.

What the registry data show. Meier-Kriesche and Kaplan used a clever design. They studied pairs of kidneys from the same deceased donor, where one kidney went to a recipient who had been on dialysis less than 6 months and the other to a recipient who had been on dialysis more than 2 years. Because both kidneys came from the same donor, donor quality was largely held constant. At 10 years, unadjusted graft survival was 63 percent in the short-wait recipients and 29 percent in the long-wait recipients. Across all deceased-donor transplants in the database, adjusted 10-year graft survival was 69 percent for preemptive transplants and 39 percent after more than 24 months of dialysis.4

A second United States registry analysis of 38,836 first transplants found that, compared with transplant after starting dialysis, a preemptive transplant was associated with a lower risk of graft failure (relative risk 0.75 for deceased-donor and 0.73 for living-donor kidneys) and of death (0.84 and 0.69).5

Both studies are observational, from the 1980s and 1990s, and from another health system. Patients who get a preemptive transplant may differ in ways the analysis could not fully adjust for, such as health literacy or support at home. So the honest summary is this: shorter time on dialysis is consistently associated with better graft and patient survival, and part of that association is probably causal. No randomized trial will ever settle it, because no one can ethically assign patients to wait longer.

Who gets the advantage. The same registry study found that preemptive transplants were less common among people with less education, racial minorities, and those who relied on public insurance.5 The data are American, but the pattern is familiar in any system. The patients most likely to miss an early referral are the ones with the fewest resources to push for it. A clinic nurse who flags every eligible patient, not only the ones who ask, narrows that gap.

RememberEvery month a suitable patient spends on dialysis waiting for a referral that could have been sent earlier is a month of risk the system added. Refer on the trend, not on the start of dialysis.
In the CKD clinic · fictional

Mr. Navarro, 39, a public school teacher, has IgA nephropathy. His eGFR has fallen from 26 to 20 over eight months. His older sister has already told the family she wants to be tested as a donor.

You notice that nobody has sent a referral, because "he is still working and feels fine." You raise it with the nephrologist that morning. The referral goes out the same day, and the sister is given the program's donor line to call herself.

What this shows. A patient who feels well, with a willing donor, is exactly the patient who can avoid dialysis altogether. Feeling fine is a reason to refer now, not a reason to wait.

Workflow

The journey in six stations

  1. Kidney failure planningTransplant is discussed alongside dialysis and comprehensive conservative care, using what matters to the patient.
  2. ReferralA complete referral reaches a named person at the transplant program, who acknowledges receipt.
  3. EvaluationMedical, surgical, psychosocial, infection, cancer and financial review; potential living donors are evaluated by a separate team.
  4. ReadinessEither a living donor is approved, or the candidate is activated on the deceased-donor waiting list and kept ready.
  5. Transplant admissionSurgery, early graft surveillance, induction immunosuppression, discharge teaching.
  6. Lifelong follow-upDrug levels, rejection and infection surveillance, cardiovascular and cancer prevention, and eventually planning if the graft fails.

Each station has its own owner, and the nurse touches all six. At station 1 you raise the topic. At station 2 you assemble and send. At station 3 you track dates and dispositions. At station 4 you keep contact details, transfusion history and illness reports current. At station 5 you assess and escalate. At station 6 you reconcile medicines, time samples, teach and triage. The modules that follow take each station in turn.

On the ward · fictional

Mr. Lacson was listed for a deceased-donor kidney last year. Tonight the coordinator, Joy, calls the ward: an offer has come in, but his phone number on file has been disconnected for three months. His daughter's number is not in the chart. By the time the family is reached through the barangay, the offer has gone to the next candidate.

Nobody in this story made a clinical error. Station 4 failed because nobody owned "keep the contact details current."

What this shows. Each station needs a named owner. Readiness is a nursing task as much as a medical one.

Communication

Raising transplant for the first time

Many patients first hear the word "transplant" in a hurry, at the end of a crowded clinic visit, from someone standing at the door. They nod, go home, and the chart says "informed." A real first conversation takes about five minutes. It changes what the patient does next.

Open with a question, not a lecture. Start with what the patient already knows or believes: "Has anyone talked with you about the ways we can treat kidney failure when it comes?" "What have you heard about kidney transplant?" The answer tells you where to begin. A patient who believes transplant is only for the rich needs a different first sentence from one who has a cousin with a transplant.

Give three options, plainly. Kidney failure can be treated with a transplant, with dialysis (hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis), or with comprehensive conservative care that focuses on symptoms and quality of life without dialysis. Name all three. A patient who hears only about transplant may feel judged later if he is not a candidate. A patient who hears only about dialysis never learns there was another road.

Use one picture. "Dialysis does part of the kidney's work, three times a week. A transplant is a kidney that works all day, every day, as long as you take your medicines and keep your follow-up." Then say where the picture stops being true: a transplant is an operation, it needs lifelong medicines that lower the body's defenses, and it can fail.

Check understanding. Ask the patient to tell you, in his own words, what he will say to his family tonight. This is teach-back, and Module 9 covers it in detail. If his summary is "the doctor will find me a kidney," you have more to explain.

"I'm too old."A belief that age alone rules a person out"Age by itself does not decide it. The team looks at your heart, your strength and your overall health."
"I can't ask anyone for a kidney."Shame, or fear of burdening family"You don't have to ask anyone. People who want to donate contact the donor team themselves. And kidneys from deceased donors also exist."

"Declines referral for now; worried about losing his job during evaluation; agrees to revisit after talking with his wife" leaves the door open and tells the next nurse where to start. Revisit at a later visit. People's answers change as kidney failure comes closer.

In the CKD clinic · fictional

Aling Teresita, 66, says she does not want a transplant. You ask what makes her feel that way. She says her neighbor "died right after the operation," and she does not want to put her children through that.

You do not argue. You tell her that the first weeks after a transplant carry real risk, and that this is why the team checks the heart so carefully first. You ask whether she would be willing to meet the transplant team just to hear about her own risk, with no promise to proceed. She agrees to think about it. You record her fear, in her words, and her agreement to revisit at the next visit.

What this shows. A refusal often holds a specific fear. Name it, answer it honestly, and keep the door open.

Within scopeYou explain the options and the process. You do not tell a patient that he is or is not a candidate, and you do not promise a timeline. The evaluation decides both.
Scope

Who does what on a transplant team

Transplant programs are built to separate decisions that could conflict. The person caring for the recipient does not also advocate for the donor. The person who wants a kidney for a patient does not also allocate kidneys. Knowing these boundaries protects patients, donors and you.

Under the Philippine Nursing Act (RA 9173), the registered nurse assesses, plans and gives nursing care, teaches, and collaborates with physicians and other professionals, within law, physician orders and institutional privileges.6 That is a broad and important scope, and it is enough to make the transplant pathway work. It does not include clearing compatibility, telling a candidate they are accepted, or changing an immunosuppressant dose. Those statements, made kindly but without authority, are the most common way a well-meaning nurse causes harm.

Stop and reportIf a patient or relative asks you to confirm acceptance, a match, or a surgery date that has not been communicated by the program, say what you know, say who will confirm it, and document the question.
At the bedside · fictional

Aling Nena's son corners you in the hallway after his mother's evaluation visit. "Nurse, the doctor looked happy. Is she accepted? Can we tell the relatives in Pampanga?"

You want to reassure him. Instead you say: "I don't have the committee's decision yet. Joy, the coordinator, will call you once it is final, usually within two weeks. I'll write down that you asked, so she knows to call you." Then you document the question and send Joy a message.

What this shows. Kindness and scope are compatible. Say what you know, say who will confirm the rest, and leave a written trail.

Practice

A referral that moves

A referral is a clinical handoff to someone who has never met the patient. It should let the coordinator decide what to schedule first without calling you back. Use the Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (SBAR) structure, and attach what the program asks for.

SituationWho the patient is, why you are referring now, and the trajectory: eGFR trend, or dialysis start date and modality.
BackgroundCause of kidney disease, prior transplants, transfusions and pregnancies, major comorbidities, current medicines, allergies, vascular or peritoneal access.
AssessmentFunction and frailty, adherence history, support at home, potential donors who have volunteered, and barriers: distance, work, cost, language.
RecommendationWhat you are asking for (evaluation appointment), contact preferences and the best time to call, and when you will follow up.

Close the loop. A referral that has been faxed, emailed or handed over is not yet received. Confirm receipt with a named person, record the date, and write the next expected action and its owner in the chart: for example, "Received by the recipient coordinator on 3 March; first evaluation visit to be scheduled by the program within two weeks; clinic RN to call the patient on 17 March to confirm." Then do the call.

In the CKD clinic · fictional

Two referrals leave the clinic on the same day. The first is a one-line note: "For transplant evaluation, please." The second gives the eGFR trend, the cause of kidney disease, two pregnancies and one transfusion, the patient's evening-only phone window, and a request for a first visit.

Three weeks later, the first patient has not been called; the coordinator needed the transfusion history and could not reach anyone. The second patient already has her cardiac tests scheduled.

What this shows. A referral is judged by whether the next person can act on it without calling you back.

Ask what matters. Shared decision-making means the patient hears the options, the honest tradeoffs, and is asked about work, travel, caregiving and fears. A jeepney driver who cannot lose four days a week to dialysis may weigh transplant very differently from a retired teacher with a supportive family nearby. Record those priorities; they guide the evaluation team.

Never recruit donors. You may explain that living and deceased donation both exist and that a living donor must volunteer freely and is evaluated separately. You must not ask a specific relative to donate, or suggest that a family "should" provide a kidney. Pressure, even gentle, undermines the voluntariness that Module 3 protects.

Local practice

The Philippine setting

Kidney transplant in the Philippines is concentrated in a limited number of licensed transplant facilities, led by the National Kidney and Transplant Institute (NKTI), which also trains coordinators for other centers.7 Living donation is the most common source of kidneys, and living non-related donation is governed by its own Department of Health (DOH) rules and ethics review (Module 3). Deceased donation is governed by the Organ Donation Act and DOH allocation rules (Module 4).8 Since January 2025, PhilHealth's Z Benefits for kidney transplantation have been substantially expanded, and post-transplant packages for adults and children were added in 2025 (Module 10).9,10

Geography shapes everything. A patient in Samar or Sulu may need a ferry, a bus and a night's lodging to reach an evaluation visit. A referral that ignores this will stall. Plan with the patient: which visits can be bundled, which tests can be done locally and sent, who can accompany, and how the program can reach them if mobile signal is poor.

Common misconceptions you will hear: that only a sibling can donate; that older patients are never accepted; that diabetes rules a person out; that transplant is only for the wealthy. Each is false or outdated. Correct them gently, and let the program make the individual decision.

By phone · fictional

Ka Lito lives in Northern Samar. His first evaluation visit requires a ferry, two buses and a night in Manila. He has missed it twice. On your third call you ask what would make it possible. He says his cousin drives to Manila every second Monday.

You and Joy rebook the visit for that Monday, bundle the dental and chest X-ray clearances into the same trip, and arrange for his local laboratory to send the blood tests ahead.

What this shows. Geography is a clinical barrier. Plan around it with the patient instead of documenting "no-show."

Problem solving

When a referral stalls: problems and fixes

Most referrals that fail do not fail with a decision. They fail with silence. Weeks pass, nobody calls, and the patient assumes he was turned down. The fix is to look for the stall early and to know its usual causes.

At the monthly log review · fictional

Your log lists eleven patients referred in the last six months. Eight have had a first visit. Three show "sent" and nothing after. All three were referred by email in the same week, when the program's coordinator was on leave.

You call the program. The emails sat in an unattended mailbox. Joy schedules all three that week, and your clinic adds a rule: every emailed referral is followed by a phone call within three working days.

What this shows. One review of a simple log found a failure that affected three patients at once. Stalls cluster, so look for the pattern.

Certainty

What is established, and what is not

  • Established: for most suitable candidates, transplant is associated with longer survival and better quality of life than remaining on dialysis, after an early period of higher risk.1,2 This rests on large, consistent observational data; a randomized trial would be neither ethical nor feasible.
  • Strong, consistent association: shorter time on dialysis before transplant is associated with better graft and patient survival in large registry analyses, including a paired-kidney design that holds donor quality constant.4,5 Part of this is probably causal, but the data are observational and older.
  • Guideline position: informing and considering every patient with CKD G4 to G5 for transplant is a strong KDIGO recommendation (1D), and a preemptive living-donor transplant is the preferred treatment (1A). The 6-to-12-month referral window itself is an ungraded KDIGO statement: expert advice, not a graded recommendation.3
  • Evidence-supported inference: that nurse-led tracking and closed-loop referral shorten time to evaluation is plausible and supported by quality-improvement experience, but trial evidence specific to the Philippines is lacking. Measure it locally (Module 11).
Practice case · synthetic

Case: The driver who works on another island

Tuesday, 10:20 a.m., CKD clinic. Mang Rodel, 46, drives a delivery truck between Cebu and Bohol. He has diabetic kidney disease. His eGFR has fallen from 28 to 19 mL/min/1.73 m² over 14 months, and his albuminuria is heavy. He has no dialysis access yet.

His wife answers most of your questions. When you raise transplant, he shakes his head. "My doctor mentioned it. But my brother is the only one who could give me a kidney, and he has his own family. Besides, I can't miss work to go to Manila." He looks at the clock; his truck leaves for the port at noon.

He has a basic phone and is reachable only after 7 p.m., when he is off the road. The chart shows no referral, no access plan, and the note "transplant: patient not interested."

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, whom you would contact and what you would say, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the debrief below.

Assess

Kidney trajectory and timing (he is within the referral window now), comorbidities, his understanding of options, what matters to him (income, family), support, and practical barriers: travel, work hours, cost, phone access.

Communicate

Send an SBAR referral to the transplant program with trajectory, comorbidities, medicines and his evening-only contact window. Confirm receipt with a named coordinator. Inform the nephrologist that referral is done and access planning should proceed in parallel.

Teach

In two minutes: transplant usually means longer life and freedom from dialysis; evaluation takes months, so starting now protects options; living donors must volunteer freely and are evaluated separately; deceased donation also exists. Correct the idea that only his brother can help, without asking anyone to donate.

Escalate

Refer to social work and the benefits office for travel, lodging and coverage questions. If he cannot reach the program, escalate to the coordinator lead rather than letting the referral lapse.
How it played out · fictional

Your colleague in the story, Nurse Liza, did three things before noon. She told Mang Rodel, in two minutes, that evaluation takes months, that starting now keeps a transplant before dialysis possible, and that a living donor must volunteer freely and is not the only route, because deceased donation exists too. She did not ask about his brother again.

She wrote the SBAR referral with his eGFR trend, his medicines and his evening-only contact window, sent it to the program, and called Joy to confirm receipt. Joy agreed to call him after 7 p.m. on Thursday. Liza wrote that plan in the chart, with her own follow-up call set for the next Monday, and told the nephrologist that access planning should start in parallel.

On Monday, Liza learned that Joy's Thursday call had not gone through; his phone was off at sea. Because the follow-up was written down, Liza noticed, and they agreed on a text-first approach that Mang Rodel had consented to. His first evaluation visit was booked for a Saturday.

Documentation task. Write the SBAR referral, the closed-loop entry (who received it, when, next action and owner, your follow-up date), and one sentence recording what matters to him.

Question 13 of 15When the patient's only phone is shared and he can be reached only after 7 p.m., what belongs in the referral?
Contact feasibility determines whether the program can reach him.
Missed calls are a common reason referrals stall.
Unrealistic and unkind.
Prejudicial and not a clinical recommendation.
Question 14 of 15What is the main purpose of shared decision-making when introducing transplant?
Shared decision-making aligns care with the patient's priorities.
Some patients reasonably choose dialysis or conservative care.
Surgical consent comes much later, from the surgical team.
Donor decisions are voluntary and handled separately.
Question 15 of 15Why are recipient care and donor advocacy separated on a transplant team?
Separation protects the donor's free and informed choice.
Not the reason.
It usually adds work; its purpose is protection.
False.
Reflect. Think of a patient you have cared for who started dialysis without ever being referred for transplant evaluation. At which station did the baton drop, and who could have picked it up?
