Training track for attending pediatric nephrologists

This module is study material for attending pediatric nephrologists and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and it gives no doses. Doses, fill volumes and thresholds must come from current pediatric guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved pediatric protocols and a pediatric pharmacist. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Explain how endothelial injury produces the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury, and separate Shiga toxin, pneumococcal and complement-mediated causes at the bedside.
  • Manage Shiga toxin–associated HUS with attention to volume, dialysis timing, the brain, and the drugs and transfusions that can harm.
  • Recognize atypical HUS early, send the right samples before treatment, and plan complement blockade, meningococcal protection and access in a Philippine setting.

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · the child with blood, platelets and kidneys failing togetherHemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) reaches you by phone from a provincial hospital, usually on day five or six of a diarrheal illness, when the child has stopped passing urine. You decide where the child is safest, what the referring team should stop and start, when dialysis begins, and whether this is the common infection-triggered disease or the rarer complement disease that needs a different drug. You also carry the family through a frightening week and the follow-up that lasts years.
In plain wordsThink of the inner lining of the small kidney vessels as a nonstick coating on a pan. Scratch the coating and everything starts to stick: platelets clump on the bare spots, red cells are sliced as they squeeze past the clumps, and the narrowed pan heats unevenly.

The picture has a limit. A scratched pan stays scratched; the endothelium can heal, and most children with the infection-triggered form recover kidney function. And the scratch has different causes that need different treatments: a bacterial toxin that injures once, a bacterial enzyme that unmasks a hidden antigen, or an immune system that keeps attacking because its brakes are faulty. The pan does not tell you which; the history, the stool and the complement studies do.

Most children with HUS have the Shiga toxin form, need supportive care done well, and recover. A few have pneumococcal disease, where plasma can do harm, and a few have atypical HUS, where days without complement blockade cost kidney and brain. Your work is to support the child through the first week, keep the wrong drugs and products away, and recognize the child who does not follow the usual course.

Opening case · fictional

Case: The toddler who stopped wetting his diaper

It is 9 p.m. on a Tuesday in July. A pediatric resident at a district hospital in San Jose, Nueva Ecija, calls your mobile phone. Gabriel Manalastas is 2 years and 3 months old and weighs 12 kg. Six days ago he had watery diarrhea that turned bloody on the second day, with cramps but little fever. The family gave oral rehydration solution and a loperamide syrup bought at a pharmacy. The barangay health worker referred him today because he is pale and puffy and his diaper has been dry for 18 hours.

His hemoglobin is 6.4 g/dL, platelets 34,000/µL, creatinine 3.2 mg/dL, potassium 6.0 mmol/L and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) far above the laboratory range. The smear shows fragmented red cells. Blood pressure is 124/80; he is drowsy but rousable. The admitting order sheet already lists intravenous ciprofloxacin, a platelet transfusion and furosemide “for oliguria.” The consultant on duty has also asked whether fresh frozen plasma should be given “because this is HUS.” The nearest pediatric unit that can dialyze a 12 kg child is three hours away in Manila. His mother, Rowena, asks you directly whether he will need a machine.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What is the most likely diagnosis, and what on the order sheet should change tonight?
  • Where should Gabriel be treated, and what decides when dialysis starts?
  • What would make you suspect atypical HUS instead, and what should be sent before any plasma or blood product?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

How the endothelium fails

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a lesion, not a diagnosis. Injured endothelial cells swell and detach, the subendothelial space widens, and platelet–fibrin thrombi form in arterioles and glomerular capillaries. Red cells are torn as they pass, which produces fragmented cells (schistocytes), a high LDH, a low haptoglobin and a negative direct antiglobulin test. Platelets are consumed. The glomerulus, with its fenestrated endothelium and high flow, is where the injury shows first, so acute kidney injury (AKI) completes the triad.

Three upstream causes dominate in children, and each injures the endothelium by a different route.

Shiga toxin. Shiga toxin–producing Escherichia coli (STEC), classically O157:H7, colonizes the colon and releases toxin that crosses the inflamed gut wall. The toxin binds globotriaosylceramide (Gb3), a receptor that is dense on glomerular endothelium and podocytes, enters the cell, and stops protein synthesis. The injured cell turns prothrombotic. The diarrhea usually precedes HUS by several days, and the window between bloody diarrhea and HUS is when volume status and drug choices matter most. In a prospective cohort of 71 children under 10 with O157:H7 diarrhea, 10 (14%) developed HUS.1

Pneumococcal neuraminidase. Streptococcus pneumoniae produces neuraminidase, which strips sialic acid from cell surfaces and exposes the Thomsen–Friedenreich (T) antigen on red cells, platelets and endothelium. Preformed antibodies in plasma bind the exposed antigen. Pneumococcal HUS accounts for about 5–15% of all HUS, often after pneumonia with empyema or meningitis, and the review that reports this notes that fresh frozen plasma, which carries more of those antibodies, should be avoided.2 The direct antiglobulin test is often positive here, unlike STEC-HUS.

Complement dysregulation. The alternative complement pathway idles continuously on every cell surface. Factor H, factor I and membrane cofactor protein (CD46) keep it from attacking host endothelium. A loss-of-function variant in a regulator, a gain-of-function variant in C3 or factor B, or an autoantibody against factor H removes the brake. A trigger such as an infection, including a diarrheal one, then sets off terminal complement (C5b-9) on the endothelium, and the attack continues after the trigger is gone. This is atypical HUS (aHUS), and it is the form where blocking C5 changes the course.3

The brain shares the same vessel biology. Seizures, a falling level of consciousness, or focal signs in a child with HUS are TMA in the brain until proven otherwise, worsened by hypertension, hyponatremia and uremia. The gut can perforate or infarct; the pancreas can fail and produce diabetes; the heart can be injured. HUS is a systemic disease that the kidney reports first.

RememberName the lesion first (TMA), then name the cause. The cause, not the lesion, decides whether you support, avoid plasma, or block complement.
On the ward, a second look at the smear · fictional

A 4-year-old in Tarlac is admitted with pneumonia and a right-sided effusion. On day 3 she is pale and her urine is dark and scant. Her platelets are 41,000/µL and the smear shows schistocytes. The resident starts setting up fresh frozen plasma “for HUS.”

You ask him to hold it. The effusion is purulent, the blood culture grows S. pneumoniae, and her direct antiglobulin test is positive.

What this shows. Pneumococcal HUS is the one form where plasma may add fuel. Washed red cells, if transfusion is needed, and treatment of the infection are the safer path.

Lesson · part 2

Shiga toxin HUS: support, fluids and what to avoid

No drug has been shown to stop STEC-HUS once it starts. Outcomes depend on supportive care: perfusion, fluid and electrolytes, blood pressure, nutrition, timely dialysis, and protection of the brain. Two decisions dominate the first days: how much fluid to give, and which drugs to keep away.

Fluid. Children with STEC infection are often hemoconcentrated when HUS begins, and hemoconcentration at onset is associated with more severe disease. Ardissino and colleagues at a single Italian center gave every child with STEC-HUS from 2012 to 2014 an early intravenous volume expansion aimed at about 10% of working weight and compared them with 38 historical patients whose fluids had been restricted. The expanded group needed dialysis less often (26.3% vs 57.9%), had less central nervous system involvement (7.9% vs 23.7%, a difference that did not reach statistical significance, P = .06), fewer intensive care days, and fewer long-term kidney and extrarenal sequelae (13.2% vs 39.5%).4 This is an observational study with historical controls, so changes in referral, recognition and other care over time may explain part of the difference. It suggests that restoring circulating volume helps; it does not show that a child who is already anuric and edematous should receive more fluid.

The practical reading: in the prodrome and the first hours of HUS, a dry child with urine still coming needs volume restored, with close weights and balance. Once the child is anuric and overloaded, more fluid without a way to remove it causes pulmonary edema and hypertension, and that child needs dialysis, not a bolus.

Antibiotics. In the 2000 cohort, 5 of the 10 children who developed HUS had received antibiotics, and antibiotic treatment was associated with HUS after adjustment (relative risk 17.3, 95% confidence interval 2.2 to 137).1 The wide interval reflects nine treated children. A 2016 meta-analysis of 17 reports and 1,896 patients found a pooled odds ratio of 1.33 (0.89 to 1.99) overall, but 2.24 (1.45 to 3.46) when restricted to studies at low risk of bias that used an acceptable HUS definition, and its authors concluded that antibiotics are not recommended in STEC infection.5 The proposed mechanism is toxin release from injured bacteria and induction of the toxin-carrying phage. The evidence is observational, the antibiotic classes differ, and the question is not settled; in practice, a child with bloody diarrhea and suspected STEC should not receive antibiotics for the diarrhea unless another indication exists, such as sepsis.

Antimotility drugs. Loperamide and similar drugs slow toxin clearance from the gut and are widely avoided in bloody diarrhea in children. The evidence is weaker than for antibiotics, but the drug offers a child no benefit worth the risk.

Transfusion of red cells. Hemolysis can drop hemoglobin quickly. Red cells are given for symptomatic anemia or a threshold set by the unit’s protocol, slowly, with attention to volume and potassium in an anuric child.

Dialysis. The usual indications apply: refractory hyperkalemia, acidosis, fluid overload with respiratory compromise or hypertension, uremic encephalopathy, and the need to make room for blood products and nutrition. Peritoneal dialysis (PD) suits small children and avoids heparin and a large central catheter in a child with low platelets; hemodialysis or continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT) is used where PD is not possible, for example after colitis with bowel injury. Module 16 covers acute PD and CKRT.

RememberIn STEC-HUS, restore volume while the child can still make urine, keep antibiotics, antimotility drugs, platelets and plasma away unless a specific reason exists, and dialyze for the usual indications before overload sets in.
Two days before HUS, in a rural health unit · fictional

A 3-year-old boy in Bulacan has had bloody diarrhea for two days. His older sister had the same illness last week. The rural health physician messages you a photograph of the stool and asks which antibiotic to give.

You advise oral rehydration, no antibiotic and no loperamide, a stool sample to the provincial laboratory if available, and a complete blood count, creatinine and urine check in 48 hours or sooner if he looks pale, sleepy or stops passing urine.

What this shows. The window before HUS is where hydration and drug choices are made, usually by someone other than you.

Lesson · part 3

Atypical HUS and complement blockade

Atypical HUS is rare, but it is the form where your speed matters most. Without complement blockade, many children progress to kidney failure or relapse, and relapses can recur after transplant. With blockade, most recover hematologically and many recover kidney function.

Suspecting it. Think of aHUS when a child has TMA without bloody diarrhea, is under 6 months old, has had an episode before, has a relative with HUS, has a low serum C3, or keeps hemolyzing and losing platelets after the first week of good supportive care. A diarrheal trigger does not exclude aHUS; complement variants can declare themselves after an infection, including STEC. In some populations, notably in South Asia, antibody against factor H is a common acquired cause; it matters because it is treated with immunosuppression as well as plasma or complement blockade.

Sampling before treatment. Plasma infusion, plasma exchange and complement blockade all change the results of complement studies. Before the first dose of any of them, draw and store serum and EDTA plasma for C3, C4, factor H, factor I, CD46 expression, anti-factor H antibodies and genetic testing, and send a von Willebrand factor–cleaving protease (ADAMTS13) activity to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. Your hospital may need to send these abroad; the sample can be stored and sent later, but it cannot be drawn later.

The evidence for blockade. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against C5, was studied prospectively in children in an open-label, single-arm phase II trial: 22 patients aged 5 months to 17 years, 11 on dialysis at baseline. By week 26, 14 had a complete TMA response, 18 normalized platelets and LDH, and 9 of the 11 on dialysis came off it; no deaths or meningococcal infections occurred.6 Ravulizumab, an engineered longer-acting C5 antibody, was studied in a single-arm phase III trial in 18 complement inhibitor–naïve children, median age 5.2 years: 77.8% had a complete TMA response by week 26 and 94.4% by week 50, again with no meningococcal infections.7 Neither trial had a control group, and both are small, so they demonstrate response but cannot estimate how much better blockade is than plasma therapy. The size of the response in a disease that otherwise often ends in dialysis is why it is accepted as first-line treatment.

The consensus. HUS International, an expert group, proposed complement blockade as first-line treatment for children with a clinical diagnosis of aHUS, ideally started within 24 to 48 hours of onset or admission, with plasma exchange, or plasma infusion if exchange is not possible, while blockade is unavailable.8 This is expert consensus, not a graded recommendation. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) Controversies Conference on aHUS and C3 glomerulopathy reached broadly similar conclusions and set out the unanswered questions, especially how long to continue treatment.3

Meningococcal protection. Terminal complement is the body’s main defense against Neisseria meningitidis. Every child who receives C5 blockade needs meningococcal vaccination and, because a vaccine response takes time and may be incomplete, antibiotic prophylaxis as the consensus and the product information describe.8 Families need a written fever plan: fever in a child on C5 blockade is an emergency.

Cost and access. C5 inhibitors are among the most expensive drugs in medicine and are not stocked in most Philippine hospitals. The attending’s job is to start the access process on day one: confirm whether the drug is obtainable, involve the hospital’s social service and medical director, document the indication so that charitable and government assistance applications can move, and arrange plasma therapy in the meantime. Do not tell a family that a drug will arrive until someone has confirmed that it will.

RememberIf you suspect atypical HUS, draw and store the complement and genetic samples before any plasma or blockade, start access to complement blockade on day one, and bridge with plasma therapy while you wait.
Lesson · part 4

What the attending does

Confirm TMA and look for the cause in the first hour. Ask for a smear review, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes, direct antiglobulin test, creatinine, electrolytes, urinalysis, blood pressure and a careful volume examination. Send stool for Shiga toxin or STEC where a laboratory can test it; send blood and other cultures if pneumococcal disease is possible; and store complement samples if the story is not typical.

Decide where the child is safest. A child with HUS who is anuric, hyperkalemic, hypertensive, overloaded or drowsy needs a bed where dialysis can start the same day and the brain can be watched. If transfer is three hours away, call the accepting pediatric nephrologist and intensivist yourself, agree on what happens before the ambulance leaves (potassium treatment, stopping harmful orders, blood pressure control, a working intravenous line), and send a written endorsement.

  4. Fix what killsPotassium, acidosis, severe hypertension, fluid overload, seizures.
  5. Choose the bedSame-day dialysis and neurologic monitoring for an anuric, overloaded or drowsy child.
  6. Store samplesComplement and genetic samples and ADAMTS13 before plasma or blockade if aHUS is possible.
  7. Write the planWeights, balance, blood pressure targets as the unit sets them, daily labs, who calls whom.

Run the week. Weigh daily, chart intake and output, and check hemoglobin, platelets, LDH, creatinine and electrolytes at least daily while hemolysis is active. Rising platelets and a falling LDH usually come before urine output returns. Treat hypertension, keep sodium within a safe range, and feed early; PD fluid and catabolism make protein and energy needs high. Look at the abdomen every day for perforation or pancreatitis, and check blood glucose.

Watch the brain. Seizures, a falling level of consciousness, or focal signs are an emergency. Treat seizures, correct sodium and blood pressure, and involve the intensivist. Whether complement blockade helps in severe neurologic STEC-HUS is debated and the evidence is limited to case series; if it is being considered, it is a decision for the attending with the family and the intensivist, with the uncertainty stated plainly.

When the course is not typical. Persistent hemolysis and low platelets after the first week, a C3 below range, no diarrhea, an infant under 6 months, or a relapse should make you revisit the diagnosis and move on complement samples and access.

Follow for years. Children who recover from STEC-HUS can carry proteinuria, hypertension and a reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) into adolescence; in the Italian cohort, 13.2% of the volume-expanded group and 39.5% of historical controls had kidney or extrarenal sequelae.4 Plan blood pressure, urine protein and creatinine checks at discharge, then at intervals the unit sets, for several years, and longer if any abnormality persists. Tell the family in writing; they are the ones who will remember.

Stop and call
  • Anuria with potassium rising or electrocardiogram (ECG) changes
  • Seizure, falling level of consciousness or focal signs
  • Fluid overload with rising oxygen need
  • Severe hypertension
  • Abdominal distension, peritonism or rectal bleeding after the diarrhea had settled
  • Fever in any child receiving complement blockade
RememberEvery child with HUS leaves with a written plan: the cause as best known, the dialysis days, the blood pressure, the urine protein, and who checks each one next.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The cluster after a fiesta

What you see
Within four days, three children from the same barangay in Pampanga are admitted with bloody diarrhea; one now has low platelets and a rising creatinine.
What is likely going on
A common food or water source carrying STEC. More children are likely in the prodrome now.
What to do
Ask the municipal health office to trace the exposure. Advise the referring physicians: hydrate, no antibiotics for the diarrhea, no antimotility drugs, and a blood count and creatinine in every exposed child with bloody stool over the next week.
Why it matters
Outbreak HUS is caught earliest by the people who see the diarrhea, not by the nephrologist who sees the anuria.

The platelet count that frightens the family

What you see
A mother asks why her son, with platelets of 28,000/µL, is not being given platelets when the child in the next bed was.
What is likely going on
She is comparing two different illnesses. In HUS the count is low from consumption in microthrombi.
What to do
Explain that platelets are given in HUS for bleeding or before a procedure such as a catheter insertion, and that otherwise they may add to the clots. Document the conversation and the plan if bleeding occurs.
Why it matters
An unexplained difference in care looks like neglect to a family; a two-minute explanation prevents a complaint and a harmful order.

The infant without diarrhea

What you see
A 4-month-old girl in Iloilo has pallor, platelets of 52,000/µL, schistocytes and a creatinine three times her baseline. There was no diarrhea. C3 is low.
What is likely going on
Atypical HUS is likely.
What to do
Draw and store serum and EDTA plasma for complement, anti-factor H antibodies and genetics, and send ADAMTS13, before any plasma. Call the hospital pharmacy and medical director the same day about complement blockade, arrange plasma therapy as a bridge, and plan meningococcal protection.
Why it matters
In atypical HUS, the samples you do not draw on day one can never be drawn again, and the days without blockade cost nephrons.

The order for furosemide every six hours

What you see
A child with STEC-HUS has been anuric for 24 hours. The chart shows repeated high-dose furosemide and no urine.
What is likely going on
The team is hoping to avoid dialysis.
What to do
Stop the repeated doses. Assess volume and potassium, explain to the team that dialysis is now the safer path, and arrange acute PD or transfer.
Why it matters
Diuretics without urine add ototoxicity and delay; they do not reverse the lesion.
Field notes
  • Ask every referring physician the same three questions: was there bloody diarrhea, what antibiotics or antidiarrheals were given, and when was the last urine.
  • Keep a labeled freezer box in the laboratory for HUS complement samples, with the send-out instructions taped to the lid.
  • Write “No platelets unless bleeding or procedure” and “No antimotility drugs” in the admission orders yourself.
  • Give every family a written follow-up schedule for blood pressure, urine protein and creatinine at discharge.
Case review

Back to the case: The toddler who stopped wetting his diaper

Diagnosis and the order sheet. Bloody diarrhea six days ago in a toddler, then hemolysis with schistocytes, low platelets and anuric AKI, is STEC-HUS until proven otherwise. Ask the resident to stop the ciprofloxacin (no other indication has been found), hold the platelet transfusion (he is not bleeding and no procedure is under way), hold the fresh frozen plasma (no benefit in STEC-HUS, and the cause is not yet confirmed), and stop the loperamide the family was giving. Treat the potassium now as the unit’s protocol directs, avoid nephrotoxins, and send stool for Shiga toxin testing if any laboratory can do it.

Where and when. He is anuric, hyperkalemic, hypertensive and drowsy, so he needs a pediatric unit that can dialyze a 12 kg child today and watch his brain. Call the accepting team in Manila yourself. Furosemide in an anuric child will not help and may delay the decision. Dialysis starts for refractory potassium, acidosis, overload or encephalopathy, and to make room for blood and nutrition; acute PD is a good choice in a toddler with low platelets. Tell Rowena honestly: yes, he probably needs dialysis for a while, most children with this illness recover kidney function, and the next days are about keeping him safe while the kidneys heal.

When to think atypical. No bloody diarrhea, age under 6 months, a previous episode, a relative with HUS, a low C3, or hemolysis and low platelets that persist after the first week would point to atypical HUS. If any of these appears, draw and store serum and EDTA plasma for complement studies, anti-factor H antibodies and genetics, and send ADAMTS13, before any plasma or complement blockade, then start the access process for C5 blockade and arrange plasma therapy as a bridge.

Question 1 of 5A 3-year-old has had bloody diarrhea for two days and is still passing urine. A sibling had the same illness. Which advice best fits the evidence?
Volume restoration is associated with less dialysis in observational data, while antibiotics are associated with HUS in cohort and meta-analysis data. Antimotility drugs slow toxin clearance.
Question 2 of 5In the Ardissino 2016 study of early volume expansion in STEC-HUS, what does the design allow you to conclude?
It was a single-center comparison with historical controls. The association is strong, but other changes over time may contribute, and it says nothing in favor of fluid for an already overloaded, anuric child.
Question 3 of 5A child with pneumococcal empyema develops TMA with a positive direct antiglobulin test. Which product should be avoided?
Neuraminidase exposes the T antigen; plasma carries antibodies that bind it. The review cited in this module advises avoiding fresh frozen plasma.
Question 4 of 5Before plasma therapy or complement blockade in suspected atypical HUS, what should be done?
Plasma and C5 blockade change complement results. Samples take minutes to draw and can be stored and sent later; treatment is not delayed waiting for results.
Question 5 of 5What did the pediatric single-arm trial of eculizumab in atypical HUS report by week 26?
Greenbaum 2016 had no control group, so it shows response rather than a comparison with plasma therapy. No meningococcal infections occurred in that trial.
