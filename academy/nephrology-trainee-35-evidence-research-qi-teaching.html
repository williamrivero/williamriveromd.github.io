Training track for nephrology fellows, residents and medical interns

Study material for medical interns, residents and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and gives no drug doses. Doses, rates and order sets come from current guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved protocols and your supervising physician. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Intern: Find the population, intervention, comparison and outcome of a trial report, and say in one sentence whether the outcome is one that patients feel or a surrogate.
  • Resident: Appraise one randomized trial, compute the absolute risk reduction (ARR) and number needed to treat (NNT) from its event rates, and explain the result to a patient in plain numbers.
  • Fellow: Design an ethical quality improvement (QI) or research project with a run chart or a protocol fit for ethics review, and teach a learner with structured feedback.
Pre-testBefore you read, answer the 5 self-assessment questions and note your score. After the module, select New set of questions and compare. The difference is what you learned; the questions you still miss are what to reread.
Start here

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · turning a paper into a decision, and a decision into a better unitThe intern reads the abstract and asks the plain questions: who was studied, what was compared, and what happened to them? The resident turns the published percentages into numbers a patient can use, and notices the traps. The fellow takes the next step: measures how the unit actually performs, tests one change at a time, knows when a project needs ethics review, and teaches all of this to the people behind them.
In plain wordsThink of a trial as a weather forecast for a crowd. It cannot tell you whether it will rain on one person’s house, but it can tell you that out of 100 houses like hers, about 21 will get wet without an umbrella and about 18 with one.

The picture has a limit. Weather does not change because you watch it; patients in trials are selected, monitored and treated more carefully than the patient in front of you, and the “rain” is often a composite of several different events, some of which patients feel and some of which are laboratory thresholds. A forecast also comes with a cost the trial never counts: in the Philippines, many kidney-protective medicines are paid for out of pocket, and the family decides whether three fewer events per 100 people over about 2.6 years is worth the monthly price. Appraisal is the skill of reading the forecast with all of that in mind.

Most journal-club arguments are about relative effects quoted without baselines, composite outcomes read as if every part mattered equally, surrogate markers read as if they were outcomes, and subgroups read as if they were proof. A few are about the deeper question of whether the trial population looks like your patients at all. Your work is to convert every claim into absolute numbers for a defined group over a defined time, to name the traps aloud, and then to carry the same discipline into measuring and improving your own unit.

Opening case · fictional

Case: Journal club on a Tuesday

Tuesday, 12:15 p.m., the conference room of a tertiary hospital in Cebu City. Dr. Mia Fernandez, a second-year internal medicine resident, presents FIDELIO-DKD. Her first slide says, in large letters, “Finerenone reduces kidney disease progression by 18%.” Dr. Carlo Aquino, a first-year nephrology fellow, says the drug is too expensive for their clinic patients and that “sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors already do the job.” Another resident shows a newer trial in which a combination lowered albuminuria by about a third more than either drug alone and says, “So the combination must prevent dialysis.”

The patient they have in mind is Mr. Teodoro Lim, 62, a retired jeepney operator from Mandaue with type 2 diabetes, an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 48 mL/min/1.73 m², a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 640 mg/g, and a potassium of 4.7 mmol/L, already on the maximum tolerated dose of an angiotensin receptor blocker and on an SGLT2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) his daughter in Singapore pays for. The attending asks the fellow to propose a unit project afterward, because two patients started on a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist last year had no potassium checked for three months.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What are the absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat for FIDELIO-DKD’s primary outcome, and how would you explain them to Mr. Lim?
  • Which interpretation traps appear in this discussion, and how would you correct each one?
  • What project should the fellow propose for potassium monitoring, and does it need ethics review?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

From the abstract to absolute numbers

Every therapeutic claim reduces to one question: in people like these, over this much time, how many fewer had the bad outcome, and at what cost in harm? Relative effects such as hazard ratios are the natural output of the statistics, but they hide the baseline. An 18% relative reduction means one thing when the baseline risk is 40% and another when it is 4%. Laupacis and colleagues proposed the number needed to treat as the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction, precisely so that clinicians could see the size of an effect in people rather than in ratios.1

Arithmetic from the FIDELIO-DKD abstract, not a target. ARR = control event rate − treatment event rate = 21.1% − 17.8% = 3.3 percentage points.
NNT = 1 ÷ ARR = 1 ÷ 0.033 ≈ 30 people treated for the median 2.6 years to prevent one primary outcome event.
Number needed to harm for stopping the drug because of hyperkalemia = 1 ÷ (0.023 − 0.009) ≈ 71.

The trial. The Finerenone in Reducing Kidney Failure and Disease Progression in Diabetic Kidney Disease (FIDELIO-DKD) trial randomized 5,734 patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD), that is, diabetic kidney disease (DKD),, all on renin-angiotensin system blockade adjusted to the maximum labeled dose that did not cause unacceptable side effects, to finerenone or placebo. Eligible patients had a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 30 to under 300 mg/g with an eGFR of 25 to under 60 and diabetic retinopathy, or a ratio of 300 to 5,000 mg/g with an eGFR of 25 to under 75. Over a median 2.6 years, the primary composite of kidney failure, a sustained fall in eGFR of at least 40%, or renal death occurred in 504 of 2,833 patients (17.8%) on finerenone and 600 of 2,841 (21.1%) on placebo, hazard ratio 0.82 (95% confidence interval 0.73 to 0.93). The key cardiovascular secondary outcome occurred in 13.0% versus 14.8%. Overall adverse events were similar; stopping the trial drug because of hyperkalemia was more frequent with finerenone (2.3% versus 0.9%).2

The pooled view. FIDELITY combined FIDELIO-DKD with its companion trial, Finerenone in Reducing Cardiovascular Mortality and Morbidity in Diabetic Kidney Disease (FIGARO-DKD), in a prespecified analysis of 13,026 patients followed for a median 3.0 years. The cardiovascular composite occurred in 12.7% versus 14.4% (hazard ratio 0.86), and a kidney composite that used a stricter threshold, a sustained eGFR fall of at least 57%, occurred in 5.5% versus 7.1% (hazard ratio 0.77). Hyperkalemia leading to permanent discontinuation occurred in 1.7% versus 0.6%.3 The crude absolute differences are 1.7 and 1.6 percentage points, about 59 and 63 people treated for three years per event prevented; a pooled analysis of two trials is still randomized evidence, but its kidney outcome definition differs from FIDELIO-DKD’s, so the numbers are not interchangeable.

The guideline. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2022 diabetes guideline places a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist with proven kidney or cardiovascular benefit in adults with type 2 diabetes, an eGFR of at least 25 mL/min/1.73 m², a normal serum potassium and albuminuria despite the maximum tolerated dose of a renin-angiotensin system inhibitor.4 Read the guideline itself for the strength and grade attached; this module does not restate a grade it has not checked. KDIGO has also begun a focused update of this chapter, so check for the newest text.

Philippine practiceA drug the family buys every month competes with food, fares and other medicines. Give the family absolute numbers over a stated time, the harm and its monitoring, and the cost, then let them weigh it. An NNT of about 30 over 2.6 years is a real benefit; it is also an honest statement that most people treated would not have had the event either way.
RememberNever present a relative effect alone. Say how many people in 100, over how many years, avoided the outcome, and how many had the harm.
Lesson · part 2

Traps: relative effects, composites, surrogates and subgroups

Misreading a trial rarely comes from bad arithmetic. It comes from four habits of mind, each with a mechanism you can name. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was built partly to make these judgments explicit: it rates the certainty of evidence for each outcome that matters to patients, and it separates that certainty from the strength of a recommendation.5

TrapHow it misleadsWhat you say at journal club
Relative effect without a baseline“18% reduction” sounds the same for a high-risk and a low-risk patient“From 21.1% to 17.8% over 2.6 years: 3.3 fewer per 100.”
Composite outcome read as one thingA benefit driven by the softest component is credited to the hardest“Which components drove the result? Is the eGFR threshold a surrogate?”
Surrogate read as an outcomeA laboratory marker improves; patients may not“Albuminuria fell. Did kidney failure, death or hospitalization fall?”
Subgroup read as proofWith enough subgroups, some differ by chance“Was it prespecified, with a stated direction, one of few, and is there a test for interaction?”
Population that does not matchTrial patients are selected and monitored closely“Would our patient have been eligible? Would we monitor potassium as closely?”
Statistical versus clinical significanceA small p value can attach to a small effect“What is the confidence interval in absolute terms?”

Surrogates. The CONFIDENCE trial randomized adults with type 2 diabetes, CKD and albuminuria to finerenone, empagliflozin or both. At 180 days, the reduction in urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio with the combination was 29% greater than with finerenone alone and 32% greater than with empagliflozin alone; symptomatic hypotension, acute kidney injury and hyperkalemia leading to stopping were uncommon.6 It was a phase 2 trial with albuminuria as its primary outcome. Albuminuria is a surrogate: it is associated with kidney failure, and lowering it is biologically plausible as a route to benefit, but CONFIDENCE did not measure kidney failure, dialysis or death. “So the combination must prevent dialysis” is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Subgroups. Sun and colleagues set out criteria for judging whether a subgroup effect is believable: among them, whether the subgroup was specified before the analysis with a predicted direction, whether it is one of a small number tested, whether the test for interaction is significant, whether the effect is consistent across related outcomes and studies, and whether there is a compelling biological rationale.7 Most subgroup claims in a slide deck meet few of these.

Reporting. The CONSORT 2025 statement is a 30-item checklist and a flow diagram for reporting randomized trials, with a new section on open science.8 Use it as a reading checklist: if the flow of participants, the prespecified primary outcome or the harms are hard to find, the report is hiding something or the trial was weak.

At your stageIntern: label every outcome you present as felt by patients or surrogate. Resident: put the absolute numbers on your first slide, not your last. Fellow: ask the subgroup and composite questions aloud, kindly, so that residents learn to ask them.
RememberBefore you believe a result, name the outcome, the baseline, the time and the comparison. If any of the four is missing, you do not yet know the effect.
Lesson · part 3

Quality improvement: PDSA cycles and run charts

Research asks whether something works in general. Quality improvement asks whether your unit reliably does what is already known to work, and whether a change helps here. The causal model is different. The evidence for checking potassium after starting a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist is not in question; what is in question is why two of your patients went three months without a test. The answer lies in the process: who orders the test, who reminds, who reads the result, and what happens when the patient cannot afford the fare to the laboratory.

The plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycle tests one change on a small scale, predicts what will happen, measures, learns and then adapts, adopts or abandons it before the next cycle. A systematic review of 73 articles reporting PDSA in health care found that many applications did not follow the method’s main features: fewer than 20% (14 of 73) fully documented a sequence of iterative cycles, and only 15% (7 of 47) used quantitative data at monthly or more frequent intervals to guide the cycles.9 The lesson is to keep cycles small, predictions explicit and data frequent.

The run chart plots a measure over time, in order, with a median line. It lets a team see whether changes are followed by real improvement or by ordinary variation, with little mathematics, and it is the foundation for more formal control charts.10 An average before and after hides exactly what a run chart shows: when the change happened and whether it held.

Figure 4 · A small QI project for potassium monitoring

  1. AimBy the end of three months, 90% of clinic patients started on a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist have a potassium result within the interval the attending and the unit protocol set.
  2. MeasureEach week, count patients started and those with a timely result. Plot the percentage on a run chart.
  3. Change idea 1A pre-printed laboratory request and return date given with every new prescription.
  4. PredictWrite down what you expect: for example, timely tests rise from half to most.
  5. StudyAfter two to four weeks, look at the run chart and ask the patients who missed why.
  6. ActKeep, adapt or drop the change. If fares are the barrier, test a second idea, such as a nearby partner laboratory or a home extraction service.
QI elementGood practiceCommon error
AimSpecific, measurable, time-bound“Improve monitoring”
MeasureOne outcome measure, one process measure, one balancing measureOnly an end-of-year average
ChangeSmall, tested on a few patients firstA unit-wide policy on day one
DataWeekly or monthly points on a run chartBefore-and-after comparison only

At your stageIntern: collect data honestly and record misses without blame. Resident: run one PDSA cycle with your team during the rotation. Fellow: lead a project through several cycles, present the run chart at the unit’s continuous quality improvement (CQI) meeting, and hand it on when you rotate.
RememberMeasure often, change one thing at a time, and let the run chart, not the average, tell you whether it worked.
Lesson · part 4

When a project becomes research: ethics review in the Philippines

The line between QI and research matters because research asks people to accept risks or burdens for knowledge that may benefit others, and that requires independent review. A unit project that changes how reminders are given, uses data the unit already collects, and is meant only to improve local care usually sits on the QI side. A project that randomizes patients, adds tests or contacts beyond usual care, collects data for a general conclusion, or is planned for publication often crosses into research. When in doubt, the decision belongs to a research ethics committee, not to the investigator.

In the Philippines, the Philippine Health Research Ethics Board (PHREB) promulgated the National Ethical Guidelines for Research Involving Human Participants in 2022.11 Internationally, the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki, revised in 2024 with the title “Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Participants,” requires that a research protocol be submitted to a research ethics committee for consideration, comment, guidance and approval before the study begins.12 Data handling falls under the Data Privacy Act (Module 33).

Feature of the projectLeans toward QILeans toward research
PurposeImprove care in this unitProduce generalizable knowledge
InterventionImplementing known good practiceTesting an unproven intervention
AllocationEveryone gets the changeRandom or deliberate allocation
Burden on patientsNone beyond usual careExtra tests, visits, questionnaires or risks
DataRoutine data, kept within the unitNew data, shared or published
What to doFollow the hospital’s QI process; ask the research ethics committee if unsureWrite a protocol and get research ethics committee approval before starting

Two Philippine pressures deserve care. Families often defer to the physician and may agree to anything asked of them in a hospital; consent must be informed, voluntary and free of any suggestion that care depends on it. And patients who pay out of pocket for medicines may join a trial to get free drugs; that is a legitimate benefit, but it raises the duty to explain what happens when the trial ends.

At your stageIntern: never collect data for a project without knowing whether it has approval. Resident: ask the research ethics committee before you start a case series or audit you plan to publish. Fellow: write the protocol, including how data are protected, and submit it before the first patient.
RememberIf you plan to publish, randomize, or add any burden for patients, it is research until a research ethics committee says otherwise. Ask before you start.
Lesson · part 5

Teaching on the run: the one-minute preceptor and feedback

Most clinical teaching in a Philippine hospital happens in the corridor, between admissions, with a fellow teaching a resident and a resident teaching an intern. The mechanism of good teaching under these conditions is to make the learner’s reasoning visible, correct it precisely and give one rule they can carry.

Neher and colleagues described five microskills for this setting: get a commitment, probe for supporting evidence, teach a general rule, reinforce what was done right, and correct mistakes.13 The model was described from experience and was well received by teachers; it is a practical framework rather than a trial-tested intervention. Ramani and Krackov’s review of the feedback literature offers twelve tips for clinical teachers, among them establishing a respectful learning climate, basing feedback on direct observation, making it timely and regular, using specific, neutral language about performance rather than the person, and ending with an action plan.14

MicroskillWhat you sayIn the journal club case
Get a commitment“What do you think we should do for Mr. Lim?”Mia commits: add finerenone
Probe for evidence“What makes you say that?”She cites the 18% reduction
Teach a general rule“Always translate a relative effect into absolute numbers.”21.1% to 17.8%, NNT about 30 over 2.6 years
Reinforce what was right“You chose a trial that matches his eGFR and albuminuria.”Specific praise
Correct mistakes“The headline slide left out the baseline and the harm.”Add hyperkalemia and cost

Feedback works best when it is about something you saw, given soon, in private when it is corrective, and paired with one thing to do next time. “Good job” teaches nothing; “Your first slide gave the relative reduction without the event rates; next time put both on the same line” teaches a habit.

At your stageIntern: ask for feedback on one specific task each week. Resident: use the five microskills once a day with your intern. Fellow: observe a resident directly at least once a week, give feedback that names the behavior, and agree on one action.
RememberTeach by getting a commitment and correcting the reasoning, not by lecturing the answer. Feedback names a behavior you saw and one thing to do next.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The drug representative’s brochure

What you see
In a clinic in Bacolod, a brochure says a new drug “cuts the risk of kidney failure by 30%.”
What is likely going on
A relative effect with no baseline, time or comparison.
What to do
Find the trial, read the event rates in both arms and the follow-up, compute the absolute difference and NNT, and check whether the outcome was a surrogate.
Why it matters
Relative effects make every drug look large; patients pay for absolute benefit.

The case series for the regional convention

What you see
A resident in Baguio wants to present ten patients with leptospirosis-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) seen last year, with laboratory tables, at a conference.
What is likely going on
A retrospective case series planned for presentation, using identifiable health data.
What to do
Ask the research ethics committee whether review is needed, remove identifiers, and follow the hospital’s data protection rules before collecting data.
Why it matters
Intent to present or publish often moves a project into research; privacy rules apply either way.

The audit that changed everything at once

What you see
A dialysis unit in General Santos introduces a new catheter dressing, a new hand-hygiene poster and a new nurse schedule in the same week; infections fall.
What is likely going on
No way to know which change helped, if any.
What to do
Plot monthly rates on a run chart, and in future test one change at a time in small PDSA cycles with a written prediction.
Why it matters
Changing everything at once teaches nothing about what to keep.

The intern who never gets feedback

What you see
An intern on the renal rotation in Naga says the only comment she has received in three weeks is “okay.”
What is likely going on
Teaching without observation or specific feedback.
What to do
As her resident, observe one presentation, give specific feedback that names one behavior done well and one to change, and agree on an action for the next day.
Why it matters
Specific, timely feedback based on observation is how habits change.
Field notes
  • Write the control and treatment event rates and the follow-up time on the first slide of every journal club.
  • Label each outcome you present as patient-felt or surrogate.
  • Keep a small notebook of PDSA cycles: date, change, prediction, result, decision.
  • Ask the research ethics committee before you collect data for anything you might present.
  • End every teaching moment with one general rule the learner can carry.
Case review

Back to the case: Journal club on a Tuesday

Absolute numbers for Mr. Lim. The ARR for FIDELIO-DKD’s primary outcome is 21.1% minus 17.8%, 3.3 percentage points over a median 2.6 years, and the crude NNT is about 30. For Mr. Lim and his daughter: “In the trial, out of 100 people like you, about 21 on placebo and about 18 on finerenone had kidney failure, a large fall in kidney function, or died from kidney causes over about two and a half years. About 2 in 100 had to stop the drug because of high potassium, compared with about 1 in 100 on placebo. The drug is paid out of pocket.” Then check whether he resembles the trial population, including the SGLT2 inhibitor he already takes, and let the family weigh it with the attending.

The traps. “Reduces progression by 18%” is a relative effect without a baseline; replace it with event rates and time. The primary outcome is a composite that includes a laboratory threshold, so say which parts are felt by patients. The combination trial measured albuminuria, a surrogate, over 180 days, so “prevents dialysis” is a hypothesis. “SGLT2 inhibitors already do the job” is a claim about background therapy: check the baseline table for how many FIDELIO-DKD participants were on one, and do not rescue the argument with a post hoc subgroup unless it meets credibility criteria. Cost is a legitimate part of the decision, not a reason to skip the numbers.

The fellow’s project. The missed potassium checks are a process failure, so a QI project fits: an aim such as 90% of new starts with a timely potassium within three months, a weekly run chart, and small PDSA cycles starting with a pre-printed request and return date, then a nearby laboratory if fares are the barrier. It implements known good practice, uses routine data and adds no burden, so it leans toward QI under the hospital’s process. If the team later wants to publish it or compare strategies by allocation, they write a protocol and ask the research ethics committee first.

Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 5 questions at random from this module’s 5-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 4 of 5 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 5An intern summarizes a trial: “The drug reduced albuminuria by 30%.” Which description of this outcome is correct?
Albuminuria is a laboratory surrogate. Treating it as equivalent to preventing dialysis (the tempting distractor) is the error CONFIDENCE illustrates: it measured albuminuria at 180 days, not kidney failure.
Question 2 of 5In FIDELIO-DKD, a trial in diabetic kidney disease (DKD), the primary outcome occurred in 17.8% with finerenone and 21.1% with placebo over a median 2.6 years. What are the absolute risk reduction and crude number needed to treat?
ARR = 21.1% minus 17.8% = 3.3 percentage points; NNT = 1 divided by 0.033, about 30 over the trial period. The 18% figure (the tempting distractor) is the relative reduction implied by the hazard ratio, not the absolute one.
Question 3 of 5A fellow wants to compare two reminder methods for potassium checks by randomly assigning clinic patients to each, and to publish the results. What is required?
Random allocation and intent to publish make this research; the Declaration of Helsinki requires research ethics committee review before the study begins, and PHREB’s 2022 guidelines govern Philippine practice. Low risk (the tempting distractor) affects the type of review, not whether review is needed.
Question 4 of 5A resident’s slide says a drug “worked especially well in women” based on one of 24 subgroups in the forest plot, not prespecified, with no interaction test reported. How should the fellow respond?
Subgroup credibility criteria ask about prespecification with a stated direction, the number of subgroups and the interaction test. A confidence interval within one subgroup (the tempting distractor) does not show that the effect differs between subgroups.
Question 5 of 5A dialysis unit tested a new catheter-care checklist and reports only that infections were 1.1 per 100 patient-months the year before and 0.9 the year after. What is the best next step to judge whether the change helped?
Run charts show timing and persistence that a before-and-after average hides. Concluding success from two averages (the tempting distractor) cannot separate improvement from ordinary variation or other changes.
