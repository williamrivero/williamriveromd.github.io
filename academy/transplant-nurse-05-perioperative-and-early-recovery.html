Perioperative Care and Early Graft Surveillance

Where the new kidney sits and how it is plumbed, what hourly urine, vital signs and the surgical drain are telling you, which early complications kill grafts within hours, and what the newest trials say about the fluid in the bag.

Specialty subtrack for registered nurses

This subtrack prepares Philippine registered nurses for supervised kidney transplant ward and clinic work. It teaches assessment, communication and escalation within nursing scope. Candidacy, listing, organ allocation, compatibility decisions, dose changes and benefit approval belong to the authorized transplant team, the histocompatibility laboratory, the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) and the facility. All cases are synthetic.

Learning objectives · about 3 hours · week 3

After this module you should be able to

  • Describe the surgical anatomy of a kidney transplant and relate each connection to a complication you can detect.
  • Receive a structured OR-to-ward handoff and set escalation parameters.
  • Interpret urine output in context: timing, native kidney output, fluid balance, perfusion and drain findings.
  • Recognize and escalate bleeding, vascular thrombosis, catheter obstruction, urine leak and delayed graft function.
  • Explain the evidence on balanced crystalloids and ureteric stents and what it means for practice.
Intuition

Three pipes and a pump

A transplanted kidney is joined to the body by three pipes. An artery brings blood in, a vein takes it out, and a ureter carries urine to the bladder. The heart is the pump that pushes blood through. Almost every early complication is one of these pipes kinking, clotting, leaking or blocking, or the pump not delivering enough pressure. Hourly urine output is your window onto all of it at once.

The picture has a limit. Pipes either carry flow or they do not; a kidney is not a pipe. Its output depends on how much blood reaches the glomeruli, on tubules that are still recovering from hours on ice, and on the patient's volume. Urine output is also a sum, not a single signal. The patient's own kidneys may still make urine, so a new kidney that is not working can hide behind native output, and a well-functioning graft can look poor if the patient is dry. That is why urine is never read alone, but always with vital signs, fluid balance, the drain, and the laboratory trend.

Physiology

Where the kidney goes and what can go wrong

The donor kidney is usually placed in the right or left iliac fossa, outside the peritoneum, just above the groin. The donor renal artery is joined to the recipient's external iliac artery, the renal vein to the external iliac vein, and the ureter to the bladder, usually over a temporary internal stent. A urinary catheter drains the bladder for several days to protect the bladder join, and a surgical drain sits near the kidney. The native kidneys are usually left in place.

Is it urine or lymph? If drain output suddenly increases, the team may send drain fluid for creatinine. A drain creatinine far above the serum creatinine means urine, so a leak; a value similar to serum suggests lymph or serous fluid.

On the transplant ward, day 3 · fictional

Mrs. Bautista's urine output, steady at 120 mL an hour, falls to 30 mL. At the same time her drain, which had given 20 mL of pink fluid per shift, has filled with 180 mL of clear, pale yellow fluid in four hours. Her blood pressure and pulse are unchanged.

You check the catheter (no kink, no clot), record both trends side by side, and call the surgical resident. The team sends drain fluid for creatinine. It comes back many times higher than her serum creatinine: urine is leaking from the bladder join.

What this shows. Read urine and drain together. Falling urine with rising clear drain fluid points to a leak, not to a quiet kidney.

Practice

Before and after the operating room

Before surgery. Verify identifiers, consent, allergies, the crossmatch status the team has communicated, last dialysis, potassium and other ordered results, dialysis access (no blood pressure cuffs or venipuncture on a fistula arm), and the team's readiness checklist. Record baseline weight and native urine output, a number you will need for days.

OR-to-ward handoff. Receive a structured report and do not accept the patient until you have it:

ProcedureSide, donor type, vascular joins, stent, any difficulty, cold ischemia time.
Intraoperative eventsBlood loss, transfusions, hypotension, vasopressors, whether the kidney made urine on the table.
Lines and drainsCentral line, arterial line, catheter, drain position and character of output so far.
OrdersFluid replacement protocol, blood pressure and central venous pressure targets, immunosuppression doses and timing, laboratory schedule.
Escalation parametersExactly which urine, blood pressure, drain and laboratory values require a call, and to whom.

Living donors follow their own recovery pathway (Module 3), on a separate chart. Do not discuss the donor's progress with the recipient's visitors without the donor's permission.

At the operating room door · fictional

The anesthesia resident wheels Mr. Ocampo in and says, "Everything went well, he's stable, orders are in the system," and turns to leave.

You ask him to stay two more minutes. Did the kidney make urine on the table? Any hypotension or pressors? What blood pressure target, and which values should prompt a call? He checks his notes: the kidney made only a little urine, and the patient needed a low-dose vasopressor for 20 minutes. Those two facts change how closely you watch the first night.

What this shows. A complete handoff is a safety step, not a courtesy. Do not accept the patient until you have it.

Pharmacology

Induction and the first doses

The immune system meets the new kidney at the moment the clamps come off. Donor cells, donor human leukocyte antigen (HLA) and the inflammation of ischemia-reperfusion injury all arrive in the recipient's circulation at once. This is when the alarm is loudest. Induction is the strong, short course of immunosuppression given around surgery to keep the first response from taking hold, while the maintenance drugs of Module 6 reach steady levels.

What the guideline says. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) recipient guideline recommends starting a combination of immunosuppressive medicines before, or at the time of, transplantation (1A), and including induction with a biologic agent (1A). It recommends an interleukin-2 receptor antagonist, such as basiliximab, as first-line induction (1B), and suggests a lymphocyte-depleting agent, such as anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG), rather than an interleukin-2 receptor antagonist for recipients at high immunologic risk (2B).1 The guideline notes the trade-off: lymphocyte-depleting induction reduces acute rejection compared with an interleukin-2 receptor antagonist, but has not been shown to prolong graft survival, and it increases infections and cancers.1 Acute rejection is a surrogate here; graft survival, infection and cancer are the outcomes patients feel. In KDIGO's system, "1" means "we recommend" and "2" means "we suggest"; the letter (A to D) is the certainty of the evidence. The guideline is from 2009, and practice has moved toward wider ATG use in many centers, but the reasoning still holds.

AgentHow it worksWhat you watch
BasiliximabBlocks the interleukin-2 receptor on activated T cells, so they cannot receive the signal to multiply. It does not destroy T cells.Usually well tolerated. Give the doses at the times ordered, typically one around surgery and one a few days later. Rare allergic reactions.
Anti-thymocyte globulinAntibodies raised in animals against human T cells. They destroy and remove T cells for weeks to months.Infusion reactions: fever, chills, rigors, low blood pressure, rash, and occasionally breathing difficulty, especially with the first dose. Giving ATG safely. ATG is given through a large or central vein over several hours, at the rate and with the premedication your protocol orders, usually a steroid, an antihistamine and paracetamol. The early reactions come from the release of cytokines as T cells are destroyed. They are expected, but they must be watched. Take vital signs at the intervals your protocol sets, more often during the first dose. If a reaction occurs, slow or stop the infusion as the protocol directs and call the team. Check the white cell and platelet counts before each dose; the prescriber may reduce or hold a dose when they are low.

The first maintenance doses. Tacrolimus is usually started around surgery, as ordered, and its first trough levels guide early dose changes. Give each dose on time and record the exact time, because Module 6 explains that a trough level is only as good as its timing. The steroid dose that keeps rejection away also pushes glucose up, often in a patient who never had diabetes. Check glucose as ordered and report high values early.

On the transplant ward, first night · fictional

Forty minutes into the first dose of ATG, Mr. Aguilar starts to shake. His temperature is 38.9 °C, his blood pressure has fallen from 134/80 to 98/58 mmHg, and his heart rate is 118. He says he feels cold and frightened.

You stop the infusion as your protocol directs, keep the line open, call the transplant resident, and repeat vital signs every five minutes. You reassure Mr. Aguilar that this is a known reaction to the medicine. The resident examines him, gives the ordered treatment, and restarts the infusion at a lower rate once he settles.

What this shows. First-dose ATG reactions are common and usually manageable. Your part is to recognize the reaction early, follow the protocol and get the team to the bedside.

Practice

Reading urine, pressure, drain and laboratory trends

Hourly urine output. In the first 24 to 72 hours many programs order hourly urine measurement with intravenous replacement calculated from the previous hour's output plus a baseline. A kidney recovering from ischemia may produce large volumes of dilute urine; replacement prevents dehydration and protects perfusion. Large outputs can also strip potassium, magnesium and phosphate, so follow the ordered electrolyte checks.

Sort a fall in urine output like acute kidney injury (AKI). Before the kidney: low circulating volume, low blood pressure, bleeding, or arterial thrombosis. In the kidney: delayed graft function (DGF), rejection, drug toxicity. After the kidney: a blocked catheter, ureteric obstruction or a leak. Work from the quickest to check:

  1. Check the catheter and tubing for kinks and clots; flush only if your policy and orders allow.
  2. Check vital signs, perfusion, and the cumulative fluid balance against baseline weight.
  3. Look at the drain: volume, color, and change over the last hours.
  4. Look at the wound and graft site for swelling, tenderness or leakage.
  5. Review the latest laboratory values and when the next set is due.

If correcting a kink restores urine flow and the patient is stable, document and keep watching closely. If urine stays low, or falls together with hypotension, rising heart rate, increasing bloody drainage, severe graft pain or worsening laboratory results, escalate immediately using your emergency pathway. Do not wait for rounds, and do not give extra fluid beyond the prescription on your own initiative; a falling urine output with rising blood loss is not a fluid problem.

On the transplant ward, 11 p.m. · fictional

Six hours after surgery, Mr. Reyes's urine falls from 150 mL to 40 mL an hour. His heart rate has crept from 88 to 112, his blood pressure from 132/80 to 104/62, and the drain holds 250 mL of dark red blood since 9 p.m. The fluid protocol tells you to replace the last hour's output plus a baseline.

Nurse Carla stops you before you run in more fluid. "Look at all three numbers together. This is bleeding until proven otherwise." You activate the emergency response, stay at the bedside, and repeat vital signs every 15 minutes while the surgeon comes.

What this shows. A fall in urine with rising heart rate and bloody drainage is a surgical problem. More fluid on your own initiative is the wrong reflex.

Stop and callAbrupt anuria in a previously flowing graft, signs of bleeding with hemodynamic change, or severe graft pain are surgical emergencies. Activate the emergency response, stay with the patient, maintain monitoring and prepare for ordered imaging or return to theater.

Preserved urine does not prove a working graft. If the native kidneys make 1 liter a day, a graft that is not functioning can be masked. Creatinine and potassium trends, compared with the native baseline output you recorded, tell the real story.

On the transplant ward, day 2 · fictional

Mrs. Ilagan still passes about 900 mL of urine a day, as she did on dialysis. Her family is relieved. But her creatinine is unchanged from before surgery, and her potassium is rising.

You compare her output with the native baseline you recorded on admission, and report that the new kidney may not yet be working. The team arranges an ultrasound and plans dialysis.

What this shows. The baseline native output you wrote down on admission is what lets you read the urine honestly.

Physiology

Electrolytes, glucose and recovery in the first week

A new kidney recovering from cold ischemia does not regulate the body's chemistry smoothly at first. Its tubules, the parts that fine-tune sodium, potassium, magnesium and phosphate, are the parts most injured by ischemia. Add high-dose steroids, a calcineurin inhibitor and large fluid volumes, and the first week becomes a week of predictable chemical swings.

ValueWhy it changesWhat you do
Potassium, highDelayed graft function leaves potassium uncleared; calcineurin inhibitors reduce potassium excretion by the tubule; tissue injury releases potassiumReport rising values early; record an electrocardiogram if ordered; know the protocol for severe values
Potassium, lowA large diuresis washes potassium outFollow the replacement orders; report values outside the range set in the orders
Magnesium, lowTacrolimus makes the tubule waste magnesiumGive replacement as ordered; low magnesium makes low potassium harder to correct
Phosphate, lowParathyroid hormone and the phosphate-losing hormone fibroblast growth factor 23, both high from years of kidney failure, stay high after the new kidney starts, so the kidney sheds phosphateReport values; replacement is ordered by the team
Calcium, highOvergrown parathyroid glands from the dialysis years keep pushing calcium upReport; the team may follow it for months
Glucose, highHigh-dose steroids and tacrolimus raise glucose and reduce insulin releaseCheck as ordered; follow the insulin protocol; report persistent highs

The pattern teaches a principle. The new kidney is not yet a finished organ. It recovers over days to weeks, while the body's hormones adjust from years of kidney failure. That is why the laboratory schedule is so dense at first, and why each result needs someone to look at it the same day.

Ordinary surgical care still matters

Breathing and mobilityDeep breathing, early sitting and walking as soon as the team allows. Recipients are surgical patients at risk of lung collapse and pneumonia.
ClotsGive ordered clot prevention and check the legs. Report new swelling, especially in the leg on the side of the graft.
PainTreat pain well but avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which reduce blood flow to the new kidney. Use the agents the team orders.
The fistula armNo blood pressure cuffs or blood draws on the access arm. The fistula may still be needed if the graft is slow.
Catheter and drainKnow the planned removal days. After the catheter comes out, check that the patient voids and measure the volumes.
Teaching starts nowMedicine names, timing and the reasons for each can be introduced from the first days, while family members are present (Module 9).
On the transplant ward, day 4 · fictional

Miss Lagman's kidney is working well, with 4 liters of urine a day. Her morning results show potassium 3.1 mmol/L, magnesium 0.55 mmol/L and phosphate 0.5 mmol/L. Her glucose before lunch is 254 mg/dL (14.1 mmol/L). She feels weak and has leg cramps.

You report all four values together, with her urine output and her cramps, instead of one at a time. Dr. Santos orders replacement for all three electrolytes and adjusts her insulin. He explains to her that the low values come from a kidney that is working hard and a body that is still adjusting.

What this shows. Early electrolyte changes travel together and share causes. Report them as a picture, with the urine output and symptoms that explain them.

Evidence

What the trials say about fluids and stents

Balanced crystalloid versus saline. Normal saline contains 154 mmol/L of chloride, well above plasma. High chloride loads cause hyperchloremic acidosis and are thought to constrict the afferent arteriole through tubuloglomerular feedback, reducing kidney blood flow. That mechanism is plausible; the trial below is what shows the clinical effect. The BEST-Fluids trial randomized 808 deceased-donor recipients in Australia and New Zealand to a balanced low-chloride solution (Plasma-Lyte 148) or saline during surgery and for 48 hours. DGF occurred in 30 percent of the balanced-crystalloid group versus 40 percent of the saline group (adjusted relative risk 0.74; adjusted risk difference 10.1 percentage points), without more serious adverse events.2 An absolute reduction of about 10 points means roughly one fewer recipient needing early dialysis for every 10 treated. Delayed graft function in this trial meant needing dialysis within 7 days of transplant, an outcome patients feel. The primary result does not by itself show that the kidney lasts longer. This is high-quality randomized evidence; it applies most directly to deceased-donor transplants, and the fluid choice is the team's.

Ureteric stents. A Cochrane review of seven randomized trials (1,154 recipients) of low or moderate quality found that routine stenting reduced major urological complications such as leaks and obstruction (risk ratio 0.24; number needed to treat 13). Urinary tract infections were more common in stented patients (risk ratio 1.49) unless prophylactic cotrimoxazole 480 mg a day was given, in which case rates were similar.3 Stents are removed after a few weeks; the date must be tracked, because a forgotten stent becomes encrusted and infected.

In the transplant clinic · fictional

At the six-week visit, you notice that Jomar's chart has no stent removal date. He has had two urinary tract infections since discharge.

You check the operative note: a ureteric stent was placed. You flag it to the transplant urologist the same day, and the cystoscopy is booked for the following week.

What this shows. Every stent needs a removal date and an owner from the day it goes in.

Delayed graft function itself is associated with more rejection and worse short- and long-term outcomes, although a kidney with DGF can still function well for years.4 These associations come from observational data, so DGF marks a kidney at risk rather than proving that DGF alone causes the later problems. Patients with DGF need dialysis planning, careful fluid management and early surveillance for rejection, because a quiet kidney cannot signal rejection through falling urine.

Practice case · synthetic

Case: Two hours after transfer

4:10 p.m., transplant ward. Mr. Salazar, 41, arrives from the operating room after a deceased-donor kidney transplant. His native kidneys made about 300 mL a day before surgery. On arrival his blood pressure is 138/82 mmHg, heart rate 88, urine output 220 mL in the first hour, and the drain has 40 mL of light pink fluid.

5:10 p.m. Blood pressure 124/76, heart rate 96, urine 140 mL, drain 70 mL, now darker.

6:10 p.m. Blood pressure 102/64 mmHg, heart rate 112. Urine has fallen to 35 mL in the last hour. The drain has 180 mL of bright red fluid in the same hour. He says the pain over the graft is "getting worse, not better," and the dressing is tight over a new swelling. His wife asks if this is normal after surgery.

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, whom you would contact and what you would say, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the debrief below.

Assess

Airway, breathing, circulation; full set of vital signs; perfusion; catheter patency; drain volume and character over time; wound swelling; cumulative balance; latest hemoglobin.

Communicate

Activate the emergency pathway and notify the transplant surgeon immediately with an Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (SBAR) that leads with the trends: blood pressure and heart rate over two hours, urine and drain volumes per hour, pain and wound findings.

Teach

Explain to the patient calmly what is happening and that the team is coming; avoid speculating about losing the kidney.

Escalate

This pattern suggests bleeding; prepare for ordered blood tests, crossmatch for transfusion, imaging and possible return to theater. Do not give additional fluid beyond orders while waiting.
How it played out · fictional

The nurse in the story laid the three hourly readings side by side and saw one pattern, not three problems. She checked the catheter (no kink, no clot), then activated the emergency pathway. Her call to the surgeon opened with the trends: "Blood pressure down from 138 to 102 over two hours, pulse up from 88 to 112, urine 220 to 35 an hour, drain 40 pink to 180 bright red in the last hour, graft pain and new swelling."

While waiting, she did not open the fluid wide on her own; she followed the replacement order exactly and drew the ordered blood count and crossmatch. She told his wife that the team was coming to check for bleeding, without guessing about the kidney.

The surgeon arrived within ten minutes. Mr. Salazar returned to theater, where a bleeding vessel near the graft was tied off. The kidney recovered. The surgeon's comment at handover the next morning: "Your call gave me everything I needed in the first sentence."

Documentation task. Write a timed escalation note with the trend table, the time the surgeon was called, the instructions received and the next check time.
Second round · what changesAfter a catheter kink is corrected, urine output stays low and the pulse keeps rising; the drain is unchanged.

Supervised practice

With your preceptor

Under supervision on the transplant ward. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own protocols, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record.

  • Receive an OR-to-ward handoff using the structured checklist and record escalation parameters.
  • Maintain an hourly urine and fluid replacement chart for one shift and explain each entry to your preceptor.
  • Demonstrate catheter patency checks according to policy.
  • Present a trend-based SBAR for a simulated falling urine output.
  • Locate the stent removal date in a recipient's record and the system that tracks it.
