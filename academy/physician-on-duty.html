The doctor in the room

For four hours at a time, a hemodialysis (HD) machine takes over part of the work of two failed kidneys. Most sessions are uneventful. Some are not: blood pressure falls, a patient has chest pain, a fever starts, or an alarm will not clear. The physician on duty is the doctor in the room when that happens.

Most physicians on duty are not nephrologists. Medical school teaches the healthy kidney and the failing kidney, but very little about the patient who has no kidney function at all and lives by a machine three times a week. That patient breaks many rules of general medicine. A liter of intravenous fluid that would help another patient can put this one in pulmonary edema. A “normal” laboratory result may be alarming, and an alarming one may be this patient’s normal.

⚠️

What this series is, and is not

It is study material written independently from published guidelines, textbooks, and research. It is not a certificate, and it does not replace the requirements for physicians on duty in the Philippines. The Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) standards for hemodialysis clinics expect a physician on duty to hold a current advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) certificate and a certificate from a PSN-conducted or PSN-accredited course for physicians on duty, and to work under the supervision of the attending nephrologist and the clinical head of the unit.1 This series is not that course and is not affiliated with or endorsed by PSN. Use it to prepare, to review, and to support orientation in your own unit.

Who can hold this role

Physician-on-Duty: qualifications at a glance

  • PRC-licensed, in order of preference: nephrologist (board-certified, then board-eligible); internist or pediatrician with at least 3 months’ HDC experience; nephrology fellow or qualifying resident; general or family practitioner with at least 6 months’ experience. DOH, PSN
  • Current ACLS from a DOH-accredited provider, and a PSN-conducted or accredited post-graduate course for PODs. DOH, PSN
  • Present during all operating hours. Ratio no more than 1:15 stations (DOH); PSN allows up to 1:20 where PODs are scarce. DOH, PSN

Three tiers

Start with what you need for your first shift

The series has 32 modules in seven parts. You do not need all of them before your first duty. Each module is marked with one of three tiers.

Figure 2 · The three tiers

How each module works

The same seven steps every time

Figure 3 · How to work through a module

  1. ObjectivesRead what you should be able to do by the end.
  2. Big pictureA plain-words summary and the key terms, before any detail.
  3. Opening caseA fictional shift problem. Decide what you would do first.
  4. LessonShort parts with pictures, tables, and worked examples.
  5. Case reviewCompare your answer with one sound response.
  6. Skills checklistPractice with a preceptor on real or training equipment.
  7. Self-assessmentFive questions drawn from a larger bank, with explanations.

New terms are explained the first time they appear, and every page ends with a glossary. Abbreviations are spelled out at first use. If a sentence still reads like jargon, check the glossary, then ask your preceptor to show you the real object on the unit: most of this work makes sense faster when you can see and touch the equipment.

Assessment

Self-assessment is not sign-off

The questions at the end of each module are for practice. Each visit draws eight from a bank of fifteen, and every question explains its best answer. The questions were written for this series.

Competence is shown on the unit, not on a screen: examining an access, assessing a patient before a session, responding to a fall in blood pressure, and calling the attending nephrologist with a clear report. Each module has a skills checklist for a preceptor, usually the attending nephrologist or a senior physician on duty, to complete while watching you work.

End-of-course examination. When you have finished every module, take the end-of-course examination. It draws questions at random from all 32 modules, so no two sittings are the same.

Your record

Personal study record

Need to show your head nurse or preceptor how far you have gone? Make a one-page study record: the modules you finished, your best quiz score and the date you passed each, the time you spent on each module page, your end-of-course examination result, and the date each checklist was signed off. Each module with a skills or competency checklist has a Preceptor sign-off box at the end of that section; enter the date your preceptor signed it, or enter it in the table below. Print it or save it as a PDF.

It is a record of your own self-study, not a certificate and not CPD units, and it stays on this device. Your signed checklists and the unit’s competency form remain the official record.

Curriculum sources

What the series is built on

The series is built from published sources. Physiology follows standard textbooks.2,3 Clinical recommendations follow the guidelines of Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and its conference reports, and the research they rest on.4 Philippine expectations for physicians on duty come from the Department of Health (DOH) licensing order for dialysis facilities and the PSN standards for hemodialysis clinics.5,1 Each module lists its own sources.

Where the evidence is strong, the text says so. Where a practice rests on observation, expert opinion, or local custom, the text says that instead. Doses and cut-off values are left to your unit’s protocol and the attending nephrologist.

For trainers and preceptors

Using the series in a training program

An attending nephrologist, medical director, or senior physician on duty can use these modules as the guided-study part of unit orientation. The trainer and preceptor page gathers the proposed hours, completion criteria, and every skills checklist in one printable place. These are proposals for a provider to adapt and validate on its own equipment; they are not requirements of any regulator.
