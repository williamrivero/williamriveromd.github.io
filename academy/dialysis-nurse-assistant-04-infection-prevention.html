Infection Prevention and Exposure Safety

Supervised support role · not a registered-nurse specialty and not a technician equivalency

This subtrack prepares supervised support workers in adult maintenance hemodialysis: the Dialysis Nurse Assistant, also called the Patient Care Assistant (PCA). You work on the delegation of a registered nurse (RN). The course does not authorize assessment or treatment decisions, needle or catheter work, connection or disconnection, medicines, machine settings, alarm resetting, dialyzer reprocessing or water-system work. Your employer’s written job description, your signed-off competencies and the RN on duty decide what you may do. All people and cases are fictional.

Learning objectives · about 10 hours (4 study, 2 simulation, 4 supervised workplace) · week 2 · Observation only in the workplace

After this module you should be able to

  • Explain how hepatitis B and C travel between patients in a dialysis unit and why visible cleanliness is not enough.
  • Perform hand hygiene at the five moments and change gloves at the right times, and explain why gloves lower hand hygiene.
  • Plan work from clean to dirty and keep clean supplies away from contaminated items.
  • Handle sharps safely and never recap or sort used needles by hand.
  • Start the occupational exposure response immediately and report through the unit route.
Intuition

Invisible paint

Imagine that blood is a wet paint you cannot see. A tiny drop on a glove marks the blood pressure cuff. The cuff marks the next patient’s arm. The glove also marks the clamp, the chair arm, the pen, the supply tray. By the end of a shift, the paint has traveled from one station to five, and nobody saw it move.

The picture has a limit. Paint dries and stays where it is. Viruses dry too, but some stay infectious. Hepatitis B virus remained infectious after one week dried and stored at room temperature in a laboratory study.1 Hepatitis C also survives on surfaces for days or longer. A surface that looks clean can still carry an infectious dose.

In plain wordsClean hands at the right moments. Fresh gloves for each patient and each dirty task. Clean things never touch dirty things. Never recap a needle.
Evidence

How bloodborne viruses spread in dialysis units

Dialysis brings together many people whose blood leaves their bodies, for hours, three times a week, in a shared room. That is why dialysis units have had outbreaks of hepatitis B and C. In the United States, an analysis of health care-associated hepatitis outbreaks outside hospitals from 1998 to 2008 found repeated outbreaks in dialysis settings, linked to lapses in basic infection control.2

When investigators trace an outbreak, the story is usually ordinary. In one outpatient unit, eight new hepatitis C infections were traced to transmission between patients treated in the same or consecutive shifts at the same or nearby stations. Investigators saw several lapses, including in how injectable medicines were prepared and handled.3 In a French unit, contamination of the environment and failure to follow standard precautions were the main drivers of hepatitis C transmission.4

Prevention works. In a United States collaborative that introduced a bundle of core interventions (hand hygiene and glove audits, catheter and access care, staff competency and feedback), bloodstream infection rates fell 32% and access-related infections 54% at the start of the intervention period. The project had no control group, so other changes may have contributed.5,6

Practice and evidence

Hand hygiene and gloves

Use the World Health Organization’s five moments: before touching a patient, before a clean task, after a body-fluid exposure risk, after touching a patient, and after touching the patient’s surroundings.7 Alcohol handrub is the default; use soap and water when hands are visibly dirty.

Hand hygiene programs change outcomes. In a Geneva hospital, a campaign built around bedside alcohol handrub raised compliance from 48% to 66%, while hospital infection prevalence fell from 16.9% to 9.9%. The design was before-and-after, so not every change can be credited to hand hygiene, but nursing assistants were among the groups whose compliance improved.8

Gloves protect you, but they do not clean your hands, and they can trick you. In an observational study of 7,578 hand hygiene moments, wearing gloves was associated with lower hand hygiene compliance (adjusted odds ratio 0.65). Gloves were often worn when not needed and missing when needed.9

Gloves off, hands cleaned, every time. Change gloves between patients and between any dirty task and any clean task. Never touch your phone, pockets, the keyboard or clean supplies with used gloves.
Practice

Clean to dirty, and never back

Plan your movement so that you go from clean tasks to dirty tasks. Prepare supplies first, help the patient next, handle waste last. If you must go back to a clean task, stop, remove gloves, clean your hands, and start fresh.

  • Keep food and drinks out of treatment areas, for patients’ safety and yours.
  • Do not touch the access, dressings, catheter hubs, needles or connections to help. Access care is the registered nurse (RN)’s.
  • If a clean item touched a dirty surface or a used glove, set it aside and tell the RN. Never put it back in stock.
  • Patients with hepatitis B are dialyzed in an isolation room or area with dedicated equipment and staff, per Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Philippine practice.10 Never carry anything out of that area to the main floor.
Practice and evidence

Sharps and the exposure response

Needlestick injuries are common. A meta-analysis of 87 studies with 50,916 health workers estimated that 44.5% had at least one needlestick in a year, with the highest prevalence in the Southeast Asia region.11 Assistants handle waste, linen and trays where hidden sharps end up.

NeverNever recap a used needle. Never sort used sharps by hand or reach into a sharps container or waste bag. Never pick up a needle on a chair with your fingers: keep people away and call trained staff to use the sharps procedure.

If blood or body fluid enters a cut, a needlestick, your eyes, nose or mouth:

  1. First aid nowWash the wound with soap and water; flush eyes or mouth with plenty of clean water. Do not squeeze or scrub.
  2. Report nowTell the RN and use the unit’s exposure route immediately. Do not wait for the end of the shift.
  3. AssessmentA clinician assesses the exposure and the source, and decides on testing and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). Some PEP works best when started within hours.
  4. RecordComplete the exposure report factually.

Your hepatitis B vaccination and antibody status matter. CDC guidance sets out how health workers are evaluated for hepatitis B protection and managed after an exposure.12 Your occupational health service, not this course, decides what you need.

Practice case · fictional

Case: The same gloves

Monday, 9:35 a.m. Bea has just carried a full waste bag from Station 11 to the soiled holding area. On her way back, Nurse Jessa asks her to bring a new pack of gauze and a blood pressure cuff to Station 12 for Mr. Ramon Villanueva, who has just arrived. Bea opens the clean supply cabinet and has her hand on the gauze pack when she realizes she is still wearing the gloves she used for the waste bag.

Before reading on, decide what you would notice, whom you would tell and what you would say, what you would do within your role, and what would make you call for help at once. Then compare with the debrief below.

Notice

Used gloves have touched the cabinet handle and the gauze pack. The pack’s outside may now carry blood from the waste.

Communicate

“Ma’am Jessa, I touched the clean cabinet and this gauze pack with gloves I used for waste. I have set the pack aside. The cabinet handle needs cleaning.”

Do

Stop. Remove gloves, clean hands, set aside the gauze pack, take a fresh pack with clean hands, and bring the cuff only after hand hygiene.

Escalate

If she is unsure what else she touched, tell the RN so the cabinet can be checked before anyone uses it.
How it played out · fictional

The RN had the cabinet handle disinfected and the set-aside pack discarded. At the huddle, the head nurse moved the soiled holding route so it no longer passed the clean cabinet, and added a handrub dispenser at the soiled room door.

Documentation task. List everything Bea touched after the waste bag, and write a one-line log entry.

Performance evidence for this module: Observed hand hygiene and glove changes across a simulated workflow; correct donning and removal of personal protective equipment (PPE); separation of clean and dirty items; exposure response rehearsal.

Critical failure: Contaminating clean supplies without correcting it, recapping or hand-sorting sharps, or delaying the report of an exposure.
Supervised practice

With your preceptor

Practice in the skills lab with fluorescent gel or a marker, then observe in the unit. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own procedures, device instructions, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record. Proposed evidence: at least three satisfactory observations across two shifts for each workplace skill that applies to you.

  • Demonstrate hand hygiene and glove changes across a simulated four-task workflow.
  • Select, put on and remove PPE without contaminating skin or clothing.
  • Separate clean stock from contaminated equipment at a mock station.
  • Show the sharps container route without recapping or hand sorting.
  • Rehearse immediate first aid and exposure reporting.

Terms

Standard precautions
Basic infection control used with every patient: hand hygiene, gloves and other PPE by risk, safe sharps, clean surfaces.
Five moments
The World Health Organization’s five times for hand hygiene in patient care.
Isolation station
A separate room or area with dedicated equipment and staff for patients with hepatitis B.

Medicines given soon after an exposure to prevent infection.
