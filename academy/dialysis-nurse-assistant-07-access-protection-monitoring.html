Protect the Access and Observe During Treatment

Supervised support role · not a registered-nurse specialty and not a technician equivalency

This subtrack prepares supervised support workers in adult maintenance hemodialysis: the Dialysis Nurse Assistant, also called the Patient Care Assistant (PCA). You work on the delegation of a registered nurse (RN). The course does not authorize assessment or treatment decisions, needle or catheter work, connection or disconnection, medicines, machine settings, alarm resetting, dialyzer reprocessing or water-system work. Your employer’s written job description, your signed-off competencies and the RN on duty decide what you may do. All people and cases are fictional.

Learning objectives · about 10 hours (4 study, 2 simulation, 4 supervised workplace) · week 4 · Supervised practice after sign-off

After this module you should be able to

  • Recognize a fistula, graft and catheter at an awareness level, and protect each during support care.
  • Explain why a dislodged needle is an emergency and why the absence of an alarm is not reassurance.
  • Keep the access visible and the patient observed during treatment.
  • Report bleeding, displacement concerns, symptoms and alarm messages at once, with the exact facts.
  • State what you never touch: needles, tape, dressings, hubs, clamps, lines and machine controls.
Intuition

A garden hose under a blanket

Imagine a garden hose running at full pressure under a thick blanket. If the hose slips off the faucet, water pours out. But the blanket soaks it up. Nothing looks wrong for a while. By the time the water runs onto the floor, a lot has been lost.

The picture has a limit. In dialysis, the hose is carrying blood. The blood pump moves about a cup or more of blood every minute through the circuit. If the needle that returns blood to the patient slips out, the pump can keep pushing blood onto the bed. The machine watches pressure in the return line, but a needle lying in a pool of blood or a soaked blanket can keep that pressure high enough that the alarm does not sound. Experts describe venous needle dislodgement as one of the most serious accidents in dialysis: if the blood pump is not stopped, a patient can bleed to death within minutes.1

In plain wordsKeep the access arm uncovered and in view. Look at it every time you pass. Any wetness, blood, or a needle that looks out of place: call the registered nurse (RN) at once. Do not touch the lines.
Awareness

Three kinds of access, one rule

The access is the patient’s lifeline. Without it, there is no dialysis. Kidney teams work hard to create and preserve it; the national vascular access guideline puts great weight on protecting veins and the access over a patient’s whole life.2

Vascular access at an awareness level
AccessWhat it isWhat you protect
Arteriovenous fistula (AVF)An artery joined to a vein, usually in the arm; you can feel a buzz (thrill)No cuff, tight sleeve, watch or bag strap on that arm; no lying on it; tell the RN if the buzz is gone
Arteriovenous graft (AVG)A soft tube under the skin joining artery and veinThe same as a fistula
Central venous catheterA tube in a large neck or chest vein, covered with a dressingNever touch the hubs, caps or dressing; keep it dry; report fever or shivering at once

Follow the RN’s plan for positioning and for which arm to use for blood pressure (Module 5). Never inspect a dressing or connection by moving it.

Evidence and practice

Keep the access visible

European dialysis nurses’ recommendations for preventing and catching venous needle dislodgement include keeping the needle sites and lines visible throughout treatment, securing the needles and lines well, and regular checks by staff.1 Securing the needles is the RN’s job. Keeping the site in view and noticing is where you help most.

She is cold and pulls the blanket up

Why it matters
A blanket over the arm hides blood and soaks it up.
What you do
Offer a second blanket for the body and shoulders and fold it back from the access arm. Explain why, kindly.

He is restless and keeps moving the arm

Why it matters
Movement can pull a needle; restlessness can also be a sign of low blood pressure or blood loss.
What you do
Stay with him and call the RN now. Do not restrain the arm yourself.
No alarm does not mean no problem. Your eyes are the second safety system.
Practice

Observe the patient, not only the machine

On your assigned rounds, ask how the patient feels and take the delegated observations. Watch for change. Many problems during dialysis show first in the patient.

What you see, what it might mean, what you do
What you noticeWhat it might meanWhat you do
Yawning, cramps, dizziness, nausea, sweatingBlood pressure falling as fluid is removedTell the RN now; stay
Wetness or blood near the access; a needle or tape out of placeBleeding or dislodgementCall the RN and the emergency route at once (below)
Shivering, fever, feeling cold and unwell, often with a catheterInfection in the bloodTell the RN now
New chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, drowsinessA serious problemCall the RN at once; Module 8

Write the actual findings and the time. Never write “stable” in place of what you observed; “stable” is an assessment, and assessment is the RN’s.

Practice

When an alarm sounds or you see blood

  1. Look at the patientAwake? Breathing? Any blood or a displaced line?
  2. CallThe RN, by name, with the station, what you see and the exact screen message if you can read it without touching anything.
  3. Major bleedingCall the emergency route at once and do exactly what your practical unit training taught you, under the team’s direction.
  4. StayKeep the access visible, ask the patient to keep still, keep others back.
NeverNever silence or reset an alarm, change a limit, clamp or unclamp a line, reconnect a detached line, re-tape a needle or stop the blood pump on your own, unless your unit has trained and authorized you in an emergency step through its practical protocol. Monitoring must never become managing the circuit.

The RN stops the pump and controls bleeding. Your speed in noticing and calling is what gives the RN time to act.

Practice case · fictional

Case: A wet blanket, no alarm

Wednesday, 8:40 a.m. Nanay Lorna Bautista, 77, is in her second hour with a left arm fistula. She complained of the cold and pulled her blanket over both arms. Bea, passing on her round, notices that the blanket near her left elbow looks darker and damp. Nanay Lorna is drowsier than usual and mumbles that she is fine. The machine is quiet. Nurse Jessa is two stations away.

Before reading on, decide what you would notice, whom you would tell and what you would say, what you would do within your role, and what would make you call for help at once. Then compare with the debrief below.

Notice

Damp, dark blanket at the access plus new drowsiness may mean blood loss from a dislodged needle. A quiet machine is not reassurance.

Communicate

“Ma’am Jessa, Station 5 now! Nanay Lorna’s blanket is wet at the fistula and she is drowsy.” If the RN does not come at once, use the emergency call.

Do

Fold back the blanket to uncover the arm without touching the lines, keep her still, stay, and keep others back.

Escalate

If there is visible active bleeding or she becomes unresponsive, activate the emergency response and follow your practical training under direction.
How it played out · fictional

The venous needle had partly pulled back. The RN stopped the blood pump and controlled the bleeding; the physician assessed her and ordered a blood count. She recovered. At the review, the unit agreed that blankets go over the shoulders and body, never the access arm, and added a visual check of every access to each assistant round.

Documentation task. Write the factual record of what you saw, what time, and whom you called.

Competencies

Performance evidence for this module: Detection of simulated bleeding under a blanket and an immediate RN call; correct positioning and covering that keep the access visible; factual recording of observations.

Critical failure: Missing or delaying the report of possible blood loss, covering the access, or manipulating the needles, lines or machine.
Supervised practice

With your preceptor

Practice in simulation with a mock arm and moulage blood, then on supervised rounds. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own procedures, device instructions, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record. Proposed evidence: at least three satisfactory observations across two shifts for each workplace skill that applies to you.

  • Identify fistula, graft and catheter on a model and name what to protect for each.
  • Cover a cold patient while keeping the access arm visible.
  • Detect simulated bleeding under a blanket and call the RN with the exact facts.
  • Read and relay an alarm message without touching the controls.
  • Record observations as facts, without “stable.”

Abbreviations

AVF
Arteriovenous fistula — an artery joined to a vein in the arm, used for dialysis needles
AVG
Arteriovenous graft — a tube joining an artery to a vein, used for dialysis needles

Basic life support.
BP
Blood pressure.
PCA
Patient Care Assistant — the title used for the Dialysis Nurse Assistant in this course; never “Patient Care Technician”
RN
Registered nurse.

Terms

Thrill
The buzz felt over a working fistula or graft.
Venous needle dislodgement
The needle that returns blood to the patient slipping out during treatment.
Blood pump
The part of the machine that moves blood through the circuit.
Catheter hub
The end of a dialysis catheter where lines connect; only trained staff touch it.
