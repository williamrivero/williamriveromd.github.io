Before you begin

How this examination works

This examination covers the whole Dialyzer Reprocessing Technician series. Each sitting draws 40 questions at random from a bank of 144, in random order, with the answer choices shuffled. Choose the single best answer for each question, then select Check answers.

When to take itAfter you have finished every module and reviewed the questions you missed.
How to take itIn one sitting, without notes. Allow about one minute per question.
Proposed pass mark32 of 40 best answers (80 percent). Your trainer may set a different mark.
If you fall shortEach explanation names its module. Reread those modules, then select New set of questions.
What a pass meansA pass shows that you know the material. It does not show that you can do the work. Competence is demonstrated on your unit’s own equipment and signed off by a preceptor.

End-of-course examination

Question 1A biomedical technician scores 5 of 5 on the common knowledge module. What may he now do?
A knowledge pass is one of several states. Release authority is granted by the facility after an observed practical. Module 1.
Question 2Who decides whether a patient is eligible for dialyzer reuse?
Eligibility is a clinical program decision. The technician holds any dialyzer whose eligibility is unknown. Module 1.
Question 3A nurse asks for a dialyzer whose record has a missing test result. What is the safest response?
An unproven gate is a failed gate until proven otherwise. A new dialyzer protects the patient today. Module 1.
Question 4Which task is within a reprocessing operator’s usual scope?
Labeling and storage are core operator duties. The others belong to nurses, physicians or service staff. Module 1.
Question 5Why must two disagreeing staff members not settle the question by using the dialyzer?
Quarantine first, then a written decision by the person who owns it. Module 1.
Question 6What does an observed practical assessment show that a quiz cannot?
A quiz tests knowledge. Only observation shows performance on the unit’s own reprocessor. Module 1.
Question 7A job posting lists the title “adding machine technician” but describes dialyzer reprocessing. What should you rely on?
Titles vary between employers. The duties tell you what the job is. Module 1.
Question 8According to the improvement report from a United States unit, where are most failed dialyzers made?
Standardizing heparin and rinsing on the floor raised the average number of uses. It was a single-unit report, not a trial. Module 1.
Question 9Which report from the reuse room is most useful to the head nurse?
A useful report gives what, when, where it may come from, and what was done, without blame. Module 1.
Question 10What does the DOH licensing order list among dialysis technician duties?
administrative order (AO) 2012-0001 lists the reuse-room tasks among technician duties. Module 1.
Question 11Which statement about this track’s place in the Academy is correct?
Placement tells learners where to find the track. It confers no nursing or clinical scope. Module 1.
Question 12You are asked to photograph a dialyzer label to send to a colleague on a personal phone. What do you do?
Labels carry patient identity and sometimes infection status. Use approved channels only. Module 1.
Question 13Why does a clotted fiber reduce the dose of dialysis?
Diffusion depends on surface. Closed fibers remove surface. Module 2.
Question 14In the HEMO analysis, how much did urea clearance change with reuse?
A small, consistent fall regardless of membrane or method. Module 2.
Question 15In the same analysis, what happened to beta-2-microglobulin clearance?
Middle-molecule clearance depends on the membrane material and the reprocessing technique. Module 2.
Question 16What did the 2012 systematic review conclude about reuse and death?
Fourteen observational studies; very low quality; no consistent difference. Module 2.
Question 17What kind of study was the 2011 report of facilities that stopped peracetic acid reuse?
Before-and-after designs cannot exclude changes over time, so the finding is an association. Module 2.
Question 18A dialyzer’s volume has gone 92%, 88%, 84%, 81%. What is the best action?
The trend is information for the floor and the nephrologist. The baseline is never reset. Module 2.
Question 19Where does most fiber clotting begin?
Clotting happens while blood flows. Heparin and rinsing at the end of treatment matter. Module 2.
Question 20What did the 2025 study of super high-flux dialyzer reuse show?
Five patients, 15 dialyzers: beta-2-microglobulin kept, other toxins fell. A small study only suggests. Module 2.
Question 21Which reason for reuse has largely faded with modern membranes?
First-use reactions were mainly a problem of older cellulose membranes. Module 2.
Question 22What does total cell volume estimate?
The blood-compartment volume shrinks as fibers close. Module 2.
Question 23A colleague says reuse is proven safe. What is the most accurate reply?
The honest summary is uncertainty about outcomes and certainty that execution failures cause harm. Module 2.
Question 24Which outcome is a surrogate?
Clearance is a laboratory measure. It matters, but it is not an outcome the patient feels. Module 2.
Question 25A dialyzer has been used for four treatments. Under a five-use ceiling, how many more treatments may it be used for if every gate passes?
Five uses total. Four done, one left. Module 3.
Question 26“Five uses” under the PhilHealth package means:
The count includes the first treatment. Module 3.
Question 27A dialyzer failed its first processing cycle and was processed again before passing. What changed?
A processing cycle is not a treatment. Module 3.
Question 28The IFU allows three uses; PhilHealth pays for up to five. How many uses are allowed?
The strictest rule that applies wins. Module 3.
Question 29What is PhilHealth Circular 2024-0029’s volume criterion?
At least 80% of baseline, as one of several criteria. Module 3.
Question 30Under PSN 2024 guidance, dialyzer reuse for a patient with HIV is:
The Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) guidance does not allow dialyzer reuse in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Module 3.
Question 31Which statement about the United States federal reuse rule is correct?
The US rule excludes hepatitis B positive patients. It does not apply to Philippine units. Module 3.
Question 32A patient’s hepatitis C result is missing from the transfer folder. What do you do with his dialyzer?
Unknown status holds. Eligibility is decided by the clinical team. Module 3.
Question 33What does a PhilHealth circular decide?
A payer rule decides what is paid for, not whether something is safe. Module 3.
Question 34You find a 2018 regional standard in a drawer that mentions very high reuse numbers. What should you do?
Always check edition, issuer and applicability. Module 3.
Question 35Where does the exact germicide contact time come from?
Chemistry comes from the product instructions and the validated procedure. Module 3.
Question 36A dialyzer arrives from another facility with a handwritten label and no processing record. Under most programs it is:
Without your unit’s record, nothing about its history is proven. Module 3.
Question 37How long can hepatitis B virus survive on surfaces at room temperature?
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes survival for at least seven days, which is why surfaces matter. Module 4.
Question 38In the Vietnam hepatitis C outbreak, what was the most likely route for most cases?
Investigators judged indirect contact likely for 10 of 11 cases. Module 4.
Question 39Which arrangement in the Vietnam unit increased risk?
Clean work beside dirty work invites contamination. Module 4.
Question 40Your face shield fogs during header cleaning. What do you do?
A lifted shield protects nothing. Fix the equipment. Module 4.
Question 41What is the first action after blood splashes into your eye?
Flush first, report second, record third. Module 4.
Question 42Under the academy default, a patient’s hepatitis B surface antigen has turned positive. What happens to his dialyzer?
A change in infection status stops reprocessing. The clinical team decides the plan. Module 4.
Question 43Where should a patient’s infection status be confirmed?
Labels identify; the clinical record holds the diagnosis. Module 4.
Question 44Which is the main reason the reuse room needs one-way flow?
Layout prevents contamination when people are busy. Module 4.
Question 45What does the CDC recommend for susceptible dialysis staff?
Vaccination protects staff against hepatitis B. Module 4.
Question 46A colleague cleans a cut on her hand with bleach after an injury. Is that correct?
Bleach damages skin. Washing and prompt reporting are the right steps. Module 4.
Question 47Why should an exposure be reported within the hour?
Delay can close the window for preventive treatment. Module 4.
Question 48Which item should never be in the reuse room?
Eating and drinking where blood and chemicals are handled is a route of exposure. Module 4.
Question 49Why must reprocessing water meet dialysis water quality?
Anything left in the fibers meets the blood. Module 5.
Question 50What can cause fever and chills even when bacteria are dead?
Endotoxin from bacterial walls triggers fever and chills. Module 5.
Question 51What happened at Caruaru, Brazil, in 1996?
Water reaches the blood, and anything in it reaches the patient. Module 5.
Question 52Under the DOH order, how often is product-water TDS measured?
Weekly, with corrective action above 10 mg/L. Module 5.
Question 53Under the DOH order, how often at minimum is microbiology tested at the point of use?
Before operation, then at least monthly. Module 5.
Question 54What is an action level?
It warns while the water still meets the limit. Module 5.
Question 55A water sample bottle was opened before it was labeled, and the outlet name was forgotten. What is the result worth?
Label before opening. Module 5.
Question 56Where does persistent bacterial contamination of a water loop usually come from?
Biofilm shelters bacteria and sheds endotoxin. Module 5.
Question 57You find an unsigned failed result in the water log at the start of a shift. What do you do?
An unresolved failure must be addressed before any processing. Module 5.
Question 58In the Hanoi report, what were the bacteria found in patients mostly like?
Waterborne organisms point to water or reprocessing. Module 5.
Question 59According to a CDC review of dialysis outbreaks, most were:
Execution, again, is the lesson. Module 5.
Question 60Who is accountable for water quality under the DOH order?
The medical director is accountable; the technician samples, reads and reports. Module 5.
Question 61In the 1988 CDC investigation, what caused the uneven germicide strength?
The concentrate layered: weak on top, strong at the bottom. Module 6.
Question 62Which check would have caught the weak germicide before storage?
Only a potency or presence test proves strength. Module 6.
Question 63To make 10 L at 2.5% from a 50% stock, how much concentrate is needed?
V1 = (2.5 × 10) ÷ 50 = 0.5 L. Module 6.
Question 64What does the dilution formula not tell you?
Arithmetic predicts; testing proves. Module 6.
Question 65Where do the germicide concentration and contact time come from?
No concentration on this page is a working value. Module 6.
Question 66Which germicide is a strong sensitizer associated with occupational asthma?
Glutaraldehyde sensitizes skin and airways. Module 6.
Question 67Mixing bleach with an acid germicide releases:
Never mix chemicals outside the SOP’s exact sequence. Module 6.
Question 68What did the NIOSH studies of hospital staff using peroxide and peracetic acid products find?
Observational, but consistent: these vapors irritate airways. Module 6.
Question 69You can smell germicide strongly throughout your shift. What does that suggest?
Persistent odor means exposure. Module 6.
Question 70Which SDS section tells you what glove material to wear?
Exposure controls lists protective equipment. Module 6.
Question 71An automated reprocessor dilutes germicide in-line. Do you still need potency or presence checks?
The same proof is needed whatever the system. Module 6.
Question 72Why can a weak germicide cause fever in the next patient?
Weak disinfection lets bacteria grow; endotoxin causes pyrogenic reactions. Module 6.
Question 73Why is a wrong-patient dialyzer serious even if it was perfectly cleaned?
Identity failure exposes the patient and invalidates the history. Module 7.
Question 74A nurse asks for “Santos’s dialyzer.” What do you do?
A surname alone is not identification. Module 7.
Question 75When should the label be applied?
Labeling before first use anchors the history. Module 7.
Question 76A label is smudged and you cannot read the second identifier. What do you do?
Unidentified dialyzers are quarantined. Module 7.
Question 77How should a dialyzer be picked from storage?
Position and model are not identity. Module 7.
Question 78What should never appear on a label that other patients can read?
Protect privacy; use a coded marker only if the program requires one. Module 7.
Question 79A dialyzer’s use count is unclear after a record error. What is the safest course?
A broken history cannot prove eligibility. Module 7.
Question 80Why does the record show who received the dialyzer at each step?
Traceability depends on a named person at every hand-over. Module 7.
Question 81Which practice best protects patients with similar names?
Several layers together prevent mix-ups. Module 7.
Question 82Under the United States federal rule, dialyzer reuse is limited to:
Same-patient reuse is required. Module 7.
Question 83May a label be moved from a cracked dialyzer to a new one of the same model?
Labels never move between devices. Module 7.
Question 84How should used dialyzers travel to the reuse room?
Transport contains blood and keeps clean and dirty apart. Module 7.
Question 85Why does time before processing matter?
Residue and bacteria make cleaning less effective and raise endotoxin. Module 8.
Question 86Where do the maximum time to processing and the refrigeration rules come from?
No number on this page is a working limit. Module 8.
Question 87What does reverse ultrafiltration do?
It cleans the inside of the fibers by backflow. Module 8.
Question 88Where does clot often collect in a used dialyzer?
Blood enters the fibers at the headers. Module 8.
Question 89A dialyzer has been in a warm hallway for three hours. Your procedure’s limit is shorter. What do you do?
A dialyzer outside the time limit is not processed. Module 8.
Question 90What may a different cleaning agent do to a membrane?
The Hemodialysis (HEMO) Study (HEMO) data showed that technique changes membrane behavior. Module 8.
Question 91A colleague suggests adding an extra bleach step to get dialyzers cleaner. What is the correct response?
A stronger clean is not a better clean. Module 8.
Question 92What may be stored in the used-dialyzer refrigerator?
Mixing food, medicines or clean items invites contamination. Module 8.
Question 93According to the FDA, what should happen when reprocessing or required refrigeration is not working?
Without functioning facilities, reuse stops. Module 8.
Question 94Which check must be done before starting a processing run?
A run on an unproven system makes every dialyzer in it unproven. Module 8.
Question 95When should each cleaning step be recorded?
Records made later from memory are not evidence. Module 8.
Question 96The IFU for a dialyzer model says the headers must not be removed. What do you do?
The IFU sets what is allowed for that model. Module 8.
Question 97Baseline 100 mL, measured 85 mL. What is the retained TCV?
85 ÷ 100 × 100 = 85%. Module 9.
Question 98Baseline 100 mL, measured 79 mL, floor 80%. What is the decision on volume?
79% is below the floor. Module 9.
Question 99A dialyzer has 90% retained volume but failed its integrity test. What happens?
Each gate is independent. Module 9.
Question 100A record has no baseline TCV. What do you do?
Without a valid baseline the result means nothing. Module 9.
Question 101Measured volume is above the baseline. What is the correct response?
A value above baseline suggests an error. Module 9.
Question 102What does a pressure-hold test detect?
A leak lets pressure fall faster than allowed. Module 9.
Question 103What did the PhilHealth quality circular set for retained TCV?
At least 80%, as one criterion among several. Module 9.
Question 104Your colleague repeats a failed volume test four times and records only the passing result. What is wrong?
Selective recording destroys the evidence. Module 9.
Question 105Several dialyzers today gave inconsistent volume readings. What should you suspect and do?
A pattern across dialyzers points to the machine. Module 9.
Question 106Why is a catalog volume not used as the baseline?
Each dialyzer is its own reference. Module 9.
Question 107A dialyzer passes volume, integrity and inspection, but its processing record has a blank where the reprocessor run number should be. Is it ready?
An unlinked record cannot prove the run’s water, germicide and machine checks. Module 9.
Question 108A dialyzer at exactly 80% retained TCV means:
Meeting one floor is not release. Module 9.
Question 109Why must there be no air pockets after filling with germicide?
Contact is required for disinfection. Module 10.
Question 110A dialyzer was filled two hours ago. The IFU minimum contact time is longer than that. May it be used now?
The window opens only after the contact time. Module 10.
Question 111Filled Monday 14:00, minimum contact 11 h (training values). What is the earliest use?
14:00 + 11 h = 01:00 the next day. Module 10.
Question 112A dialyzer’s fill time is missing from its label and log. What do you do?
No fill time, no window. Module 10.
Question 113The residual strip is read two minutes late. What does the result mean?
Timing is part of the test. Module 10.
Question 114A residual strip shows a faint color. What is the correct interpretation?
Doubt is not a negative. Module 10.
Question 115Which strip should be used for a dialyzer disinfected with a peracetic acid mixture?
Each germicide needs its own test. Module 10.
Question 116What makes a second person a real second check?
Independent verification catches what one person misses. Module 10.
Question 117A stored dialyzer is found with a missing cap and a damp shelf. What do you do?
Leakage breaks the proof of disinfection. Module 10.
Question 118Why are processed dialyzers stored in individual labeled positions?
Storage protects identity. Module 10.
Question 119What is the purpose of the rinse before treatment?
Germicide in the blood is a chemical injury. Module 10.
Question 120During handoff, the nurse and technician disagree about whether a result is acceptable. What happens?
Doubt stops connection. Module 10.
Question 121A reprocessor’s pressure sensor is found reading high. What does that mean for recent integrity tests?
Integrity depends on accurate pressure measurement. Module 11.
Question 122After a fault is found, which dialyzers are in question?
The trace-back window starts at the last proof that the equipment was good. Module 11.
Question 123Who returns a repaired reprocessor to service?
Return to service is a service decision. Module 11.
Question 124Does returning the reprocessor to service release the dialyzers processed while it was faulty?
Repairing the machine does not prove past results. Module 11.
Question 125Two patients have chills in the first hour of treatment on the same day, both with reprocessed dialyzers. What does the reuse room do?
A possible cluster needs evidence kept and records checked. Module 11.
Question 126A daily check written only as “OK” is weak because:
Evidence needs the reading. Module 11.
Question 127Which task is outside the reprocessing operator’s scope?
Internal repair belongs to authorized service staff. Module 11.
Question 128What does the FDA advise when reprocessing or required refrigeration is not working?
Reuse stops when the proving system is broken. Module 11.
Question 129Power fails in the middle of a reprocessing cycle. What happens to the dialyzers in that cycle?
An interrupted cycle proves nothing. Module 11.
Question 130During a water treatment failure, patients:
Treatment continues; reuse waits for proof. Module 11.
Question 131Why does the CDC advise tracking pyrogenic reactions over time?
Surveillance finds clusters early. Module 11.
Question 132What should a continuity plan for the reuse room say?
Planning prevents unsafe improvisation. Module 11.
Question 133When should a processing record be completed?
Records made later from memory are not evidence. Module 12.
Question 134How do you correct an error in a paper record?
The original must stay readable. Module 12.
Question 135In a month, 440 treatments used 100 dialyzers. What is the average number of uses?
440 ÷ 100 = 4.4. Module 12.
Question 1362 pyrogenic reactions in 1,600 treatments is how many per 1,000 treatments?
2 ÷ 1,600 × 1,000 = 1.25. Module 12.
Question 137Why is a rising average use number not a quality target?
A result of good care becomes dangerous as a goal. Module 12.
Question 138Average uses rose and volume failures almost disappeared, while reactions appeared. What is the best response?
Read the numbers together. Module 12.
Question 139What did the improvement report from a United States unit show raised average uses safely?
Upstream care, not looser tests. Module 12.
Question 140In the capstone, RU-1104 has baseline 96 mL and measured 77 mL. What is the retained TCV?
77 ÷ 96 × 100 = 80.2%, meeting the floor only. Module 12.
Question 141In the capstone, after Monday’s treatment RU-1104 has had five uses. What happens at its next return?
No sixth use, whatever the tests. Module 12.
Question 142How should germicide in a discarded dialyzer be handled?
Follow the SDS and the waste procedure. Module 12.
Question 143What did the HEMO secondary analysis suggest about extensive reuse?
An association that argues against treating more uses as better. Module 12.
Question 144You are offered a bonus if the average uses reaches 4.8. What should you do?
Incentives tied to uses can make the room unsafe. Module 12.
