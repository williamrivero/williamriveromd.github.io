Study series for dialysis water treatment technicians

This module is study material for dialysis water treatment technicians. It explains the reasons behind safe practice; it does not replace your unit’s approved procedures, the manufacturer’s instructions for your equipment, or the limits your facility has adopted. Limits quoted here come from the international standard and are given so the numbers make sense; your facility’s written limits and action levels are the ones you work to. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Tell apart free chlorine, chloramine, and total chlorine.
  • Explain empty-bed contact time and why two carbon tanks are placed in series.
  • Perform a total chlorine test correctly, and act on the result at each sample port.
  • Describe carbon replacement and the checks required before the system is released.

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsThink of the last checkpoint before a mall entrance, the one where the guard opens every bag. Everything that got past the street and the parking lot still has to pass this one table. Of everything in the water room, the carbon tanks are that table. They stand most directly between a poison and the patient.

The picture has a limit. A guard who misses something at the mall entrance can still be backed up by other guards inside. The carbon tanks have no such backup further down the line. Chlorine and chloramine cross the dialyzer membrane and destroy red blood cells, and nothing after the carbon removes them reliably. This module is about one tank arrangement and one test, done properly, several times a day, for as long as the unit runs.

Figure 1 · Key terms for this module

Free chlorineChlorine that is not combined with anything.
ChloramineChlorine combined with ammonia. Also called combined chlorine.
Total chlorineFree chlorine plus chloramine.
Empty-bed contact timeThe minutes of contact between the water and the carbon.
Worker and polisherThe first and second carbon tanks in series.
BreakthroughChlorine passing through a tank.
ReagentThe chemical in the test that produces the color.
Opening case · fictional

Case: A row of ticks

It is Monday, 05:30. Mr. Dennis Aquino, 27, a newly hired water technician, takes over the water room today. The chlorine log for the past month shows a tick in every box, the same initials, and no numbers. The test strips in the drawer expired four months ago. He tests with a fresh kit. The result is 0.06 mg/L after the first carbon tank and less than 0.02 mg/L after the second. The first patients are due at 06:15, and the charge nurse, Ms. Rowena Santiago, asks whether she can start.

Before reading on, decide:

  • Can the past month’s log be relied on, and what do you do about it?
  • What does today’s result tell you, and may treatments start?
  • What do you report, and what should change so this does not happen again?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Free, combined, and total chlorine

A utility disinfects water in one of two ways. Some add chlorine alone. Others add chlorine and then ammonia, which together form chloramine. Chloramine is a weaker disinfectant than chlorine, but it lasts longer, so it survives the long journey through a city’s pipes.

Total chlorine = free chlorine + chloramine (combined chlorine)
Free chlorineChloramine
Removed by carbonQuicklyMore slowly; needs the full contact time
Detected by a free chlorine testYesNo
Detected by a total chlorine testYesYes
Harm in dialysisDamages red blood cellsDamages red blood cells; the more common cause of patient injury

A utility can change from chlorine to chloramine, or raise the dose, without telling you. A unit that tests only for free chlorine would see nothing at all. The practical point is that the test is always for total chlorine, and the limit of 0.1 mg/L applies to the total.1

Lesson · part 2

Carbon tanks: the chlorine barrier

Activated carbon is charcoal processed so that each grain has an enormous internal surface. Chlorine and chloramine are taken out of the water as it passes over that surface. The removal takes time, so the water must stay in contact with the carbon long enough for it to finish.

Empty-bed contact time (minutes) = volume of the carbon bed ÷ water flow rate.
Example: a tank holding 300 liters of carbon with water flowing at 30 liters per minute gives 300 ÷ 30 = 10 minutes.

Guidance commonly calls for two carbon tanks in series with a total contact time of at least 10 minutes at the highest flow, about 5 minutes in each.2,3 Chloramine is harder to remove than free chlorine and needs the full contact time. The practical point is that contact time is set by the busiest moment of the day, not the average one.

Figure 4 · Why two tanks in series

The workerThe first tank removes nearly all the chlorine, and it is used up first.
The sample port between themTesting here tells you when the worker is becoming exhausted.
The polisherThe second tank protects patients while the worker is replaced.
BreakthroughChlorine passing through a tank. After the worker it is a warning; after the polisher it is a stop.

Carbon tanks are backwashed so that water does not cut channels through the bed. Backwashing does not restore exhausted carbon; exhausted carbon is replaced. Carbon is also a comfortable home for bacteria, because it has removed the chlorine that would have kept them down. That is one reason the stages after it must control microbes.

Stop and reportTotal chlorine above the limit after the second carbon tank means dialysis on that water stops until it is corrected and an authorized person releases the system.
Lesson · part 3

The total chlorine test, step by step

The total chlorine test is the most important test a water technician performs, because it is the one that tells you the carbon is still protecting patients. Units test before the first patient of the day and then at set intervals, commonly before each patient shift or every four hours. Follow your own procedure.2,3

Figure 6 · Performing the test

  1. Run the systemThe system must already be running, so that the sample is water that passed through the carbon at working flow.
  2. Choose the portSample after the first (worker) tank. If chlorine is found there, sample after the second.
  3. Flush the portLet water run for the stated time, so you are not testing water that sat still in the tap.
  4. Use a total chlorine testA free chlorine test will miss chloramine. Check the kit’s name and expiry date.
  5. Time itRead the color at exactly the time the kit states. Too early or too late gives a wrong answer.
  6. Record the numberWrite the value and unit, the port, the time, and your name.

Figure 7 · Acting on the result

At or below the limit after the worker tankRecord the result and release the water.
Above the limit after the worker tankTest after the polisher at once. Tell the responsible person, because the worker tank needs replacement. Test more often, as your procedure states.
Above the limit after the polisherStop. No treatments on this water. Tell the charge nurse and the medical director immediately.

The limit in the international standard is 0.1 mg/L of total chlorine.1 Your test must be sensitive enough to read at that level. A swimming pool test kit is not.

Stop and reportNever write a result you did not see. A test that was skipped is recorded as not done, with the reason, and reported.
Lesson · part 4

Getting the test right every time

A chlorine test is only as good as the way it is done. Each error below makes the result wrong, and several of them make it read falsely low, which is the dangerous direction.

ErrorEffect on the resultHow to avoid it
Sampling before the system has runFalsely low: the water sat in the carbon for hoursRun the system for the time your procedure states
Not flushing the portDoes not represent the water in serviceFlush for the stated time
Free chlorine reagent or strip usedMisses chloramine entirelyCheck that the kit says total chlorine
Expired or badly stored reagentFalsely low, or no color at allCheck the dates; store cool, dry, and closed
Wrong sample volumeColor too strong or too weakFill exactly to the mark
Reading too early or too lateWrong valueUse a timer, not a guess
Dirty vial, or fingers on the glassFalse color or cloudinessRinse the vial with sample water; hold it by the top
Reading the color in poor lightShade misjudgedRead against white in good light, or use a meter
RememberOnce a week, test the feed water with the same kit. It should read clearly positive. If it does not, the kit cannot be trusted for any of that week’s results.

Two kinds of test are common. Color-comparison kits and strips are quick and cheap, but they depend on your eyes. A handheld meter (colorimeter) reads the color electronically and gives a number; it needs its own zero check and periodic verification against a standard. Either is acceptable if it can read reliably at 0.1 mg/L.

Lesson · part 5

Replacing carbon and returning to service

Carbon cannot be recharged in place. When the worker tank shows breakthrough, or at the interval the unit has set, the carbon is replaced. A common practice is to move the polisher into the worker position and put the fresh carbon in the polisher position. That way the freshest carbon is always the last barrier before the patient.

Figure 8 · From exhausted carbon to release

  1. PlanNo patients on dialysis. Tell the charge nurse.
  2. ReplaceNew, virgin carbon of the specified grade. Regenerated carbon is not used for dialysis.
  3. RinseTo drain until the water runs clear of black fines, by the supplier’s method.
  4. Check downstreamInspect or change the fine filter.
  5. TestTotal chlorine after each tank, with the system running at working flow.
  6. Record and releaseDate, carbon type and batch, test results, and your name.

Carbon is specified by grade. Dialysis systems commonly call for granular carbon with a high adsorption rating (an iodine number of 900 or more is a usual specification) and, where the feed contains chloramine, a type made for chloramine removal. Use what the system designer specified. A cheaper substitute can be exhausted within weeks.4,3

Stop and reportIf chlorine is above the limit after the second tank, no dialysis starts on this water. There is no workaround in the water room. The medical director decides what happens next.
Lesson · part 6

The fine filter and the handover to reverse osmosis

A cartridge filter of about 5 micrometers usually sits after the carbon tanks. It catches carbon dust and resin fragments that would otherwise plug the membrane. Record its pressure drop, and change it on schedule or when the drop reaches the stated value. A new cartridge that clogs within days is telling you something: a stage upstream is shedding, and that stage needs to be found.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

Black water after new carbon

What you see
On Sunday, after the carbon is replaced, the water running from the tank’s rinse line is gray and full of fine black particles.
What is likely going on
New carbon sheds dust until it has been rinsed thoroughly.
What to do
Rinse to drain until the water runs clear, by the supplier’s method. Check the fine filter, and test total chlorine at both ports before the system is released.
Why it matters
Carbon dust plugs the cartridge filter and then the membrane.

The strip that never changes color

What you see
Reviewing the log on a Friday, you see that every chlorine test for a month has read zero. The feed water has never been tested with the same strips.
What is likely going on
The strips may be expired, the wrong type, or not sensitive enough to show a positive.
What to do
Test the feed water with the same kit. It should show chlorine. If it does not, the kit is at fault: replace it and report the gap in testing.
Why it matters
A test that cannot read positive is not a test.

Tested too early

What you see
At 5:45 in the morning, a colleague switches the system on and takes the chlorine sample a few seconds later.
What is likely going on
Water that sat in the carbon all night has had hours of contact time and will read falsely low.
What to do
Run the system for the time your procedure states, then take the sample.
Why it matters
The test must represent water moving through the carbon at working flow.

The utility changes disinfectant

What you see
A notice in the newspaper says the water district will switch from chlorine to chloramine next month.
What is likely going on
Chloramine is harder to remove and needs the full carbon contact time. Carbon that coped with chlorine may not cope with chloramine.
What to do
Give the notice to the medical director and the system supplier today. Ask whether the carbon type and contact time are adequate, and plan more frequent testing through the changeover.
Why it matters
Chloramine changeovers are a classic cause of patient injury in dialysis units.
Field notes
  • Use a total chlorine test every time. Never a free chlorine test.
  • Test after the worker tank first. If anything shows, test after the polisher at once.
  • Once a week, prove that the kit reads positive on feed water.
  • Never leave the carbon bypass open, and never accept regenerated carbon.
Case review

Back to the case: A row of ticks

Can the past month’s log be relied on? No. A month of ticks, made with strips that had already expired, is not evidence that the water was safe. It is also not proof that it was unsafe. It is a gap in the record. Report it to the chief technician and the medical director as a gap, without blame, and do not rewrite or fill in the old entries.

What today’s result tells you. Today’s result is 0.06 mg/L after the worker tank and less than 0.02 after the polisher. If the unit’s limit is 0.1 mg/L, both are within it, and Mr. Aquino can record the numbers and release the water to Ms. Santiago. But chlorine that can be detected after the worker tank is a trend worth reporting the same morning, because the carbon may be nearing exhaustion. Test after both tanks at the next interval and write every number.

What to report and what should change. Report the gap in the old log and the detectable chlorine after the worker tank. Then fix the system that allowed the ticks: in-date kits with a reorder point, a log sheet that asks for numbers and ports instead of boxes to tick, and a second person who reviews the log each week.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Total chlorine test and daily log

Materials: the unit’s total chlorine test kit, a timer, the sample ports, and the log. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • Confirms the system has been running for the stated time before sampling.
  • CriticalChecks that the kit is a total chlorine test and is in date.
  • CriticalSelects the correct sample port and flushes it for the stated time.
  • CriticalFollows the kit instructions exactly, including the reading time.
  • Records the value, unit, port, time, and name.
  • CriticalStates the correct action for a result above the limit after the worker tank and after the polisher.
  • Completes the remaining daily readings with numbers and units and signs the release.
  • Points out one trend in the past month’s log and says whom to tell.
Placement activity. Perform the start-of-day round with your preceptor on three different days. On the third, the preceptor only observes.
Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 5 questions at random from this module’s 12-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 4 of 5 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 12A carbon bed of 240 liters receives 40 liters per minute. What is the empty-bed contact time?
240 divided by 40 is 6 minutes.
Question 2 of 12Chlorine is detected after the first carbon tank but not after the second. What does this mean?
Breakthrough after the worker is a warning to increase testing and arrange replacement as the procedure states.
Question 3 of 12Does backwashing restore exhausted carbon?
Backwashing prevents channeling but does not renew the carbon’s capacity.
Question 4 of 12A new 5-micrometer cartridge clogs in three days. What is the best interpretation?
Rapid clogging points to a problem upstream that needs to be found.
Question 5 of 12Which test detects chloramine?
Total chlorine includes free chlorine and chloramine. A free chlorine test misses chloramine.
Question 6 of 12Where is the first chlorine sample of the day taken?
Testing after the worker shows whether it is still doing its job.
Question 7 of 12Chlorine is above the limit after the second carbon tank. What happens next?
Breakthrough after the polisher means chlorine can reach patients.
Question 8 of 12Why must the color be read at exactly the stated time?
Test chemistry is timed; follow the kit instructions.
Question 9 of 12A unit uses a free chlorine test. The utility switches to chloramine. What will the test show?
A free chlorine test does not detect chloramine.
Question 10 of 12Why test the feed water with the same kit once a week?
A kit that cannot read positive on chlorinated feed water cannot be trusted.
Question 11 of 12After carbon replacement, what must be done before release?
New carbon sheds fines and must be proven to remove chlorine.
Question 12 of 12Which carbon is not used for dialysis water treatment?
Regenerated carbon may carry contaminants from its previous use.
