Study series for dialysis technicians

This module is study material for dialysis technicians. It explains why safe practice works the way it does. It does not replace the physician’s orders, the supervising nurse’s direction, your unit’s approved procedures, or the manufacturer’s instructions for use. What a technician may do differs between countries and between facilities, so do only the tasks you are trained, assigned, and signed off to do. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Trace the blood and dialysate paths and identify the key components in each.
  • Explain what common safeguards monitor and recognize the limits of alarms.
  • Demonstrate assigned preparation and required pre-use checks on a training machine using the applicable instructions for use (IFU) and facility policies.
  • Hold equipment with an unresolved failure, notify the appropriate team members and document the facts.

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsA dialysis machine runs two separate loops that meet only at the dialyzer. The blood loop takes blood out of the patient, through the dialyzer, and back. The fluid loop mixes dialysate, sends it past the fibers, and drains it. The machine watches both loops with sensors, the way a guard watches a building through security cameras, and it stops when something looks wrong.

The picture has a limit, and it is the limit of every camera. A sensor sees only the point where it sits. A needle that has slipped out, or a concentrate of the wrong formula, can pass without an alarm. Sensors miss things. Your eyes are the last safeguard.

Figure 2 · Key terms for this module

Extracorporeal circuitThe path blood takes outside the body: the tubing, the pump segment, the dialyzer, and the air trap.
Blood pumpA roller pump that squeezes the tubing to move blood.
Arterial and venous linesBy convention, the “arterial” line carries blood from the patient to the dialyzer, and the “venous” line returns it.
Air detectorA sensor on the return line that stops the pump and clamps the line when it senses air.
PrimingFilling the circuit with fluid to remove the air before blood enters.
Instructions for useThe manufacturer’s official directions for the exact machine model in front of you.
Opening case · fictional

Case: Failed before the first treatment

It is Friday, 06:40, in the training unit. The first patient is due at 07:00. Machine hemodialysis (HD)-06 shows a failed required pre-use self-test, and the display names the test that failed. The outside of the machine and the disposables look intact. Your coworker, Mr. Ronnie Castillo, looks at the clock and says, “Just clear the message and run it again. We don’t have time.” Another machine in the unit may be available.

Before reading on, decide:

  • Is HD-06 ready for use?
  • Whom do you tell, and what will they need to know?
  • If another machine is available, which preparation and verification steps still apply to it?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Trace the two circuits

Think of a house with two separate water systems: the clean water that comes in through the tap and the drain water that leaves through the sink. They run side by side through the same walls but never mix. A dialysis machine works with two paths in the same way, one for blood and one for dialysate, and they meet only across the thin wall of the dialyzer membrane. The picture has a limit. In a house, the two systems never exchange anything. In a dialyzer, exchange across that wall is the whole purpose.

Extracorporeal means outside the body. The blood path runs from the patient’s vascular access, through the blood tubing and the dialyzer, and back to the patient. A blood pump moves blood through the circuit. The line that draws blood out is often labeled arterial, and the line that returns it venous. These labels describe the direction of flow in the HD circuit, not the type of vessel. With a fistula or graft, both needles may be placed in the same vascular access. Connecting a patient is beyond the simulation in this module.1,2

Dialysate is made from treated water and concentrates. It flows through a separate compartment of the dialyzer, outside the hollow fibers that carry the blood. Used dialysate flows on to the machine’s effluent, or drain, pathway. The semipermeable membrane is where exchange takes place. In normal operation, blood and dialysate stay in their own compartments. The fluid pathways and protective components of a particular machine are shown in that model’s IFU.3,4

ComponentWhat to identify in the skills lab
Blood tubing and pump segmentThe withdrawal and return limbs, their labeled ends, and the section the pump acts on.
DialyzerThe hollow-fiber filter and its separate blood and dialysate connections.
Venous chamber and return-line clampThe chamber on the return side in a typical circuit, and the protective clamp that can close off the return line when triggered.
Pressure monitor interfacesWhere the machine senses pressure in the blood circuit. Some designs use external monitor lines with transducer protectors.
Dialysate fluid pathThe supply of treated water and concentrate, delivery to the dialyzer, and discharge of used fluid.
Fluid balance and ultrafiltration (UF) systemThe machine system that regulates fluid removal according to the verified plan.
Lesson · part 2

Safeguards support direct observation

Figure 4 · What each safeguard watches, and what it cannot see

Pressure monitorsWatch the pressure at set points. They can miss a needle that has slipped out.
Air detector and clampWatch for air at one point on the return line. The tubing must be seated correctly for it to work.
Blood leak detectorWatches the used dialysate for blood. It cannot see bleeding outside the circuit.
Conductivity and temperatureWatch the strength and warmth of the dialysate. They cannot confirm which concentrate is connected.

Think of the smoke detector in a kitchen. It is very good at one thing, smoke at the ceiling, and it says nothing about a gas leak at the floor. Each safeguard on a dialysis machine is like that. It measures one condition at one place in the circuit. Read the displayed message in its context, and do not assume that an alarm proves a single cause, or that silence proves safety. Depending on the machine’s design and the event, some alarms stop the blood pump, close a clamp, or divert the dialysate. Keep the patient, the access site, and the circuit under observation as the facility’s workflow assigns.4,5

SafeguardWhat it monitors and what to remember
Blood-side pressure monitorsSense pressure at their monitoring points. The patient’s access, kinks or restrictions in the tubing, flow, and other factors can affect the readings. A venous pressure alarm may miss some needle dislodgements, so keep the access and connections visible as directed.
Air or level detector and venous clampWatch for air or a low blood level at the designated point, depending on the design. Correct placement of the tubing or chamber matters. A working detector does not replace a correctly prepared circuit or your own visual checks.
Blood leak detectorChecks used dialysate for blood that has crossed the dialyzer membrane. It does a different job from watching for external bleeding or disconnected tubing.
Conductivity and temperature monitorsMonitor important properties of the prepared dialysate. An acceptable conductivity alone cannot confirm the identity of the concentrate, the level of each electrolyte, or the status of any other required check.
UF and fluid balance monitoringSupports controlled fluid removal. The authorized prescription and the recorded settings still need to match before a session begins.

When an alarm sounds during treatment, alert the dialysis nurse and follow the unit’s emergency and equipment procedures. Give the exact message and what you can see. Stay within your assigned role. Protective features and alarm limits stay in place while the problem is assessed.4,2

RememberAn alarm tells you where to look. Silence does not tell you that everything is fine. Keep the access and every connection in view.
Lesson · part 3

Verify readiness using the installed model’s IFU

Practice on an unconnected training machine with your preceptor. The sequence, the supplies, the checks, and the acceptance criteria depend on the machine, its software version, and local procedure. Use the prompts below alongside the approved checklist.

  1. Station and machine. Verify the machine’s identity, service status, cleanliness, and any required disinfection or rinse status. Confirm through the unit’s process that the required utilities and validated dialysis water are available.
  2. Supplies. Match the dialyzer, tubing, and concentrate labels against the authorized prescription and the equipment requirements. Check that each package is intact and in date. Use aseptic technique, as applicable, when you open or handle sterile components.
  3. Circuit. Trace the designated paths. Verify the assigned placement of tubing, pump segments, monitors, detectors, and clamps. Demonstrate the facility-approved priming process in simulation, and verify that the circuit is ready by the model’s own criteria.
  4. Machine checks. Confirm that the required self-tests completed successfully. Complete the assigned dialysate and equipment checks, including independent verification and residual-disinfectant checks where the applicable procedure requires them. Record the actual results, not “passed.”
  5. Handoff. Before any patient is connected, confirm all assigned checks and any unresolved issues with the supervising nurse. Ask who has the authority to clear support equipment after a fault or a service intervention.

A successful built-in test confirms only the functions that test covers. It does not confirm the patient’s identity, does not match the disposables to the order, and does not replace any other required check.4,6

Lesson · in practice

Operating the machine: from switch-on to shutdown

In plain wordsThink of riding jeepneys from different makers. The seats, the handrails, and the place you pay all differ, but every trip goes through the same stages: board, pay, ride, call “para,” get off. Dialysis machines are similar. Each model has its own screens and buttons, but all of them move through the same stages in the same order.

The picture has a limit. A jeepney passenger can skip paying and the trip still ends. A machine stage that is skipped leaves a hazard in place for the patient. If you know the stages, you can find your place on any model, and you can tell when a step has been missed.

Figure 8 · The stages of one machine day

  1. Switch on and self-testThe machine checks its own sensors, pumps, clamps, and alarms.
  2. RinseStored disinfectant and standing water are flushed out.
  3. Connect concentratesAcid and bicarbonate, matched to the prescription.
  4. StabilizeConductivity and temperature settle within their limits.
  5. Set up and primeThe lines and dialyzer are mounted and filled with saline.
  6. Pre-treatment checksAlarms, pressures, dialysate, and residual disinfectant.
  7. TreatmentThe nurse connects the patient; you observe and report.
  8. End and stripThe nurse returns the blood; the used circuit is removed and discarded safely.
  9. DisinfectThe outside surfaces and the internal fluid path.
  10. Shutdown and recordThe machine log is completed.

Lesson · in practice

Priming the dialyzer and blood lines, step by step

Think of filling a new garden hose before you water the plants: you let the water run until the air sputters out and a steady stream comes through. Priming does the same for the circuit. A new dialyzer and its blood lines are full of air, and the dialyzer may hold traces of the substances used to make and sterilize it. Priming replaces the air with saline and rinses the dialyzer before blood enters it.

Figure 10 · What priming achieves

Removes airAir in the fibers blocks them, so less blood is cleaned. Air in the line is a danger to the patient.
Rinses the dialyzerWashes out manufacturing and sterilization residues, or the germicide in a reprocessed dialyzer.
Wets the membraneA wet membrane works properly from the first minute.
Tests the circuitA leak, a kink, or a loose connection shows up with saline, not with blood.
Readies the machineThe machine can complete its pressure and alarm checks on a filled circuit.

Figure 11 · Priming a new dialyzer, in general terms

  1. Check and washHand hygiene and gloves. Confirm that the dialyzer and lines match the prescription. Check the packages and expiry dates.
  2. MountDialyzer in its holder. Arterial line through the blood pump. Venous chamber in its holder. Venous line in the air detector and clamp.
  3. Connect salineSpike the saline bag with clean technique and connect it to the arterial side, as the set is designed.
  4. Fill the arterial lineLet the saline push the air ahead of it. Fill the arterial chamber to its mark.
  5. Prime the dialyzerRun the pump at the priming speed. Saline enters at the bottom of the blood side and pushes the air out at the top. Tap and rotate gently to free trapped bubbles.
  6. Fill the venous sideSet the venous chamber level, usually about three quarters full.
  7. Connect dialysateOnly when the machine is out of bypass. Dialysate enters at the bottom and flows opposite to the blood. Clear the air from the dialysate side.
  8. Rinse and recirculatePass the volume of saline the dialyzer’s instructions state, then recirculate if your procedure calls for it.
  9. Final checkNo air anywhere, all caps and clamps correct, levels set, machine checks passed.

The practical point of the bottom-up fill is physics: air rises. Fluid entering from below pushes air out through the top. Fluid forced in too fast, or from the wrong end, traps it.

Common priming errorWhat it causesHow to prevent it
Pump run too fast at the startAir trapped inside the fibers, seen as pale streaks; lower clearance and more clottingUse the priming speed in the procedure; fill from the bottom upward
Dialyzer left with the wrong end upAir cannot escapeThe blood side fills from bottom to top; turn the dialyzer as the procedure states
Too little saline passed throughResidues reach the patient; reactions in the first minutes of treatmentMeasure the volume against the instructions; do not judge it by eye
Venous line not fully seated in the air detectorThe detector cannot see airPress the line in until it sits flat; close the door
Transducer protector wet with salineFalse pressure readingsClamp the pressure lines while filling the chambers; replace a wet protector
Touching the open ends of lines or the saline spikeContamination of the blood pathKeep caps on until the moment of connection; replace anything that touches a non-sterile surface
Primed circuit left standing for a long timeGrowth of bacteria in warm saline and dialysateFollow your unit’s time limit; if it is exceeded, discard and set up again

A reprocessed dialyzer adds four steps. Module 11 covers them in full. First, two people confirm that the name on the dialyzer is the patient’s. Second, before rinsing, test that germicide is present, which shows the dialyzer stayed disinfected in storage. Third, rinse and recirculate exactly as the procedure states. Fourth, test that the germicide is gone, and record the result, before the nurse connects the patient.7

Stop and reportAir in the circuit, a leak, or a positive residual germicide test means the circuit is not ready. Tell the nurse. Do not hand it over hoping the machine will catch the problem.
Lesson · in practice

Disinfecting the machine: the operator’s part

Cleaning a kitchen after cooking has two parts: you wipe the counter, and you also wash inside the pots. A clean counter says nothing about the inside of the pot. A dialysis machine is the same. There are two different jobs, and both are needed after every patient.

Outside surfacesInside fluid path
What is contaminatedThe screen, front panel, clamps, pole, holders, and sides, by gloved hands and splashes of bloodThe tubing, valves, and chambers that carry dialysate, by bacteria growing in warm fluid
How it is cleanedWiped with the disinfectant your unit has approved, kept wet for the full contact time on the labelA built-in program using heat, a chemical, or both
WhenAfter every patient, as part of station turnover (Module 5)As your unit’s policy and the manual state: commonly a rinse or disinfection between patients and a full program at the end of the day
How you know it workedEvery surface was wiped and stayed wet for the contact timeThe machine reports the program complete, and after a chemical program the residual test is negative

Figure 13 · An internal disinfection program, operator steps

  1. Remove the circuitNo dialyzer or blood lines on the machine. Dialysate connectors on their rinse ports.
  2. Choose the programThe one your procedure names for this time of day.
  3. Check the supplyThe correct disinfectant container is connected, in date, and not empty.
  4. Start and leave itDo not interrupt it. Label the machine so that nobody uses it.
  5. RinseAfter a chemical program, the machine rinses itself.
  6. TestUse the correct test strip to show that the chemical is gone.
  7. RecordThe program, the agent, the time, the test result, and your name.
Program typeWhat it is forOperator points
RinseFlushes out used dialysate; it does not disinfectNever counted as disinfection
Heat, or heat with citric acidKills microbes; citric acid also removes scaleSurfaces and the drain line become hot. Allow time to cool
Acid descalingDissolves the white crust that bicarbonate leaves behindScale shelters bacteria and upsets sensors, so this program is not optional
Bleach or another chemical disinfectantKills microbes and removes films of protein and fatWear the protective equipment on the label. Never mix bleach with acid. A residual test is required
Stop and reportThree things take a machine off the floor until they are settled: a disinfection program that did not finish, a positive residual test, and a machine that has stood unused longer than your unit allows without disinfection. Label it and tell the nurse.

Machines assigned to patients with hepatitis B stay with those patients, and their outside surfaces need particular care at turnover.8,9 The internal program is the same. What protects the other patients is the assignment.

Lesson · part 4

Hold, escalate and document an unresolved failure

Is HD-06 ready for use? No. A failed required test means the machine has not shown that it is safe. Keep it unavailable for patient use and apply the facility’s hold or out-of-service process. Clearing the display message does not make the machine ready; it only hides the evidence. Perform only the operator checks you are authorized to do under the applicable procedure, and use the facility’s formal clearance process once the problem is resolved.

Whom do you tell, and what do they need? The supervising or charge nurse, and the designated equipment or biomedical team. Give them the machine identifier, the time, the name of the test, the exact message, whether a patient was connected, and what you did. Example handoff: “Machine HD-06 failed its required pre-use test at 06:40. The exact display message is logged. No patient was connected. I have placed it on hold and contacted the equipment lead. Please confirm the treatment assignment.”

What applies to a replacement machine? Everything. If a replacement is assigned, verify its identity and complete that machine’s own required preparation, checks, and documentation before connection. Mr. Castillo’s worry about time is real, and the nurse can rearrange the schedule. Nobody can undo a treatment started on an unsafe machine.2,4

Stop and reportA machine that fails a required check stays out of service until an authorized person clears it. Do not restart it “to see if it passes this time” and then use it.

Figure 14 · When a machine fails a check

  1. HoldTake the machine out of use and label it.
  2. RecordWrite down the machine number and the exact message.
  3. ReportTell the nurse and the biomedical contact. Say whether a patient is connected.
  4. ReplaceA substitute machine needs its own full set of checks.
  5. ReleaseOnly a designated person returns the machine to service.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

Self-test failed, shift waiting

What you see
At 05:45, with the first patients already in the waiting area, a machine fails its pressure self-test. A coworker says, “Restart it, that one always passes the second time.”
What is likely going on
A test that fails sometimes is telling you that something is marginal. Passing on a retry does not explain the failure.
What to do
Record the message and tag the machine. Report to the nurse and the biomedical contact, and prepare another machine with its own full set of checks.
Why it matters
The self-test checks the alarms that protect the patient. An unreliable alarm is worse than none, because people trust it.

A wet transducer protector

What you see
Midway through priming, you see that fluid has reached the small filter on one of the pressure lines.
What is likely going on
A wet protector blocks the pressure signal, and fluid or blood may have reached the machine side.
What to do
Tell the nurse. The protector is replaced by the unit’s method, and if fluid passed through it, the machine is checked before use.
Why it matters
False pressure readings give false reassurance, and blood inside the machine is an infection risk for the next patient.

Air alarm during priming

What you see
The air detector alarms again and again while you prime the circuit for the first patient of the day.
What is likely going on
Usually the level in the venous chamber is low, or the line is not seated in the detector. Sometimes the detector is dirty.
What to do
Reseat the line and correct the level by the approved priming method. If the alarm continues, hold the machine and report it.
Why it matters
An air detector that alarms wrongly during priming may also fail to alarm when it should.

The disinfection log is blank

What you see
At the start of the shift a machine shows “ready,” but the log has no record that last night’s disinfection ran or that a residual test was done.
What is likely going on
You cannot tell whether the machine was disinfected, or whether chemical remains inside it.
What to do
Do not use it. Report to the nurse. By procedure, the machine is rinsed and tested, or disinfected again.
Why it matters
Disinfectant reaching a patient’s blood, and a machine that was never disinfected, are both serious harms.
Field notes
  • Before you call a circuit ready, trace each tube with your finger from one end to the other.
  • Write alarm messages down exactly, including any code. Nobody can diagnose “It beeped.”
  • Never silence an alarm before you know why it sounded.
  • The instructions for use differ between models. What was right on the old machine may be wrong on the new one.

Question 1 of 20Which component propels blood through the extracorporeal circuit?
The blood pump moves blood through the extracorporeal bloodlines and dialyzer.
Question 2 of 20Where do blood and dialysate exchange small solutes across a membrane?
The dialyzer houses the membrane between the separate blood and dialysate pathways.
Question 3 of 20A diagram labels one line ‘blood to patient.’ Which pathway is this on a typical two-line circuit?
In standard circuit nomenclature, the arterial line withdraws blood and the venous line returns blood to the patient.
Question 4 of 20How does the conventional circuit expose blood to dialysate for exchange while keeping the fluids separated?
The dialyzer membrane allows selective transport between separate fluid pathways.
Question 5 of 20A detector monitors spent dialysate for evidence that blood crossed the membrane. Which safeguard is this?
The dialyzer blood leak detector monitors spent dialysate for blood. External leakage at a connection requires separate observation and response.
Question 6 of 20On a disconnected trainer, you are asked to identify the area where the machine monitors for air before blood would return to the patient. What do you trace?
Trace the return path and identify the air detection and clamp functions on the actual model using its instructions.
Question 7 of 20What written reference determines how your station’s specific machine and compatible disposables are prepared?
Models and disposables differ. Use the applicable instructions and the facility’s validated pre-use process.
Question 8 of 20What is a purpose of priming the extracorporeal circuit before use?
Priming prepares the prescribed circuit for use and clears air as specified. Independent readiness checks are still required.
Question 9 of 20The machine self-test passed. One assigned independent readiness check is not yet documented as complete. What does this tell you?
A passed self-test covers its defined test functions. Confirm that assigned independent checks were completed before releasing the station.
Question 10 of 20The available bloodline set resembles the usual one, but compatibility with this machine is uncertain. What should you do?
Appearance is insufficient to verify compatibility. Confirm the exact disposable and configuration before opening or using it.
Question 11 of 20After a supervised setup, you notice visible air remains in the mock return line. What is your next step?
Visible air requires correction and verification under the model instructions before the circuit is considered ready.
Question 12 of 20What does the absence of a machine alarm establish about the access site and external connections?
Alarms monitor defined conditions within their ranges and have detection limits. Keep the access and external connections visible and follow the authorized monitoring process.
Question 13 of 20Before the patient arrives, a required self-test fails and the failure remains unresolved after authorized checks. What should you do?
A failed readiness check leaves equipment unverified. Prevent use and follow the local reporting and release procedure.
Question 14 of 20Which equipment log entry best supports follow-up after a failed pre-use self-test?
The entry identifies the equipment, exact observed failure, time, use status and notifications, so authorized staff can investigate and track follow-up.
Question 15 of 20During treatment you notice blood at an external connection, but the blood leak alarm has not activated. What should you do?
External blood loss can occur without triggering the dialyzer blood leak detector. Respond to the observed hazard promptly and within scope.
Question 16 of 20What is the main reason a dialyzer is primed from the bottom upward at a slow pump speed?
Air trapped in fibers blocks them and lowers clearance.
Question 17 of 20A primed circuit shows pale streaks along the dialyzer. What do they most likely mean?
Streaks are air-locked fibers; the dialyzer needs further priming or replacement by the unit’s procedure.
Question 18 of 20Which statement about the machine’s rinse program is correct?
A rinse is never counted as disinfection.
Question 19 of 20After a chemical disinfection program, what must be shown before the machine is used?
Only the residual test proves the chemical has been rinsed out.
Question 20 of 20When should the dialysate lines be connected to the dialyzer?
Dialysate that is out of range must not reach the dialyzer.

HD
Hemodialysis.
IFU
Instructions for use — The manufacturer’s official directions for a device or product.
UF
Ultrafiltration — Removal of plasma water across the dialyzer membrane by a pressure gradient.

Terms in plain words

Pump segment
The thicker section of blood tubing that fits inside the blood pump.
Venous chamber
The drip chamber on the return line that traps air and small clots. It is also called the air trap or bubble trap.
Transducer protector
A small filter between the tubing and the machine’s pressure sensor. It keeps blood out of the machine.
Self-test
The automatic checks that a machine runs on its own alarms and sensors before treatment.
Residual disinfectant
Traces of cleaning chemical left in the machine. A test must show none before use.
