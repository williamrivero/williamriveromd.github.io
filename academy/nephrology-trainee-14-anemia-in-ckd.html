Training track for nephrology fellows, residents and medical interns

Study material for medical interns, residents and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and gives no drug doses. Doses, rates and order sets come from current guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved protocols and your supervising physician. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Intern: Recognize anemia in a patient with chronic kidney disease (CKD), take the bleeding, diet, medicine and transfusion history, and collect the first test set your resident orders without missing a sample.
  • Resident: Work up anemia and iron status in CKD, look for every correctable cause before blaming the kidneys, and propose iron therapy using the thresholds of the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2026 anemia guideline.
  • Fellow: Apply the KDIGO 2026 guidance on iron, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs), hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors (HIF-PHIs) and transfusion with their stated grades, keep hemoglobin (Hgb) below the recommended ceiling, and manage hyporesponsiveness.

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · the tired patient with a low hemoglobinThe intern notices the pallor, asks about black stools, pain tablets and past transfusions, and gets the right tubes to the laboratory before any iron or blood is given. The resident builds the work-up, names the correctable causes, and proposes iron with the guideline threshold that justifies it. The fellow decides with the attending whether and when to add an ESA or a HIF-PHI, sets the hemoglobin ceiling, and keeps transfusion for the patient who truly needs it.
In plain wordsPicture a garment factory in Taytay. A foreman watches the orders and tells the sewers how many shirts to make. The sewers need cloth from a bodega. If the foreman stops calling for shirts, production falls. If the bodega is locked, the sewers stand idle even when the foreman shouts. In the body the kidney is the foreman, erythropoietin is the order slip, the bone marrow is the sewing floor, and iron is the cloth.

The picture has a limit. A factory has one foreman and one bodega. In CKD both fail together, and for linked reasons. The kidney’s oxygen sensor sits in fibroblast-like cells around the tubules. When they sense low oxygen, the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway switches on the erythropoietin gene. In diseased kidneys these cells are scarred and the signal is too small for the degree of anemia. At the same time, inflammation and reduced kidney clearance raise hepcidin, the liver hormone that locks iron inside gut cells and macrophages. The marrow then has orders it cannot fill. Blood lost to dialysis circuits, phlebotomy and the gut drains the bodega further.1

Most anemia in CKD is a mix of too little erythropoietin and too little usable iron, and it responds to patient, stepwise correction of both. A few patients are anemic because they are bleeding, have a marrow or blood disease, or have a deficiency the kidneys did not cause. Your work is to find those first, give iron when the numbers and the patient call for it, use ESAs to avoid transfusion rather than to chase a normal hemoglobin, and remember that a hemoglobin value is a surrogate, not an outcome the patient feels.

Opening case · fictional

Case: The ward consult on a Thursday

Thursday, 10 a.m., medical ward of a tertiary hospital in Quezon City. Mrs. Corazon Villanueva, 61, a retired market vendor from Novaliches, has type 2 diabetes and CKD G4 with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 22 mL/min/1.73 m². She was admitted last night for breathlessness on climbing the stairs to her apartment and swelling of both ankles. She takes losartan, insulin, and a ferrous sulfate tablet she buys from the botika “when there is money.” For her knees she takes mefenamic acid most days. For two weeks her stools have been darker than usual.

Her Hb is 8.1 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume (MCV) 78 fL, platelets normal, ferritin 90 ng/mL and TSAT 14%. No reticulocyte count was sent. Her son, an overseas Filipino worker (OFW) in Dubai, has asked about donating a kidney to her. The admitting resident, Dr. Paolo Mendoza, wants to transfuse two units tonight and “start erythropoietin (EPO) so she can go home.” Her daughter has already been told to find two blood donors for the blood bank.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What must be done before anyone gives blood, iron or an ESA, and which correctable causes do you see?
  • Does she meet a KDIGO 2026 threshold for iron, and would you give it by mouth or intravenously?
  • Does she need a transfusion tonight, and when would an ESA or a HIF-PHI enter the plan?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Why the kidney makes people anemic: four linked failures

A healthy adult replaces about one percent of red cell (RBC)s each day. The marrow keeps pace because the kidney adjusts the order and the gut and macrophages release enough iron. In CKD this loop breaks at four points, and each points to a different action at the bedside.1

First, erythropoietin falls short. The level in a patient with CKD may be within the laboratory’s normal range, yet far too low for the degree of anemia. That is a relative deficiency, and it is the reason a normal erythropoietin result does not exclude renal anemia. Second, iron is locked away. Hepcidin rises with inflammation and with falling kidney clearance. It blocks the iron exporter ferroportin, so gut absorption falls and iron stays trapped in macrophages. The patient may have stored iron and still be unable to use it, which we call functional iron deficiency. Third, iron is lost. Every hemodialysis session leaves blood in the dialyzer and lines, every admission brings phlebotomy, and uremic platelets and pain tablets make gut bleeding easier. Fourth, red cells live a shorter time in a uremic environment, and other contributors such as severe hyperparathyroidism and deficiency of vitamin B12 or folate add to the problem.1

The proportion matters. Anemia that is severe while the eGFR is still well preserved, anemia with a low platelet or white cell count, or a microcytic picture with a ferritin far below 100 ng/mL should each make you suspect a second process. The kidney explains the anemia only when the anemia fits the kidney.

In the Philippines, add what the region brings. Ask about hookworm and other intestinal parasites in a patient from a farming area, malaria in a patient from an endemic province such as Palawan, and thalassemia trait in a microcytic patient whose iron stores are normal or whose relatives have “always been pale.” Ask about herbal products and pain tablets bought without prescription; mefenamic acid and other nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) cause gut bleeding as well as kidney injury.

In childrenChildren with CKD use age-specific Hb thresholds, and anemia matters for growth, school performance and heart size as well as energy. KDIGO 2026 groups children with adults who are not on dialysis when it advises choosing the Hb at which to start an ESA by symptoms, expected benefit and risk (2D), and it publishes a separate illustration for children.2,3 Transfusion carries the same allosensitization cost for a child who will one day need a kidney. See Attending Pediatric Nephrologist, Module 18.
RememberBefore you call it renal anemia, ask which of the four failures is present: too little erythropoietin, iron locked away, iron lost, or a second cause. Each one changes what you do next.
Lesson · part 2

The work-up: what to send, how often, and how to read it

KDIGO 2026 asks you to test for anemia in CKD at referral, regularly during follow-up, and whenever symptoms suggest it. The first test set is a complete blood count (CBC), reticulocytes with the reticulocyte production index (RPI), ferritin and TSAT (practice point).3,4 The minimum frequency rises as kidney function falls: at least yearly in CKD G3, twice a year in G4, and every three months in G5 and on dialysis (practice point).3

The intern’s job is concrete. Draw the iron studies before the first iron tablet or infusion in hospital, or the numbers will mislead everyone for weeks. Note the date of the last transfusion; transfused cells change the MCV, the reticulocyte count and the ferritin. Ask about black or bloody stools, heavy menses, nosebleeds, aspirin, NSAIDs and anticoagulants, and record them in the history, not just the order sheet.

Reading ferritin and TSAT together

Ferritin reflects stored iron, but it is also an acute-phase protein. Infection, inflammation, liver disease and malignancy push it up. TSAT reflects iron in transit to the marrow, and it falls quickly when supply cannot meet demand. Neither alone tells the whole story, which is why the guideline thresholds always pair them.

PatternLikely meaningNext step for the resident
Ferritin low, TSAT low, MCV fallingAbsolute iron deficiencyLook for blood loss; replace iron

When the first set does not explain the anemia, KDIGO lists an expanded panel: blood smear, haptoglobin, lactate dehydrogenase, C-reactive protein, vitamin B12, folate, liver tests, serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains, thyroid-stimulating hormone, parathyroid hormone and fecal occult blood (practice point).3 When iron deficiency has no clear explanation, the guideline asks you to consider evaluation for blood loss with referral to the right specialist (practice point).3 In practice, that means asking the gastroenterology service about endoscopy, especially in an older adult, in anyone with dark stools, and in anyone on an nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID).

How the reticulocyte production index works (arithmetic, not a target). RPI = reticulocyte % × (patient hematocrit ÷ 45) ÷ maturation factor. The correction removes the false rise caused by fewer red cells, and the maturation factor accounts for reticulocytes released early in severe anemia. An RPI below 2 in an anemic patient means the marrow is not responding as it should.

The Philippine reality is that a CBC is cheap and a full iron panel is not, and many families pay for laboratory tests out of pocket. If you must choose, the CBC with indices, a reticulocyte count, ferritin and TSAT answer most first questions. Write down why each extra test is needed; a family will often find the money for a test they understand.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist when
  • Hb has fallen 2 g/dL or more since the last result without an obvious explanation;
  • there is melena, hematemesis, fresh rectal bleeding or a falling blood pressure;
  • the platelet or white cell count is also low;
  • the patient has chest pain, breathlessness at rest or confusion with the anemia.
Do this per unit protocol, but always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance.
RememberDraw the iron studies before the first dose of iron or the first unit of blood. Numbers taken after either one mislead the next three doctors.
Lesson · part 3

Iron: when the thresholds say yes, and by which route

Iron comes first because an ESA cannot build red cells without it, and because correcting iron deficiency alone raises Hb in some patients. KDIGO 2026 lists iron deficiency among the correctable causes to address before starting an ESA or a HIF-PHI (practice point).3

PopulationKDIGO 2026 statementGrade
HemodialysisStart iron if ferritin ≤500 ng/mL and TSAT ≤30%2D

Hemodialysis, approachGive intravenous iron proactively to keep iron status stablePractice point
Not on dialysis, or on peritoneal dialysisStart iron if ferritin below 100 ng/mL with TSAT below 40%, or ferritin 100 to 300 ng/mL with TSAT below 25%2D
Not on hemodialysis, routeOral or intravenous, chosen by preference, severity, efficacy, tolerability, availability and cost2D

These statements come from the guideline and its executive summary.3,4 Notice the grades. Every iron threshold is 2D: a weak suggestion on very low certainty evidence. The trials that informed them used these cut points as entry criteria; none tested the thresholds against each other for outcomes patients feel. Treat them as sensible boundaries, not as proof.

Monitoring and safety

Test Hb, ferritin and TSAT about every three months in patients not on dialysis or on peritoneal dialysis, and every one to three months on hemodialysis (practice points). Switch from oral to intravenous iron if oral iron fails after one to three months or is not tolerated, and consider pausing iron during a systemic infection (practice points). Give intravenous iron only where an acute reaction can be managed; routine test doses and premedication are usually not needed (practice points).3 The doses come from your unit formulary and the label, not from memory.

The evidence behind proactive intravenous iron in hemodialysis

PIVOTAL randomized 2,141 adults starting maintenance hemodialysis in the United Kingdom to a proactive high-dose intravenous iron sucrose strategy, held only when ferritin was above 700 µg/L or TSAT was 40% or more, or to a reactive low-dose strategy triggered by ferritin below 200 µg/L or TSAT below 20%. Over a median of 2.1 years, the composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, heart failure admission or death occurred in 29.3% of the proactive group and 32.3% of the reactive group (hazard ratio [heart rate (HR)] 0.85, 95% confidence interval 0.73 to 1.00). That is about 3 fewer events per 100 patients over two years, a number needed to treat (NNT) of roughly 33. The proactive group needed about a fifth less ESA, and infection rates did not differ.5 This was an open-label randomized controlled trial (RCT) with blinded end-point review, in incident hemodialysis patients. It does not tell you about patients not on dialysis, and its iron ceiling is where the KDIGO practice point to withhold iron comes from.

For patients not on dialysis, trials comparing intravenous with oral iron showed modest differences in Hb, which is a surrogate. KDIGO therefore leaves the route to shared choice (2D).3 In the Philippines that choice is shaped by money and travel. Oral iron is cheap and taken at home; it causes constipation and abdominal upset, and absorption is poor when hepcidin is high. Intravenous iron needs a facility visit and a trained nurse, but one or a few visits may replace months of tablets.

For patients on maintenance hemodialysis, Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) describes its hemodialysis package of PHP 6,350 per session, effective October 9, 2024, as including drugs and medicines for anemia management, and its advisory lists erythropoietin preparations among the minimum standards of the package.6,7 Confirm what your unit actually supplies on the date of service. A patient not yet on dialysis has no such package and usually pays for every vial and tablet.

RememberPair ferritin with TSAT, name the KDIGO threshold you are using and its 2D grade, and choose the route by setting and patient: intravenous in hemodialysis, a shared choice everywhere else.
Lesson · part 4

ESAs and HIF-PHIs: treat to avoid harm, not to reach normal

Once iron and other causes are corrected, the remaining anemia is mostly erythropoietin deficiency. ESAs replace the missing signal by injection. HIF-PHIs are tablets that block the enzymes that break down HIF, so the body’s own erythropoietin rises and iron handling improves. Both raise Hb. The question that cost nephrology a decade of trials was how high to raise it.

The trials that set the ceiling

CHOIR (Correction of Hemoglobin and Outcomes in Renal Insufficiency) randomized 1,432 patients with CKD not on dialysis to epoetin alfa targeting Hb 13.5 g/dL or 11.3 g/dL. Over a median of 16 months, the composite of death, myocardial infarction, heart failure admission or stroke occurred in 125 patients (17.5%) in the high-target group and 97 (13.5%) in the low-target group (HR 1.34, 95% confidence interval 1.03 to 1.74). Quality of life improved equally in both groups.8 That is about 4 more events per 100 patients for aiming higher, with nothing gained in how patients felt. CHOIR was open label.

TREAT (Trial to Reduce Cardiovascular Events with Aranesp Therapy) randomized 4,038 patients with type 2 diabetes, CKD not on dialysis and anemia to darbepoetin alfa targeting Hb about 13 g/dL, or to placebo with rescue darbepoetin when Hb fell below 9.0 g/dL. Death or a cardiovascular event did not differ (632 versus 602 patients; HR 1.05). Stroke occurred in 101 patients (5.0%) on darbepoetin and 53 (2.6%) on placebo (HR 1.92). Transfusions were fewer with darbepoetin (297 versus 496 patients, about 15% versus 25%), and fatigue improved only modestly.9 Read the trade-off in absolute terms: for every 100 patients treated to the higher target, about 10 avoided a transfusion and about 2 more had a stroke.

That combination is deliberate: the panel judged that the harms seen in CHOIR and TREAT were serious enough to recommend against higher targets for almost everyone, even though no trial tested 11.5 g/dL itself.

HIF-PHIs: an alternative, not a default

ASCEND-ND randomized 3,872 patients with CKD not on dialysis to oral daprodustat or injected darbepoetin alfa. The rise in Hb was similar, and a first major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) occurred in 19.5% versus 19.2% over a median of 1.9 years (HR 1.03, 95% confidence interval 0.89 to 1.19), which met the trial’s noninferiority margin.10 Noninferiority with a margin of 1.25 is not proof of equal safety, and longer experience with these agents is still limited. KDIGO keeps ESAs first (2D) and asks you to avoid HIF-PHIs in people with active cancer, recent cardiovascular events or recent thrombosis (practice points).3 A tablet may still suit a patient who cannot reach a clinic for injections, after a frank discussion.

When the Hb will not rise

ESA hyporesponsiveness means Hb fails to rise, or needs escalating ESA, despite treatment. The guideline asks you first to identify and treat the cause (practice point).3 The usual list is iron deficiency, infection or inflammation, ongoing blood loss, hyperparathyroidism, inadequate dialysis, missed injections, and, rarely, antibody-mediated pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), which needs the ESA stopped and hematology involved (practice point).3 Do not answer hyporesponsiveness by doubling the dose; in the trials, patients who needed the most ESA did worst.

In the Philippines, the ESA for a patient not on dialysis is usually bought by the family, a few vials at a time. Ask how the vials are stored; most ESAs need refrigeration, and a brownout in a typhoon can spoil them. Ask whether doses were skipped because of money. Missed doses look like hyporesponsiveness in the chart.

RememberUse an ESA to spare the patient symptoms and transfusions, never to reach a normal Hb. In adults keep Hb below 11.5 g/dL (1D), and treat a poor response by finding its cause.
Lesson · part 5

Transfusion stewardship: blood is a scarce drug with long effects

A transfusion raises Hb within hours and can relieve angina or breathlessness the same night. It also carries volume, potassium, infection risk and, for a future transplant candidate, sensitization to human leukocyte antigens (HLA) that can make a matched kidney harder to find for years. KDIGO 2026 frames transfusion as part of a whole strategy decided with the patient (practice point).3

KDIGO 2026 practice pointWhat it means on your ward
Base transfusion on symptoms and signs of anemia, not an arbitrary Hb thresholdAsk what the patient feels and what the heart shows; do not write “transfuse if Hb below 8” by reflex
In transplant candidates, avoid transfusion when possibleAsk about transplant plans before every unit; a living donor offer changes the calculus
Transfuse when benefits outweigh risks in acute anemiaHemorrhage, unstable coronary disease, urgent surgery
Transfusion may be preferred when an ESA or HIF-PHI is ineffective or harmfulRecent stroke, active cancer, thrombosis
Use strategies that reduce transfusionFewer and smaller blood draws, continue ESA and iron in hospital, follow Hb trends

These practice points are from the guideline.3 There is no Hb threshold recommendation in the chapter: the panel did not find evidence to set one for people with CKD.

The trials remind you that preventing transfusion is a real benefit. In TREAT, about one in four patients on placebo was transfused, against about one in seven on darbepoetin.9 The same trial showed the price of chasing a high Hb. The fellow holds both facts and helps the patient choose.

The Philippine blood bank and the family

Many Philippine hospitals ask the family to bring replacement donors before or after a transfusion. A daughter who spends a day recruiting donors for a unit that was not needed has lost wages and goodwill, and the blood bank has lost a unit someone else needed. Before you send the family out, write down the reason for the transfusion. If the patient is stable, symptomatic only on exertion and has a correctable cause, treat the cause first and reassess in the morning.

Fewer blood draws matter more than most residents think. Combine tests, use pediatric tubes where your laboratory accepts them, and stop daily CBCs once the trend is clear.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist when a patient with CKD is bleeding actively, has chest pain or breathlessness at rest with anemia, has a fall in Hb that changes your plan, or is a transplant candidate and a transfusion is being considered. Do this per unit protocol, but always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance.
RememberBefore every unit, write three things in the chart: the symptom or sign that justifies it, whether the patient is a transplant candidate, and what you are doing about the cause.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The ferritin of 1,100 in the infected catheter patient

What you see
A 58-year-old man on hemodialysis in Pampanga has a fever from his tunneled catheter, Hb 8.6 g/dL, ferritin 1,100 ng/mL and TSAT 15%. The unit’s standing order gives intravenous iron today.
What is likely going on
Ferritin is high because of inflammation; TSAT is low because hepcidin traps iron. More iron now adds little and may not be safe during infection.
What to do
Ask the attending nephrologist to hold the iron during the systemic infection, treat the catheter infection, and recheck iron studies after recovery.
Why it matters
KDIGO suggests withholding routine iron above ferritin 700 ng/mL and considering a pause during systemic infection (practice points).

The Hb of 12.4 on a busy Saturday

What you see
A 64-year-old woman on hemodialysis in Marikina, on an ESA for years, has Hb 12.4 g/dL. Nobody has changed the dose in three months.
What is likely going on
She is above the KDIGO ceiling for adults on an ESA.
What to do
Report it to the attending nephrologist so the dose can be reduced per the unit protocol, and recheck Hb in two to four weeks.
Why it matters
Higher Hb targets were associated with more strokes and cardiovascular events in trials; the ceiling is graded 1D.

The grandfather who wants his son’s kidney

What you see
A 52-year-old jeepney driver with CKD G5 not yet on dialysis has Hb 7.9 g/dL and mild exertional breathlessness. His son is being worked up as a living donor. The admitting team writes for two units.
What is likely going on
Symptomatic but stable anemia in a transplant candidate.
What to do
Look for correctable causes, start iron if the thresholds are met, discuss an ESA, and keep transfusion for instability, active bleeding or symptoms at rest, after talking with the transplant team.
Why it matters
Transfusion can sensitize him to HLA antigens and narrow his transplant options; KDIGO advises avoiding it in candidates when possible.

The vials in the ice box

What you see
A 47-year-old teacher with CKD G4 in Tacloban has a falling Hb on an ESA. She keeps her vials in a styrofoam box with ice because power was cut after a typhoon.
What is likely going on
Likely spoiled ESA or missed doses, not true hyporesponsiveness.
What to do
Ask about storage and doses taken, check iron status, and work with the family and the social worker on safe storage before changing the plan.
Why it matters
Missed or spoiled doses look like hyporesponsiveness in the chart and lead to needless dose escalation.
Field notes
  • Write the iron threshold and its grade next to every iron order: “ferritin 90, TSAT 14%, meets KDIGO 2026 non-dialysis threshold (2D).”
  • Ask “Are you waiting for a kidney transplant?” before you sign for a unit of blood.
  • Combine blood draws and stop daily CBCs once the trend is clear; phlebotomy is a cause of anemia in hospital.
  • Ask the family how ESA vials are stored and whether any doses were skipped for cost.
  • When Hb will not rise, list the causes you checked in the note before anyone suggests a higher dose.
Case review

Back to the case: The ward consult on a Thursday

Before anything is given. Send a reticulocyte count and a blood smear now, before the first unit or the first iron dose, and add vitamin B12, folate and fecal occult blood. Ask the intern to document the dark stools, the daily mefenamic acid and any past transfusions. Stop the NSAID. Her picture is not simple renal anemia: microcytosis, a ferritin of 90 ng/mL and two weeks of dark stools on an NSAID point to gut blood loss on top of erythropoietin deficiency. Ask gastroenterology about endoscopy. Her ankle swelling and breathlessness also need a volume assessment, because a fluid-overloaded heart makes anemia feel worse.

Iron. She is not on dialysis, so the KDIGO 2026 threshold is ferritin below 100 ng/mL with TSAT below 40%, or ferritin 100 to 300 ng/mL with TSAT below 25% (2D). With ferritin 90 ng/mL and TSAT 14% she meets it clearly. Her irregular oral iron failed, possibly because of cost, side effects and hepcidin, and she may be losing blood faster than tablets can replace it. Intravenous iron in hospital, given where a reaction can be managed and once infection is excluded, is a reasonable shared choice. Write the doses from the formulary. Recheck Hb, ferritin and TSAT in about three months.

Transfusion and ESA. She is breathless on stairs, not at rest, has no chest pain, and her blood pressure is stable. Transfusion tonight is not clearly needed and carries a real cost: her son’s offer of a kidney makes her a transplant candidate, and KDIGO advises avoiding transfusion in candidates when possible. Transfuse if she bleeds actively, becomes unstable or develops symptoms at rest. Tell her daughter to pause the donor search until the team decides. An ESA comes after iron and the bleeding source are dealt with. If Hb stays low and she has symptoms, an ESA is the first choice over a HIF-PHI (2D), with Hb kept below 11.5 g/dL (1D). Discuss cost honestly; outside the dialysis package her family will pay for each vial.

  • Orders or collects the KDIGO first test set (CBC, reticulocytes, ferritin, TSAT) before any iron or blood is given.
  • Interprets ferritin and TSAT together and states whether the patient meets a KDIGO 2026 iron threshold, with its grade.
  • Lists the correctable causes found and the plan for each before an ESA is discussed.
  • States the Hb ceiling for an adult on an ESA, its grade, and the trials behind it, in plain words a patient can follow.
  • Writes a transfusion justification that names the symptom, the transplant status and the plan for the cause.
Placement activity. Medical ward, renal clinic or hemodialysis unit

Question 1 of 5An intern is asked to send iron studies for a patient with CKD who is about to receive a first transfusion tonight. What should the intern do?
Transfused red cells change the indices, reticulocytes and ferritin, so samples drawn afterward mislead. Sending only ferritin fails because ferritin is an acute-phase protein and KDIGO thresholds always pair it with TSAT.
Question 2 of 5A man with CKD G3b not on dialysis has Hb 10.2 g/dL, ferritin 180 ng/mL and TSAT 19%. By KDIGO 2026, what is the iron decision?
For people not on dialysis or on peritoneal dialysis, KDIGO 2026 suggests iron if ferritin is below 100 ng/mL with TSAT below 40%, or ferritin 100 to 300 ng/mL with TSAT below 25% (2D). The first option applies only the ferritin cut point and ignores the TSAT arm.
Question 3 of 5A fellow is asked why KDIGO 2026 recommends keeping Hb below 11.5 g/dL in adults on an ESA. Which answer is most accurate?
No trial tested 11.5 g/dL itself. CHOIR showed more composite events and TREAT nearly doubled stroke at higher targets, with little or no quality-of-life gain. The second option is wrong because quality of life did not meaningfully improve.
Question 4 of 5A 45-year-old woman on maintenance hemodialysis in Cebu has Hb 9.4 g/dL, ferritin 420 ng/mL and TSAT 24% with no infection. What does KDIGO 2026 suggest?
In hemodialysis, KDIGO 2026 suggests starting iron if ferritin is ≤500 ng/mL and TSAT ≤30% (2D), intravenously rather than orally (2D). The 300 ng/mL cut point applies to patients not on dialysis or on peritoneal dialysis.
Question 5 of 5A man with CKD G4 on an ESA has had a falling Hb for three months despite rising doses. What should the resident do first?
KDIGO 2026 asks you to identify and treat the causes of hyporesponsiveness first (practice point). Escalating the dose is the trap: in the trials, patients needing the most ESA did worst. A HIF-PHI trial is considered only after causes are addressed.
