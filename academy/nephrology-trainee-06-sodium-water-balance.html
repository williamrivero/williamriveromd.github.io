Training track for nephrology fellows, residents and medical interns

Study material for medical interns, residents and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and gives no drug doses. Doses, rates and order sets come from current guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved protocols and your supervising physician. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Intern: Recognize hyponatremia with moderately severe or severe symptoms, such as vomiting, confusion, somnolence or seizure, and report it to your resident at once with the sodium, glucose and medicine list.
  • Resident: Classify a sodium disorder by tonicity, volume and urine studies, and propose a monitored plan that stays within the house correction limit of 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and 8 mmol/L in each 24 hours after.
  • Fellow: Anticipate and manage overcorrection, treat the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIAD), low-solute hyponatremia (beer potomania, “tea and toast”) and hypernatremia, and explain where the guidelines and the newer evidence disagree.

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · the number that moves too fastA sodium disorder is a water disorder, and its danger lies in speed: a sodium that fell fast swells the brain, and a sodium corrected fast after it fell slowly can destroy it. The intern owns recognition and the first call. The resident owns the classification and a plan with a written ceiling. The fellow owns the trajectory: predicting the water diuresis before it comes, and stopping the rise before it crosses the limit.
In plain wordsThink of brain cells as soft water balloons packed inside a hard clay pot, the skull. If the water outside the balloons becomes more dilute, water moves in and the balloons swell against the pot. Given a day or two, the balloons let some of their contents leak out, shrink back, and fit the pot again.

The picture has a limit. Brain cells do not leak only salt. Over about 48 hours they export potassium, chloride and organic osmolytes such as glutamate, myo-inositol and taurine, and they return to near-normal volume despite a low plasma sodium. That adaptation protects the patient with chronic hyponatremia, and it is also the trap. Reloading those osmolytes takes days. If the plasma sodium is raised faster than the cells can recover their solutes, water leaves the adapted cells, they shrink, and the myelin of the pons and other regions can break down days later: osmotic demyelination. The European guideline uses 48 hours to separate acute from chronic hyponatremia, and treats hyponatremia of unknown duration as chronic.1

The plasma sodium measures the ratio of sodium to water, not the amount of either. Most hyponatremia comes from water retained under the action of antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin), not from salt lost. Most hypernatremia comes from water lost or never drunk. Every classification and every treatment follows from asking why the kidney is or is not excreting water.

Most hyponatremia on a medical ward is mild and chronic, and the work is finding the cause. A few patients arrive seizing, and a few are harmed by the treatment itself. Your work is to treat severe symptoms promptly, to raise the sodium only as far as the brain can follow, and to watch the urine for the water diuresis that signals overcorrection is coming.

Opening case · fictional

Case: The seizure at 10:50 p.m.

Tuesday night, the emergency room of a tertiary hospital in Cebu City. Lola Teresita Ybañez, 70, from Talisay City, weighs 45 kg. Three months ago a clinic started her on a fixed combination of losartan and hydrochlorothiazide for blood pressure and told her to avoid salt. Since then she has eaten mostly rice porridge, crackers and tea. During the hot weeks of the dry season she drank “many glasses of water” to stay cool. For two days she has had nausea, then vomiting, then confusion.

Ten minutes after arrival she has a generalized seizure that stops after treatment by the emergency team. Her laboratory results return: sodium 112 mmol/L, potassium 2.9 mmol/L, glucose 108 mg/dL, creatinine 0.8 mg/dL, serum osmolality 238 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 360 mOsm/kg and urine sodium 46 mmol/L. She is drowsy but rousable, with a blood pressure of 112/70 mm Hg and dry mucous membranes.

The intern, Dr. Jessa Lim, calls the internal medicine resident, Dr. Carlo Mendoza. The nephrology fellow on call, Dr. Ana Villanueva, is in the hospital.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What must happen in the first hour, and what is the target?
  • How do you classify her hyponatremia, and what caused it?
  • What will make her sodium rise too fast over the next 48 hours, and how will the team prevent it?

Lesson · part 1

How the kidney handles water

Plasma tonicity is held in a narrow range by two controls: thirst, which brings water in, and ADH, which lets the collecting duct keep it. A rise in tonicity of a few percent triggers both. ADH inserts aquaporin-2 water channels into the collecting duct, and water moves from the dilute urine into the concentrated medulla. Without ADH the duct stays watertight and the urine is dilute.

ADH has a second trigger that overrides the first. A large fall in effective arterial volume, from vomiting, bleeding, heart failure or cirrhosis, releases ADH even when the plasma is already dilute. The body chooses to defend volume over tonicity. Nausea, pain, stress and many medicines also release ADH. This is why so many hospital patients become hyponatremic when given hypotonic fluid.

The kidney’s ability to excrete water also depends on solute. Urine cannot be diluted below a floor of roughly 50 to 100 mOsm/kg, so the daily solute load sets the most water a person can excrete. Someone eating normally excretes several hundred milliosmoles a day and can clear many liters of water. Someone living on tea, crackers or beer may excrete only a fraction of that, and a few liters of water is then more than the kidney can remove. This is low-solute hyponatremia, often called beer potomania or “tea and toast” hyponatremia.

Lesson · part 2

Classifying hyponatremia: tonicity, then urine, then volume

Classification protects you from treating the wrong disorder. The European guideline sets three biochemical grades: mild, 130 to 135 mmol/L; moderate, 125 to 129 mmol/L; and profound, below 125 mmol/L. It grades symptoms separately as moderately severe (for example nausea without vomiting, confusion, headache) or severe (for example vomiting, cardiorespiratory distress, deep somnolence, seizures, coma). Symptoms, not the number, decide how urgently you act.1

First, exclude non-hypotonic hyponatremia. Glucose in the plasma draws water out of cells and dilutes the sodium; the true tonicity may be normal or high. Hillier and colleagues raised glucose in six healthy volunteers and found an average fall of 2.4 mmol/L of sodium for every 100 mg/dL rise in glucose, larger than the traditional 1.6, and larger still above 400 mg/dL.2 Mannitol and radiocontrast do the same. Severe hyperlipidemia or paraproteinemia can produce a falsely low sodium with older laboratory methods (pseudohyponatremia), which a direct ion-selective electrode avoids. Measure serum osmolality when in doubt.

Second, read the urine osmolality. A urine osmolality of 100 mOsm/kg or less means ADH is suppressed and points to excess water intake or low solute. Above that, ADH is acting.1

Third, read the urine sodium with the volume examination. A urine sodium of 30 mmol/L or less suggests low effective arterial volume, from true volume loss or from heart failure or cirrhosis. Above 30 mmol/L, consider diuretics, kidney disease, SIAD, adrenal insufficiency and vomiting with bicarbonaturia.1 Diuretics confound every urine test; a thiazide makes a dry patient look like SIAD.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIAD) is a diagnosis of exclusion: hypotonic hyponatremia, urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg, clinical euvolemia, urine sodium above 30 mmol/L on a normal salt and water intake, and no diuretic, adrenal or thyroid explanation. For SIAD with moderate or profound hyponatremia, the European guideline suggests fluid restriction as first-line treatment (2D) and, as second-line options, increased solute intake with urea or low-dose loop diuretics with oral sodium chloride (2D). It recommends against vasopressin receptor antagonists in moderate and profound hyponatremia (1C).1 The United States expert panel is more accepting of vaptans in selected patients.3 The difference reflects how each panel weighed the risk of overly rapid correction and liver injury against the benefit in sodium, a surrogate. For hypovolemic hyponatremia, the European guideline recommends restoring extracellular volume with 0.9% saline or a balanced crystalloid (1B).1

Philippine practice. Thiazides and thiazide-like diuretics are common in fixed-dose combinations, cheap and often started at primary care, and older thin women eating little salt or protein are at particular risk. During the hot months many older Filipinos drink large amounts of water while eating little. Ask the family exactly what the patient ate and drank this week; it often explains the sodium better than any test.

RememberBefore you treat a low sodium, check glucose and tonicity, read the urine osmolality and sodium, and look at the medicine list for a thiazide. The cause decides the treatment and the speed.
Lesson · part 3

Severe symptoms: treat the brain, then set the ceiling

A seizing patient with hyponatremia has cerebral edema now. A small rise in sodium, a few millimoles per liter, shrinks the brain enough to stop seizures and reduce intracranial pressure. The European guideline recommends prompt intravenous hypertonic saline for severe symptoms (1D), with a check of the sodium after each bolus, aiming for a 5 mmol/L rise (2D).1 The volumes and concentrations belong to the guideline and your unit’s protocol; this module gives none. Both the European guideline and the United States panel support this bolus approach.4

The bolus approach was tested against slow continuous infusion. The SALSA (saline bolus versus slow infusion) trial, a randomized comparison of the two methods, enrolled 178 adults with symptomatic hyponatremia (glucose-corrected sodium 125 mmol/L or less, mean 118 mmol/L) in three Korean hospitals. Overcorrection, defined in that trial as a rise of more than 12 mmol/L in 24 hours or 18 mmol/L in 48 hours, occurred in 17.2% with rapid intermittent boluses and 24.2% with slow continuous infusion, a difference that was not significant. Fewer patients in the bolus group needed treatment to re-lower the sodium (41.4% versus 57.1%; number needed to treat about 6), and more reached the target within 1 hour in the intention-to-treat analysis (32.2% versus 17.6%).5 Note that SALSA’s overcorrection threshold is looser than the house standard, so even its “safe” patients could have exceeded 10 mmol/L in a day.

Once the symptoms improve or the sodium has risen by about 5 mmol/L, the emergency is over and the problem becomes chronic hyponatremia again. Stop the hypertonic saline, treat the cause, and set the ceiling.

The house standard and the newer evidence

For chronic hyponatremia, this course uses the European guideline’s limits as its house standard: no more than 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and no more than 8 mmol/L in each 24 hours after, until the sodium reaches 130 mmol/L (1D).1 The United States panel sets goals of 4 to 8 mmol/L a day, 4 to 6 in patients at high risk of osmotic demyelination, with limits of 10 to 12 mmol/L a day, or 8 in high-risk patients.3 High-risk features include a sodium of 105 mmol/L or less, alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, hypokalemia and advanced liver disease.3

A 2025 meta-analysis challenges how slow is too slow. Ayus and colleagues pooled 16 cohort studies with 11,811 hospitalized adults with severe hyponatremia. Rapid correction (8 to 10 mmol/L or more in 24 hours) was associated with 32 fewer in-hospital deaths per 1,000 patients than slow correction (odds ratio 0.67; moderate-certainty evidence) and with no statistically significant increase in osmotic demyelination.6 The studies were observational. Patients whose sodium rises slowly are often sicker, with heart failure, cirrhosis or cancer, and that confounding can produce the association without any causal effect of speed. The analysis shows that very slow correction is not proven safe; it does not show that crossing the house limit is safe. Keep the ceiling, and do not let fear of the ceiling stop you treating the patient.

Lesson · part 4

Preventing and rescuing overcorrection

Overcorrection is usually caused by the patient, not the drip. When the stimulus for ADH disappears, the kidney suddenly excretes large volumes of dilute urine, and the sodium climbs by several millimoles per liter in a few hours. The common triggers are predictable:

TriggerWhy ADH falls or water excretion risesWhat you see
Volume restored after vomiting or diarrheaThe volume stimulus for ADH disappearsUrine output rises hours after saline
Thiazide stoppedDiluting capacity returnsRising dilute urine over the first day
Solute given to a low-solute patientFood or saline supplies the solute the kidney needed to excrete waterWater diuresis after the first meals
Nausea, pain or drug effect resolvedNon-osmotic ADH release stopsDilute urine after symptoms settle
Potassium repletedPotassium entering cells pulls sodium out of them, and potassium given raises plasma sodiumA rise larger than the saline explains

George and colleagues reviewed 1,490 patients admitted with sodium below 120 mmol/L in seven hospitals. Rapid correction, a rise of more than 8 mmol/L at 24 hours, occurred in 41%. Younger age, female sex, schizophrenia, a lower presenting sodium and a urine sodium below 30 mmol/L were associated with greater risk. Eight patients (0.5%) developed osmotic demyelination; five had beer potomania, five had hypokalemia, and seven had a rise of more than 8 mmol/L in a 24-hour period beforehand. Five of the eight recovered neurologically.7 The study was retrospective and found demyelination only in those who had imaging, so the true rate may be higher.

Three strategies

Reactive: watch the urine and the sodium, and act when a water diuresis begins. Proactive: in a patient at high risk, give desmopressin from the start to hold ADH activity constant, so the sodium rises only with the hypertonic saline given. Sood and colleagues reported this in 25 patients with sodium below 120 mmol/L; mean rises were 5.8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and 4.5 mmol/L in the second, with no correction beyond 12 mmol/L in 24 hours.8 This was a quality improvement report without controls; it shows feasibility, not superiority. Rescue: when a limit has been crossed, the European guideline recommends prompt intervention to re-lower the sodium (1D): stop the active treatment, and consult an expert about electrolyte-free water and desmopressin with strict monitoring (1D).1 Re-lowering is an attending decision.

The house standard applies to the whole 24-hour window, measured from the first sodium. A rise of 7 mmol/L in the first six hours leaves only 3 mmol/L for the next 18. Write the cumulative rise and the clock time beside every result.

RememberWatch the urine more closely than the drip. A sudden flow of dilute urine means the sodium will soon cross the ceiling unless someone acts.
Lesson · part 5

Hypernatremia: the patient who could not reach the water

Hypernatremia means water deficit relative to sodium. Because thirst is powerful, it develops almost only in people who cannot drink when thirsty: infants, older adults with dementia or stroke, sedated and ventilated patients, and anyone whose water depends on someone else. The causal chain is water lost (fever, fast breathing, sweating, diarrhea, an osmotic diuresis from glucose or urea, diabetes insipidus) and not replaced, or, less often, sodium gained (hypertonic fluids, bicarbonate, salt ingestion).

Brain cells shrink at first and then gain osmolytes over a day or two, so chronic hypernatremia carries the opposite risk to chronic hyponatremia: correcting too fast could, in theory, swell the brain. Seay and colleagues note that goals for the correction rate of hypernatremia are less well established than for hyponatremia.9 Some studies suggest a fall of no more than 0.5 mmol/L per hour. Chauhan and colleagues examined 449 critically ill adults with sodium above 155 mmol/L and found no association between faster correction and death (adjusted odds ratio 1.3 in each group, not significant), and no case of cerebral edema attributable to rapid correction on chart review.10 It was a single-database observational study. Correct steadily, monitor, and do not leave a hypernatremic patient untreated out of fear of speed.

Philippine practice. Many bedridden older patients in the Philippines are cared for at home by family members or hired caregivers, and many are fed by nasogastric tube with commercial formulas or blended food. Free water flushes are easy to forget, and hot months increase losses. When you admit an older patient with hypernatremia, ask who gives the water and how much, and teach that person before discharge. The tonicity calculators on this site help with the arithmetic; the plan belongs to the team.

In childrenChildren become hyponatremic easily in hospital because illness, pain and nausea release ADH; the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends isotonic maintenance fluids with appropriate potassium chloride and dextrose for patients 28 days to 18 years old (evidence quality A; strong recommendation).11 Infants with gastroenteritis and children with diabetic ketoacidosis are at risk of hypernatremia and of cerebral edema during treatment, and sodium targets are set by the pediatric team. On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The drinking session that ended in confusion

What you see
A 45-year-old tricycle driver in Pampanga who drinks beer daily and eats little is admitted confused with a sodium of 109 mmol/L and a potassium of 2.8 mmol/L. His urine osmolality is 90 mOsm/kg.
What is likely going on
Low-solute hyponatremia (beer potomania) with very high risk of osmotic demyelination: low sodium, hypokalemia, malnutrition, alcohol use.
What to do
Call the attending; plan correction well below the house ceiling, monitor urine output hourly, and expect a water diuresis once food and potassium are given.
Why it matters
In one large cohort, five of eight patients with osmotic demyelination had beer potomania; their kidneys excrete water briskly the moment solute arrives.

The post-operative patient on hypotonic fluid

What you see
A 60-year-old woman in Quezon City on day one after a hysterectomy has nausea and pain, and has received dextrose in water overnight. Her sodium has fallen from 138 to 127 mmol/L and she has a headache.
What is likely going on
Acute hospital-acquired hyponatremia from ADH released by pain and nausea, with free water infused.
What to do
Stop the hypotonic fluid, report the headache as a moderately severe symptom, and ask the resident to assess; acute hyponatremia carries a higher risk of brain swelling.
Why it matters
Acute hyponatremia (under 48 hours) has not given the brain time to adapt.

The thiazide started last month

What you see
A thin 78-year-old woman in Iloilo started indapamide four weeks ago and comes to clinic tired and unsteady, with a sodium of 123 mmol/L and no other symptoms.
What is likely going on
Thiazide-associated hyponatremia, moderate, without severe symptoms.
What to do
Stop the thiazide, review diet and fluid intake, and recheck within days; tell the family that a fall or new confusion needs urgent review.
Why it matters
Thiazide hyponatremia is common in older, thin women and can recur if the drug is restarted.

The lola at home on tube feeds

What you see
An 85-year-old bedridden woman in Bulacan fed by nasogastric tube is brought in drowsy with a sodium of 163 mmol/L during a heat wave. Her caregiver gives formula but no extra water.
What is likely going on
Hypernatremia from water deficit with no access to water and increased losses.
What to do
Replace the water deficit steadily with monitoring, find and treat any infection, and teach the caregiver free water flushes before discharge.
Why it matters
Hypernatremia is usually a problem of access; the fix is in the home as much as the ward.
Field notes
  • Write the baseline sodium and its clock time at the top of every correction plan; the 24-hour window starts there.
  • Order hourly urine output for every patient treated for profound hyponatremia; the urine warns you first.
  • Correct the potassium knowing that it will raise the sodium too.
  • Ask the family what the patient ate and drank this week, and write it down.
  • Use the site’s sodium trajectory tracker to plot every result against the ceiling.
Case review

Back to the case: The seizure at 10:50 p.m.

The first hour, and the target. A seizure with a sodium of 112 mmol/L is hyponatremia with severe symptoms. Dr. Lim calls immediately, rechecks glucose (108 mg/dL, so the hyponatremia is truly hypotonic), lists the losartan–hydrochlorothiazide, and starts hourly urine output. Dr. Mendoza calls the attending, moves Lola Teresita to a monitored bed, and starts the hypertonic saline bolus approach under the guideline and unit protocol (1D), checking the sodium after each bolus. The target for this phase is a rise of about 5 mmol/L (2D) or an end to the symptoms, not a normal sodium. At 11:50 p.m. her sodium is 117 mmol/L and she is more awake; the boluses stop.

Classification and cause. Her serum osmolality is low, so the hyponatremia is hypotonic. Her urine osmolality of 360 mOsm/kg shows ADH is acting. Her urine sodium of 46 mmol/L would suggest SIAD, but she is on a thiazide and has been vomiting, which confound it, and her dry mucous membranes suggest volume depletion. The likely mechanism is several at once: a thiazide impairing dilution, vomiting and low volume driving ADH, a low-solute diet limiting water excretion, and a high water intake in the heat. Hypokalemia, from the thiazide and vomiting, adds to the risk.

The next 48 hours. Dr. Villanueva counts the triggers for a water diuresis: the thiazide is stopped, her volume will be restored, potassium repletion will raise her sodium, and her first full meals will supply solute. She is also at high risk of osmotic demyelination: profound hyponatremia, hypokalemia and poor nutrition. The ceiling is written from the first sodium at 10:40 p.m.: no more than 122 mmol/L by 10:40 p.m. tomorrow, and the attending asks for a lower goal within it, as the United States panel suggests for high-risk patients. At 6 a.m. her urine output reaches 350 mL an hour, dilute, and her sodium is 120 mmol/L. The fellow calls the attending, who decides, as the guideline advises with expert input, to hold the water diuresis with desmopressin under close monitoring. The family is taught that the “low salt” advice was not meant to mean tea and crackers, and her blood pressure medicine is changed.

Question 1 of 5A 74-year-old man with a sodium of 118 mmol/L has been vomiting repeatedly and is now hard to rouse. What should the intern do first?
Vomiting and deep somnolence are severe symptoms in the European guideline. Severe symptoms decide urgency, not the number. Fluid restriction is a treatment for SIAD without severe symptoms.
Question 2 of 5A euvolemic woman not on diuretics has a sodium of 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 258 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 480 mOsm/kg and urine sodium 62 mmol/L, with normal thyroid and adrenal tests. What is the best classification and first-line treatment per the European guideline?
Low tonicity, urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg, urine sodium above 30 mmol/L, euvolemia and no diuretic, adrenal or thyroid cause fit SIAD. Saline can worsen SIAD because the kidney excretes the sodium and keeps the water.
Question 3 of 5A patient with chronic hyponatremia started at 110 mmol/L. Twelve hours later the sodium is 119 mmol/L and the urine output is 300 mL an hour, dilute. What does the house standard require?
Nine of the 10 mmol/L allowed in the first 24 hours are used in 12 hours, and a water diuresis has begun. The European guideline recommends prompt intervention to re-lower if a limit is exceeded (1D); acting now may avoid it.
Question 4 of 5A man with poorly controlled diabetes has a measured sodium of 126 mmol/L and a glucose of 900 mg/dL. Using the Hillier correction, what is his glucose-corrected sodium, and what does it mean?
Corrected sodium = 126 + 2.4 × (900 − 100) ÷ 100 = about 145 mmol/L. His effective osmolality is high; the sodium will rise as insulin lowers the glucose, revealing the water deficit.
Question 5 of 5A 2025 meta-analysis of 16 cohort studies associated rapid correction of severe hyponatremia with lower in-hospital mortality. How should a fellow explain this on rounds?
Patients whose sodium rises slowly are often sicker. Observational associations cannot establish that speed itself saves lives. The analysis found no statistically significant increase in osmotic demyelination, which is not the same as proving safety.
