How this examination works

This examination covers the whole Renal Transplant Coordinator series. Each sitting draws 20 questions at random from a bank of 200, one from each module, in random order, with the answer choices shuffled. Choose the single best answer for each question, then select Check answers.

When to take itAfter you have finished every module and reviewed the questions you missed.
How to take itIn one sitting, without notes. Allow about one minute per question.
Proposed pass mark16 of 20 best answers (80 percent). Your trainer may set a different mark.
If you fall shortEach explanation names its module. Reread those modules, then select New set of questions.
What a pass meansA pass shows that you know the material. It does not show that you can do the work. Competence is demonstrated on your unit’s own equipment and signed off by a preceptor.

How to get your certificate of completion

  1. Pass all 20 module quizzes. Each module quiz is passed at 80%, or at the pass mark the module states. The Certificate checklist above the examination shows which modules are done; each line links to that module's quiz.
  2. Use the same device and browser for the modules and this examination. Progress is saved in your browser, not in an account. On iPhone and iPad, the Home Screen app and Safari keep separate progress.
  3. Pass this timed examination (80%; where these instructions say so, every safety-critical question must also be correct). You have one minute per question from the moment you tap Start the examination; only your first check of a set counts. If you pass before finishing the modules, your pass is saved: finish the module quizzes, then come back here.
  4. Type your name the way you want it printed and tap Make my certificate. You can tick Add a preceptor sign-off line if your unit will sign the printed copy.
  5. Save or print your completion packet: two pages, the certificate and a record of your module results. Use Print or save as PDF to keep both pages together.
  6. Be online when you make it, so the certificate gets its QR code. Anyone can scan the code, or enter it at renalcarematters.com/verify, to confirm it.

Not finished yet? Use Print a progress record in the checklist to show a preceptor how far you have gone; it is clearly marked "not a certificate". The certificate is a self-study certificate of completion, not CPD units: competence is demonstrated on your own unit and signed off by a preceptor. To make the QR code work, your name, track, date and score are saved with the certificate code; to have them removed, use Report a correction on any guide.

Examination

End-of-course examination

Question 1Who decides recipient medical suitability?
Best: The coordinator assembles evidence and follows through on the team decision. Why not the others: “The insurance officer”: Payment eligibility is a separate decision. “The coordinator alone”: A coordinating role does not confer independent selection authority. “The donor family”: Family preference does not establish medical suitability. Module 1.
Question 2A donor asks to withdraw privately. What comes first?
Best: Voluntary donation requires a safe way to withdraw. Why not the others: “Ask the recipient to persuade the donor”: This introduces pressure. “Disclose the reason to the family”: The reason is confidential unless lawful disclosure applies. “Delete the donor file”: The clinical record must remain accurate and retained appropriately. Module 1.
Question 3What proves a barrier is closed?
Best: A referral sent is different from a service completed. Why not the others: “An unanswered email”: Sending does not confirm receipt or action. “A family assumption”: Assumptions cannot replace evidence. “A green dashboard chosen manually”: Status needs auditable supporting evidence. Module 1.
Question 4Can the same title automatically confer donor advocate authority?
Best: The functions and conflicts of interest require explicit review. Why not the others: “Yes, all coordinators are advocates”: Job titles are insufficient. “Yes, after this quiz”: Self-study does not confer a regulated function. “Yes, if the recipient agrees”: Recipient agreement cannot waive donor safeguards. Module 1.
Question 5Which navigation register entry is most useful?
Best: These fields support accountable follow-through. Why not the others: “Patient name alone”: Identity is necessary but not a workflow. “A list of completed tests only”: Open decisions and barriers would disappear. “Estimated surgery date only”: An estimate does not show readiness. Module 1.
Question 6A cluster randomized trial gave dialysis patients trained transplant-recipient navigators. What did it show?
Best: the trial measured process steps, and navigated patients completed about twice as many. Not shown: more transplants or lower mortality; those were not its outcomes. “No difference” is wrong for the primary outcome. Module 1.
Question 7A barrier row reads “Cardiology referral sent March 3.” Today is March 20 and no appointment exists. What is its status?
Best: a sent referral is not a completed service, and 17 days without an appointment is overdue. Sending, a family promise, or deletion all leave the barrier unresolved and unaudited. Module 1.
Question 8The recipient’s sister asks you, “Did my brother pass his donor tests?” The donor has not authorized sharing. You say:
Best: the donor’s results belong to the donor; you redirect to what you may share. Confirming, hinting, or sending her to read his file all breach his confidentiality. Module 1.
Question 9Which SBAR request is most useful to a transplant nephrologist?
Best: a specific decision, an owner, and a time. “FYI” asks for nothing; ordering a test yourself is outside coordination scope; family wishes are context, not a request. Module 1.
Question 10In the large US registry study by Wolfe and colleagues, how did death risk after transplant compare with remaining wait-listed?
Best: early surgical risk is real (relative risk 2.8 in the first two weeks), then risk falls well below the wait-listed group (0.32 at 18 months). The study was observational, so selection may explain part of it. Module 1.
Question 11Which source determines a hospital’s current transplant authorization?
Best: Facility authority must be verified with the regulator. Why not the others: “A course completion page”: Education cannot license a hospital. “A job advertisement”: An advertisement is employer evidence only. “A physician’s social media post”: This is not regulatory authorization. Module 2.
Question 12How should the 2015 60-hour criterion be described?
Best: The later surveyed tools and rules must be reconciled. Why not the others: “Guaranteed current universal minimum”: This was not verified. “Proof that any graduate may prescribe”: Training hours do not confer prescribing authority. “An American Board for Transplant Certification (ABTC) requirement”: ABTC has separate experience and examination rules. Module 2.
Question 13Does PSN guidance replace PhilHealth package rules?
Best: Both may apply to the same case. Why not the others: “Yes, all society guidance overrides payers”: The authorities differ. “Only if the patient requests it”: Patient preference cannot change payer rules. “Only for living donors”: Donor type does not merge the authorities. Module 2.
Question 14What should a new revision trigger?
Best: Effective dates and transitions affect real cases. Why not the others: “Immediate deletion of all older forms”: Legacy cases may still require them. “Automatic denial of pending cases”: Transition rules must first be applied. “No action until an audit”: Delayed review risks incorrect workflows. Module 2.
Question 15A task exceeds the coordinator’s authorized scope. What next?
Best: Navigation includes obtaining the right decision maker. Why not the others: “Do it if staffing is short”: Workload does not create authority. “Ask the family to sign a waiver”: A waiver does not expand scope. “Copy a previous order”: Prior orders do not authorize a new decision. Module 2.
Question 16PhilHealth Circular 2026-0019 was published November 21, 2026 and takes effect 15 days after publication. On November 25, 2026 it is best described as:
Best: publication starts the 15-day clock; on November 25 it has not run out. It is not a draft, and nothing limits it to deceased donors. Confirm the formal effective date before applying it. Module 2.
Question 17A recipient was pre-authorized and transplanted under PC 2024-0035. Revision 1 takes effect before the claim is filed. Which policy governs the claim?
Best: Revision 1 states that cases pre-authorized and performed under PC 2024-0035 are claimed under it. Newer is not automatically governing; neither payment size nor hospital preference decides. Module 2.
Question 18Each contracted facility must have at least one Z Benefits Coordinator. How many Z packages may one coordinator handle?
Best: the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) rule caps each coordinator at three Z Benefits packages. The other numbers are not in the circular. Module 2.
Question 19A job posting asks for a registered nurse with three years of experience. This is best understood as:
Best: a posting states what one employer wants. It is not a DOH rule, a PhilHealth condition, or a society credential. Module 2.
Question 20A support-path Z Benefits coordinator is asked by a busy resident to “just explain the consent form and get the donor to sign.” You:
Best: under administrative order (AO) 2021-0059 the surgeon obtains donor consent with independent witnessing. Donor agreement does not transfer that duty, and the recipient is never the right witness. Module 2.
Question 21What does referral establish?
Best: Selection follows multidisciplinary assessment. Why not the others: “Guaranteed surgery”: No evaluation outcome is guaranteed. “Guaranteed insurance payment”: Payer criteria remain separate. “Donor consent”: Donor consent is a separate process. Module 3.
Question 22A missed visit follows a transport failure. What is appropriate?
Best: A single event does not establish unwillingness to engage. Why not the others: “Close the file as nonadherent”: This ignores the documented barrier. “Exclude the patient from all future referral”: This is inequitable and outside scope. “Require a donor before rescheduling”: Evaluation does not require such a blanket rule. Module 3.
Question 23What is a closed-loop referral?
Best: Communication must reach an accountable recipient. Why not the others: “A referral fax sent once”: Transmission alone is insufficient. “A patient told to call around”: This transfers the entire navigation burden. “A note with no recipient”: There is no accountable handoff. Module 3.
Question 24What should orientation include?
Best: Balanced information supports informed choices. Why not the others: “Only successful transplant stories”: This hides risks and alternatives. “A promise of a short wait”: Organ availability is uncertain. “A request to recruit strangers online”: Unscreened donor recruitment raises serious ethical issues. Module 3.
Question 25A patient reports severe breathlessness during intake. What next?
Best: Potentially serious symptoms take priority. Why not the others: “Finish the financial form first”: Administrative tasks must not delay care. “Schedule a routine visit next month”: This may be unsafe. “Offer an independent medicine adjustment”: The coordinator must remain within authority. Module 3.
Question 26KDIGO 2020 advises referring potential candidates for transplant evaluation:
Best: early referral allows donor evaluation and a possible pre-emptive transplant. Waiting for dialysis, a donor, or a payer approval delays everything. Module 3.
Question 27In the House Calls trial, home education that included the family most clearly increased:
Best: inquiries (82% vs 61% vs 47%) and evaluations rose. The difference in transplants (15% vs 8% vs 6%) was not statistically significant. Survival was not measured. Module 3.
Question 28A referral arrives without a working phone number. The best first step is:
Best: the loop closes through the referrer. Waiting loses people, rejecting is unfair, and social media breaches privacy. Module 3.
Question 29Which intake question best uncovers an access barrier?
Best: open, neutral, and practical. The others are leading or shaming and invite a yes. Module 3.
Question 30A referral was faxed on May 2. On May 16 the clinic has no record of it. What happened to the loop?
Best: a referral closes only when the receiving service acknowledges it. Sending, telling the patient, or time passing confirms nothing. Module 3.
Question 31A test result has arrived. Is the workup item complete?
Best: Receipt does not establish a clinical decision. Why not the others: “Yes, arrival always means clearance”: An abnormal report may need action. “Yes, if the family has paid”: Payment cannot clear clinical risk. “Yes, if it is uploaded”: Storage is not clinical review. Module 4.
Question 32Who defines additional cardiac testing?
Best: The coordinator facilitates the authorized plan. Why not the others: “An automated completion tracker”: A tracker is not a clinician. “The patient’s employer”: Employment does not determine testing. “A generic course list alone”: Testing must be individualized. Module 4.
Question 33What does a good deferral plan contain?
Best: Deferral should lead to a navigable plan. Why not the others: “A red label alone”: No next step is defined. “An indefinite silence”: This leaves the patient without guidance. “Automatic permanent exclusion”: Deferral differs from permanent decline. Module 4.
Question 34What belongs in an MDT summary?
Best: The team needs concise traceable evidence. Why not the others: “Every unfiltered duplicated page”: Unstructured volume obscures key issues. “Only favorable results”: Selective reporting risks harm. “A coordinator’s guarantee of suitability”: Suitability is a team decision. Module 4.
Question 35Approved evaluation means automatic active listing. True?
Best: National listing and local evaluation are separate gates. Why not the others: “Yes, always”: The processes are distinct. “Yes, when a donor is present”: A donor does not create listing status. “Yes, after insurance enrollment”: Payer enrollment is another distinct process. Module 4.
Question 36Why organize the recipient workup by question rather than by test list?
Best: a normal test does not answer a question until its owner reviews it. The method does not cut tests, grant authority, or follow a payer rule. Module 4.
Question 37A stress test was done 14 months ago; the program’s protocol accepts 12 months. The case conference is next week. You:
Best: you flag; the physician decides. Presenting it as current misleads, ordering is outside scope, and hiding it removes information. Module 4.
Question 38KDIGO 2020 asks programs to assess adherence and its barriers before transplant because:
Best: barriers found early can be addressed. It does not mandate refusal, replace psychosocial assessment, or come from PhilHealth. Module 4.
Question 39The team defers a candidate until a dental infection is treated. The most accurate message is:
Best: deferral with a reason, a next step, and a review date. “Rejected” is false, “paperwork” hides a medical reason, and a guarantee is a promise nobody can make. Module 4.
Question 40A one-page case-conference summary should open with:
Best: a summary is built around the decision. Addresses add privacy risk, raw lists bury the question, and suitability is the team’s call. Module 4.
Question 41Matching ABO blood groups proves full compatibility. True?
Best: human leukocyte antigen (HLA) antibodies and crossmatch may change risk. Why not the others: “Yes, all other tests are optional”: This ignores other immunologic risks. “Yes, if donor and recipient are siblings”: Relationship does not guarantee compatibility. “Yes, if insurance approves”: Payment approval does not establish compatibility. Module 5.
Question 42A tube lacks required identifiers. What next?
Best: Specimen identity cannot safely be guessed. Why not the others: “Add identifiers from memory”: This may misattribute a sample. “Use a room number alone”: Room numbers are not reliable person identifiers. “Hide the error to avoid delay”: Concealment removes a safety barrier. Module 5.
Question 43A transfusion occurred after the last sample. What should the coordinator do?
Best: Sensitizing events can affect evaluation requirements. Why not the others: “Assume the old sample remains valid”: The team and laboratory must decide. “Diagnose rejection”: This is not a diagnosis of rejection. “Cancel candidacy permanently”: That is not the coordinator’s decision. Module 5.
Question 44A crossmatch report is equivocal. Who resolves its significance?
Best: The result needs specialist interpretation. Why not the others: “The courier”: Transport staff cannot interpret it. “The insurance liaison”: Payer staff do not resolve clinical meaning. “A color code chosen by the coordinator”: A status color cannot replace interpretation. Module 5.
Question 45Which report should guide the final readiness review?
Best: Version and identity both matter. Why not the others: “The first screenshot found”: It may be preliminary or outdated. “A family recollection”: Memory is not source documentation. “A report from a different donor”: Pair-specific evidence is required. Module 5.
Question 46A recipient received two units of blood during a hospital stay last week. Why must the transplant team and HLA laboratory know?
Best: transfusion, pregnancy, and prior transplant are sensitizing events. Blood group does not change, waiting time is unaffected, and it is a safety matter, not billing. Module 5.
Question 47A crossmatch sample is labeled with name only. The courier is waiting. You:
Best: an incompletely labeled sample is rejected, not repaired. Adding identifiers after the fact or delegating labeling breaks the chain. Module 5.
Question 48The laboratory calls with a “positive crossmatch” result. Your role is to:
Best: route a critical result immediately with read-back. Interpreting or announcing decisions is the team’s role; waiting delays a safety-relevant result. Module 5.
Question 49What does a high calculated panel reactive antibody (cPRA) mean in plain words?
Best: cPRA estimates how many donors a candidate would likely react against. It says nothing about blood group, infection, or age. Module 5.
Question 50ABO compatibility follows the same rule as blood transfusion. A group O recipient can usually receive a standard kidney from a donor of group:
Best: group O recipients carry anti-A and anti-B antibodies, so a standard transplant uses a group O donor. Other groups need a specialized incompatible program decided by the team. Module 5.
Question 51Does relatedness remove the need to assess coercion?
Best: Voluntariness must be assessed in every donor. Why not the others: “Yes, relatives are exempt”: The DOH ethical safeguards cover related donors too. “Only siblings are exempt”: No such exemption is established here. “Only if the family signs”: A signature cannot replace a free choice. Module 6.
Question 52Which donor age appears in the inspected AO 2021-0059?
Best: Use the Philippine rule and verify subsequent changes. Why not the others: “18 in every country”: International thresholds are not interchangeable. “Any age with parental permission”: This does not satisfy the cited Philippine living-donor provision. “No age criterion”: The order expressly includes one. Module 6.
Question 53Debt cancellation linked to donation should trigger what?
Best: A financial inducement concern must be examined. Why not the others: “Routine scheduling”: The concern is unresolved. “An informal promise to ignore it”: Concealment undermines safeguards. “A larger family payment”: This compounds the concern. Module 6.
Question 54Who should answer questions about the donor’s own wishes?
Best: Private discussion reduces external influence. Why not the others: “Only the recipient”: The interests are distinct. “Only the employer”: The employer may be a source of pressure. “The oldest family member”: Family hierarchy does not replace donor autonomy. Module 6.
Question 55What should the coordinator record about suspected coercion?
Best: Accurate facts support appropriate specialist review. Why not the others: “An unverified criminal label”: Do not replace assessment with accusations. “Nothing until proven in court”: Safety concerns need timely escalation. “A public social-media warning”: This exposes private information and may harm the people involved. Module 6.
Question 56Under DOH AO 2021-0059, a potential living donor must be at least:
Best: the order sets 21 or older. Do not import foreign thresholds; verify the current text. Module 6.
Question 57Which statement is a red flag for inducement?
Best: a promised benefit tied to donation is a potential reward and must be routed for review. Love, fear, and needing time are normal and deserve support. Module 6.
Question 58A donor wants to withdraw but fears the family’s reaction. The safest approach is to:
Best: neutral, truthful wording protects privacy. Disclosure breaches confidentiality, a false diagnosis is dishonest and may follow the donor, and persuasion is pressure. Module 6.
Question 59Which person should not double as the donor’s designated advocate?
Best: the recipient’s coordinator has a conflict of interest. The other options describe what an advocate should be. Module 6.
Question 60A systematic review of living donor costs found that most donors:
Best: about 80% had financial loss in 16 studies. Full reimbursement, earning money, or no travel cost do not match the data. Module 6.
Question 61Normal creatinine guarantees donor safety. True?
Best: Kidney function and other risks need specialist evaluation. Why not the others: “Yes, it is the only necessary test”: This ignores multiple risk domains. “Yes, if the donor feels healthy”: Symptoms do not exclude hidden risk. “Yes, if related”: Relatedness does not prove safety. Module 7.
Question 62Who explains individualized lifetime risk?
Best: The coordinator ensures access and understanding. Why not the others: “The insurance agent alone”: Coverage does not determine medical risk. “The recipient alone”: The donor needs independent clinical information. “An unvalidated web score alone”: A calculator cannot replace full assessment. Module 7.
Question 63When should follow-up arrangements be planned?
Best: The donor needs a feasible continuity plan. Why not the others: “Only if a complication occurs”: Routine long-term monitoring is also needed. “After insurance ends”: That creates an avoidable gap. “Never if the donor is healthy”: Donation does not remove the need for follow-up. Module 7.
Question 64A donor has an incidental abnormal result. What next?
Best: The donor’s own health matters regardless of donation. Why not the others: “Hide it from the donor”: This undermines informed care. “Tell the recipient every detail”: Private information requires lawful limits. “Ignore it if surgery is near”: Timing does not negate the finding. Module 7.
Question 65Does medical suitability replace HTEC review?
Best: Clinical and ethics review answer different questions. Why not the others: “Yes, for relatives”: Relatedness does not remove the safeguards. “Yes, after payment”: Payment cannot establish ethical approval. “Yes, with a normal scan”: Anatomic findings are not ethics clearance. Module 7.
Question 66In US national data, surgical mortality after living kidney donation was about:
Best: 3.1 per 10,000 (95% confidence interval (CI) 2.0 to 4.6). Rare but not zero; the other numbers are off by orders of magnitude. Module 7.
Question 67Muzaale and colleagues estimated 15-year kidney failure risk of 30.8 per 10,000 donors vs 3.9 per 10,000 matched healthy nondonors. The absolute difference is about:
Best: 30.8 minus 3.9 is about 27 more cases per 10,000 donors over 15 years. “8 times” is the relative ratio, not an absolute difference. Module 7.
Question 68The donor kidney failure risk calculator on this site is for:
Best: risk estimation informs a clinical decision made by the team. It does not clear donors or price benefits. Module 7.
Question 69In a national US study, how common were perioperative complications after donor nephrectomy?
Best: 16.8% any and 2.5% grade IV or higher. The other choices understate, overstate, or misplace the risk. Module 7.
Question 70A donor asks, “Am I safe for life?” The most honest answer is:
Best: calibrated, true, and paired with follow-up. Zero risk is false, certain harm is false, and ignorance is false. Module 7.
Question 71What resolves inconsistent identity documents?
Best: Source documents must remain authentic and traceable. Why not the others: “Editing the scanned certificate”: This falsifies source evidence. “Using whichever name is shorter”: Convenience does not resolve identity. “Ignoring the mismatch”: An unresolved discrepancy remains a safety and legal concern. Module 8.
Question 72Who obtains donor surgical informed consent under the cited DOH order?
Best: The coordinator facilitates and checks the process. Why not the others: “The insurance clerk”: Insurance work does not confer this authority. “The recipient”: The recipient cannot obtain consent on the surgeon’s behalf. “A quiz application”: Educational software cannot conduct the required process. Module 8.
Question 73A signed form proves valid informed consent by itself. True?
Best: Documentation records a process rather than replacing it. Why not the others: “Yes, regardless of language”: Unreadable information may not be understood. “Yes, even after withdrawal”: A donor may withdraw before donation. “Yes, if paid for”: Payment does not validate consent. Module 8.
Question 74A foreign paired-exchange pathway is offered online. What next?
Best: Jurisdiction-specific permission cannot be assumed. Why not the others: “Enroll immediately”: Foreign programs do not override local requirements. “Promise reimbursement”: Coverage is unverified. “Use a different donor identity”: This would be false documentation. Module 8.
Question 75What supports an accessible consent discussion?
Best: Understanding must be assessed in a suitable language. Why not the others: “A hurried signature”: Speed does not establish understanding. “Only an English form”: Language must meet the person’s needs. “The recipient answering every question”: The donor’s own understanding must be heard. Module 8.
Question 76A photocopied birth certificate is used to prove a sibling relationship. Before it enters the packet you should:
Best: authenticity is verified at the source. Appearance, a family signature, or retyping verify nothing. Module 8.
Question 77Under the living-donor order, who obtains the donor’s surgical consent?
Best: the surgeon obtains consent, with independent witnessing. Coordinators prepare and support; they do not obtain surgical consent. Module 8.
Question 78A donor understands Kapampangan best and reads little English. Consent should be supported by:
Best: independent interpretation plus teach-back tests understanding. The recipient has a conflict, an unread form is not consent, and shortening removes information. Module 8.
Question 79You notice a typo in the donor’s middle name on a notarized affidavit. You:
Best: corrections follow the legal route and stay traceable. Altering a notarized document yourself invalidates it; ignoring it may stop the case later. Module 8.
Question 80Consent is best described as:
Best: understanding is checked and choice remains open until surgery. One signature, permanence, and family consent all misdescribe it. Module 8.
Question 81What should a coordinator do with missing ethics evidence?
Best: An honest status supports a valid decision. Why not the others: “Mark it complete to secure a slot”: This misrepresents readiness. “Borrow another donor’s report”: Evidence must be person-specific. “Backdate the missing report”: Backdating falsifies the record. Module 9.
Question 82Should donor answers be coached to match the recipient?
Best: Coaching undermines voluntariness review. Why not the others: “Yes, to reduce delays”: Speed cannot justify misrepresentation. “Yes, if relatives agree”: Agreement does not remove coercion risk. “Only for an insured case”: Insurance does not change ethics standards. Module 9.
Question 83Who resolves an ethics approval condition?
Best: Closure must satisfy the actual condition. Why not the others: “The coordinator by deleting it”: Deletion does not resolve it. “The operating-room scheduler”: Scheduling staff do not approve ethics conditions. “An online vote”: Committee governance cannot be replaced by public opinion. Module 9.
Question 84A credible reward concern arises after approval. What next?
Best: Changed circumstances may invalidate prior assumptions. Why not the others: “Ignore it because approval exists”: Earlier review does not cover undisclosed changes automatically. “Ask the family to keep quiet”: Concealment compounds risk. “Collect a private fee”: This creates further conflict. Module 9.
Question 85Does an expiring surgery slot override ethics review?
Best: Time pressure does not remove donor protections. Why not the others: “Yes, if the recipient is distressed”: Distress requires support, not bypass. “Yes, after insurance authorization”: Payment approval is separate. “Yes, after a family waiver”: A waiver cannot replace the required review. Module 9.
Question 86Under Philippine law, recruiting or obtaining a person by coercion or deceit for the removal or sale of organs is:
Best: removal or sale of organs is an exploitative purpose under the anti-trafficking law. Consent obtained by coercion or deceit is not a defense. Module 9.
Question 87You suspect a donor is being paid. Your first action is to:
Best: document and escalate. Confronting, investigating alone, or canceling exceed your role and may put the donor at risk. Module 9.
Question 88A complete ethics committee submission should include:
Best: the committee needs the question and the evidence, including concerns. A lone form, the recipient’s history alone, or a push to approve does not support a real decision. Module 9.
Question 89The committee approves with a condition: a second psychiatric review. The case may proceed to surgery when:
Best: a conditional approval is open until the condition closes. A date, family agreement, or payer approval do not close it. Module 9.
Question 90The Declaration of Istanbul (2018 edition) is best described as:
Best: it guides professional ethics. It is not Philippine law, a payer rule, or a US policy, though Philippine law reaches many of the same acts. Module 9.
Question 91Does medical approval guarantee insurance approval?
Best: Both gates must be tracked. Why not the others: “Yes, in every case”: Clinical and payer decisions are separate. “Yes, after Hospital Transplant Ethics Committee (HTEC) approval”: Ethics review does not determine coverage. “Yes, for living donors”: Donor services also have defined pathways. Module 10.
Question 92How should a form be identified?
Best: Annex letters can change between versions. Why not the others: “By annex letter alone”: The same letter may mean a different form. “By file color”: Appearance is not version control. “By the family’s recollection”: Use the verified source document. Module 10.
Question 93A procedure date changes across a policy transition. What next?
Best: Dates can affect the governing process. Why not the others: “Assume every old rule remains”: This may be incorrect. “Assume every new rule applies retroactively”: Legacy-case provisions may apply. “Discard the prior authorization”: Preserve evidence until an authorized decision is made. Module 10.
Question 94Which is the safest coverage statement?
Best: It separates evidence from uncertainty. Why not the others: “Everything will be free forever”: This is an unsupported guarantee. “Donor costs are always the recipient’s private problem”: DOH protections require review. “Insurance will decide medical suitability”: Medical selection remains clinical. Module 10.
Question 95Where should changing benefit amounts live in the website?
Best: Centralization reduces inconsistent updates. Why not the others: “Hard-coded in every case”: Duplication makes updates error-prone. “In an unreferenced calculator”: Users cannot verify the rule. “Only in a hero image”: Images are difficult to maintain and access. Module 10.
Question 96Under PhilHealth’s kidney transplant package, an approved pre-authorization is valid for:
Best: 180 calendar days. Track the expiry; surgery after it needs a new or extended authorization as the circular allows. Module 10.
Question 97Pre-authorization approved March 1. Planned surgery September 15. What should you do?
Best: March 1 plus 180 days is August 28, before September 15. Flag early. Rescheduling is not your decision, and the benefit is not automatically lost. Module 10.
Question 98For a deceased-donor transplant, the pre-authorization request is submitted:
Best: the circular sets two working days after the procedure for deceased donors, because the timing cannot be planned. Module 10.
Question 99The facility estimate is PHP 1,200,000 for a living-donor transplant with laparoscopic donor surgery and basiliximab induction (package Z025A1, PHP 993,000). Before other assistance, the estimated gap is:
Best: 1,200,000 minus 993,000 equals 207,000. Then add confirmed assistance and check what the package actually covers for this case. Module 10.
Question 100Which statement about the post-transplant packages is correct?
Best: post-KT packages (adult and pediatric circulars) cover continuing care, including donor monitoring. The operation is the separate kidney transplant package. Module 10.
Question 101How should a date mismatch be corrected?
Best: The original record and correction history must remain traceable. Why not the others: “Rewrite the operative record to fit the claim”: This risks falsification. “Submit both dates without explanation”: The discrepancy remains unresolved. “Ask the patient to choose a date”: Care facts are not a preference. Module 11.
Question 102A payer notice is unclear. What next?
Best: The notice determines the response pathway. Why not the others: “Assume it is permanent denial”: A technical return may be remediable. “Ignore it until the patient complains”: Deadlines may be missed. “Alter the diagnosis”: Coding must reflect actual care. Module 11.
Question 103Medicine will run out while a claim is pending. What takes priority?
Best: Administrative resolution and clinical safety proceed together. Why not the others: “Wait for reimbursement before informing anyone”: This hides a foreseeable risk. “Advise dose rationing independently”: The coordinator must not alter treatment. “Tell the patient to stop attending”: This undermines continuity. Module 11.
Question 104What makes a claim auditable?
Best: The claim must be supported by actual care. Why not the others: “A complete-looking form without sources”: Appearance is not evidence. “Copied signatures”: Signatures require proper authorization. “A deleted prior submission”: Removing history weakens traceability. Module 11.
Question 105How should donor bills be handled?
Best: Payer limits alone do not settle all institutional obligations. Why not the others: “Charge automatically if a claim is delayed”: Delay does not remove protections. “Hide the bill”: The issue needs accountable resolution. “Ask the donor to sell another asset immediately”: This bypasses financial review and may worsen harm. Module 11.
Question 106A recipient is discharged on July 1. The facility’s claim filing deadline under the package is:
Best: July 1 plus 60 days is August 30. Late filing is a stated ground for denial. Module 11.
Question 107Which is a stated ground for denial of the kidney transplant claim?
Best: registry noncompliance is listed with late filing, missing mandatory services, and inconsistent data. The others are not denial grounds. Module 11.
Question 108A claim comes back asking for a corrected form. This is:
Best: a return is fixable; a denial is a decision with an appeal route. Neither is ever a reason to bill the donor. Module 11.
Question 109Under DOH AO 2021-0059, the transplant facility may charge the donor for donation-related medical care:
Best: donation-related medical expenses, donor care, and follow-up must not be charged to the donor by the facility. A signature does not change that. Module 11.
Question 110In a Canadian prospective cohort, what share of donors had total costs above CAD 5,500?
Best: 205 of 821 (25%). Costs were concentrated in a minority, which is why screening each donor’s situation matters. Module 11.
Question 111Does payer registry enrollment establish national waiting-list status?
Best: Their purposes and entry processes differ. Why not the others: “Yes, automatically”: This conflates systems. “Yes, if the claim is paid”: Payment does not determine national listing. “Only for adults”: Age does not make the systems identical. Module 12.
Question 112Who certifies death for donation?
Best: The coordinator facilitates but does not certify. Why not the others: “Any coordinator after this course”: Self-study does not confer that authority. “The recipient’s family”: A family cannot diagnose death. “The payer”: Insurance has no such role. Module 12.
Question 113A waiting candidate develops a new infection. What next?
Best: Health changes may affect readiness. Why not the others: “Keep it off the record”: Concealment threatens safety. “Remove the patient permanently alone”: The coordinator cannot make that decision. “Assume the previous clearance remains enough”: New information requires review. Module 12.
Question 114What should be promised about allocation?
Best: Allocation is governed by authorized rules. Why not the others: “Priority for whoever pays fastest”: Ability to pay is not an ethical personal allocation rule. “A fixed transplant date”: Organ availability is uncertain. “An organ from a named stranger online”: Unverified donor promises are inappropriate. Module 12.
Question 115What closes a listing transfer?
Best: A sent request alone does not finish transfer. Why not the others: “The patient leaving the city”: Travel is not registry reconciliation. “A new clinic appointment only”: Listing status still needs checking. “Deleting the old record”: Records require an authorized traceable process. Module 12.
Question 116Being registered in the PhilHealth KT Data Registry means:
Best: the payer registry and the national waiting list are separate. Neither guarantees an offer, and registry entry is not pre-authorization. Module 12.
Question 117From May 1, 2026, PhilHealth Advisory 2026-0026 requires KT registry registration for:
Best: all pre-authorized patients, through the electronic system. Manual forms were accepted until April 30, 2026. Module 12.
Question 118A wait-listed candidate moves to Cebu and changes phone numbers. Why does it matter?
Best: offers are time-critical. Contact details do not change biology, listing status, or the package. Module 12.
Question 119Who declares death before deceased organ donation?
Best: death determination is a medical act by authorized physicians, separate from the transplant team. Coordinators never do it. Module 12.
Question 120The Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) and Estimated Post-Transplant Survival (EPTS) scores on this site are:
Best: they come from US policy. Philippine allocation follows its own rules; coordinators do not accept offers on a score. Module 12.
Question 121Who accepts an organ offer clinically?
Best: The coordinator relays and documents the decision. Why not the others: “The courier”: Transport staff do not decide clinical acceptance. “The patient’s employer”: Employment is irrelevant to clinical acceptance. “The coordinator solely because the OR is ready”: Readiness does not confer decision authority. Module 13.
Question 122An identifier differs by one digit. What next?
Best: Small discrepancies can represent different donors. Why not the others: “Correct it by guessing”: Guessing risks wrong-organ events. “Trust the courier’s memory”: Independent verified evidence is needed. “Remove the label”: This destroys traceability. Module 13.
Question 123Why use read-back?
Best: Closed-loop communication reduces transcription errors. Why not the others: “To replace the original record”: The original evidence remains necessary. “To delay every offer”: Its purpose is accuracy, not delay. “To establish payment eligibility”: That is a separate process. Module 13.
Question 124Weather delays retrieval transport. What should happen?
Best: Clinical decisions may depend on updated timing. Why not the others: “Hide the delay”: The team needs accurate information. “Invent a shorter transport time”: False records undermine safety. “Guarantee the organ will remain suitable”: Suitability requires specialist assessment. Module 13.
Question 125What should a canceled offer trigger?
Best: Cancellation still needs coordinated support. Why not the others: “Automatic permanent delisting”: Cancellation does not imply permanent ineligibility. “Blame directed at the donor family”: This is harmful and inappropriate. “Deletion of the offer log”: The record is needed for care and quality review. Module 13.
Question 126During an offer call the caller says blood group “A.” Your form says the candidate is group O. You:
Best: a blood-group discrepancy is a safety event. Assuming, editing, or deferring the check can lead to a wrong-organ transplant. Module 13.
Question 127The core of a safe offer call is:
Best: structure and read-back catch errors. Memory and loose notes fail at night, and the coordinator’s role is to capture and route. Module 13.
Question 128Who decides whether to accept an organ offer for a candidate?
Best: acceptance is a clinical decision. The coordinator prepares the information and convenes the people. Module 13.
Question 129Time matters on an offer because:
Best: longer storage is associated with poorer early function. Payment, shifts, and waiting families are not the reason. Module 13.
Question 130After a call, you log the times. This matters because:
Best: the log feeds review, logistics, and documentation. It is not optional, does not replace the record, and decides nothing. Module 13.
Question 131Which approval alone clears surgery?
Best: Each approval answers a different question. Why not the others: “PhilHealth authorization alone”: Payment approval is not medical clearance. “HTEC approval alone”: Ethics approval is not current clinical readiness. “An available operating slot”: Scheduling is not clearance. Module 14.
Question 132A donor reports fever on admission. What next?
Best: New symptoms may change safety. Why not the others: “Ignore it if prior clearance exists”: Prior clearance does not cover new illness. “Offer an unsupervised antibiotic”: Treatment requires the authorized clinician. “Ask the donor not to mention it”: Concealment risks harm. Module 14.
Question 133What releases a documented hold?
Best: The release must be attributable and traceable. Why not the others: “The coordinator becoming tired of waiting”: Fatigue does not resolve risk. “An operating-room deadline”: A deadline cannot establish safety. “A family waiver alone”: A waiver does not replace required review. Module 14.
Question 134Why separate donor and recipient handoffs?
Best: Linked surgery does not merge the people. Why not the others: “To avoid communicating at all”: Relevant coordination remains essential. “Because the donor needs no assessment”: The donor needs dedicated assessment and care. “To duplicate every private finding”: Sharing must remain necessary and lawful. Module 14.
Question 135After postponement what needs review?
Best: A delay can create new clinical and administrative needs. Why not the others: “Only the surgery calendar”: Dialysis, medicine and support needs remain. “Nothing if the family agrees”: Agreement does not maintain care. “Automatic discharge without a plan”: Both people need an appropriate plan. Module 14.
Question 136Readiness for a living-donor transplant means:
Best: eight gates, two people, closed by their owners. A slot, a payment, or a signature closes only one part. Module 14.
Question 137A hold says “Repeat crossmatch within 7 days of surgery.” Surgery is in 3 days and the crossmatch was 9 days ago. You:
Best: the hold is open and the team decides. Releasing or postponing are not coordination decisions, and an old result does not satisfy a time-bound hold. Module 14.
Question 138Handoff for donor and recipient should be:
Best: separate handoffs protect privacy and accuracy. A combined update risks disclosure; families are not handoff channels. Module 14.
Question 139Which counts as evidence that the ethics gate is closed?
Best: the committee’s decision and closed conditions. Family agreement, schedules, and consent are different gates. Module 14.
Question 140A pre-surgery huddle should end with:
Best: the authorized lead decides and it is written down. Assumptions and unrecorded huddles cause errors. Module 14.
Question 141A prescription is written but medicine is unavailable. Is discharge navigation complete?
Best: Access is essential to treatment continuity. Why not the others: “Yes, the form is complete”: A form does not provide medicine. “Yes, if insurance is pending”: Pending coverage does not solve supply. “Yes, if the patient promises to try”: The barrier needs an actionable plan. Module 15.
Question 142Who resolves a formulation substitution?
Best: Formulations can differ and need clinical oversight. Why not the others: “The coordinator alone”: Coordination does not confer prescribing authority. “A neighbor with a spare pack”: Unverified medicines are unsafe. “An online price comparison alone”: Price is not clinical equivalence. Module 15.
Question 143What demonstrates teach-back?
Best: Recall of names alone does not establish usable understanding. Why not the others: “A signature without discussion”: A signature does not prove comprehension. “The coordinator reading louder”: Loudness does not confirm understanding. “A leaflet handed to a relative”: The person’s own needs still require assessment. Module 15.
Question 144Drug-level sampling instructions should come from where?
Best: Timing depends on the treatment and protocol. Why not the others: “A universal rule for all drugs”: The timing is not identical for all medicines. “The insurance form”: Payment forms do not define sampling. “The patient’s guess”: Clarify uncertain instructions. Module 15.
Question 145The donor has the recipient’s discharge sheet. What next?
Best: Wrong instructions can create both safety and privacy harm. Why not the others: “Ignore it because surgery was linked”: Care plans are person-specific. “Ask the donor to copy the recipient’s medicines”: The prescriptions are not interchangeable. “Delete all records of the mistake”: The correction and incident trail must remain. Module 15.
Question 146A recipient has 40 tablets left and takes 4 a day. The pharmacy needs 5 working days to restock. The runway is:
Best: 40 divided by 4 is 10 days of supply; with a 5-day lead time the refill must start now. 40 and 4 confuse tablets with days. Module 15.
Question 147In a meta-analysis by Dew and colleagues, immunosuppressant nonadherence in kidney recipients was about:
Best: 36 per 100 patient-years, the highest of the organs studied. Nonadherence is common, so plan support for everyone. Module 15.
Question 148Teach-back means:
Best: the person demonstrates the action. A yes, a reading, or name recall do not show they can act. Module 15.
Question 149A recipient will run out of tacrolimus during a holiday week because the pharmacy is closed. You:
Best: protect continuity in advance. Skipping, switching brands without the team, or waiting all risk rejection. Module 15.
Question 150A systematic review by Butler and colleagues associated immunosuppressant nonadherence with:
Best: odds of graft failure about seven times higher in observational studies. The design shows association, not proof of cause, but the signal is strong. Module 15.
Question 151Does fever after transplant prove rejection?
Best: A symptom is not a definitive diagnosis. Why not the others: “Yes, always”: Infection and other causes are possible. “No, so it can be ignored”: Uncertainty is a reason to assess. “Only if insurance covers biopsy”: Coverage does not determine diagnosis. Module 16.
Question 152Severe breathlessness in a donor should trigger what?
Best: Potential serious complications need prompt care. Why not the others: “A routine appointment next month”: Delay may be dangerous. “An insurance appeal first”: Administration must not delay emergency response. “A web quiz”: Education cannot assess an acute emergency. Module 16.
Question 153What information is immediately useful on a call?
Best: These enable safe contact and response. Why not the others: “Social-media password”: It is irrelevant and inappropriate. “Only the insurance number”: It does not establish current clinical need. “The recipient’s employer details first”: This delays relevant assessment. Module 16.
Question 154The first on-call clinician is unreachable. What next?
Best: A reliable backup route is essential. Why not the others: “Wait indefinitely”: The concern remains unaddressed. “Make the prescribing decision alone”: Scope limits still apply. “Tell the patient to stop calling”: This abandons the handoff. Module 16.
Question 155What completes an urgent handoff?
Best: Sending a message alone does not close the loop. Why not the others: “A voicemail only”: Receipt and action are uncertain. “A draft note”: It has not reached the service. “A message to an unrelated department”: The responsible team must receive it. Module 16.
Question 156A recipient two weeks after transplant calls with fever and less urine. A support-path coordinator should:
Best: urgent symptoms go to a clinician now, and the loop is closed. Advice and delays are outside scope and unsafe. Module 16.
Question 157Why does delay matter for a possible rejection?
Best: rejection is treatable, and timing matters. It does not reliably resolve without treatment. Module 16.
Question 158A recipient has vomiting and cannot keep tablets down. This matters because:
Best: inability to take immunosuppressants is urgent. The other answers misunderstand the risk. Module 16.
Question 159Closing the loop on an urgent call means:
Best: receipt, action, and the patient’s understanding, documented. A text or voicemail is dispatch, not closure. Module 16.
Question 160A donor one week after nephrectomy reports increasing abdominal pain and fever. You:
Best: the donor has their own team and needs urgent review. The recipient’s team is not the donor’s channel; waiting and reassurance are unsafe. Module 16.
Question 161When does donor follow-up end?
Best: Initial recovery is only one phase. Why not the others: “At recipient discharge”: The donor’s needs are separate. “When the first claim is paid”: Payment does not define follow-up duration. “After the donor moves”: A move requires an accepted continuity plan. Module 17.
Question 162A recipient returns to dialysis. What next?
Best: Medicines, symptoms and future options still need review. Why not the others: “Stop immunosuppression independently”: The clinical team determines treatment. “Delete the transplant record”: Past care remains relevant. “Assume no more psychological support is needed”: This transition can be distressing. Module 17.
Question 163What should precede a nonadherence label?
Best: Evidence and support are more useful than assumptions. Why not the others: “A single unanswered call”: This is insufficient to infer motive. “A travel delay”: Travel failure may be outside the person’s control. “A low income estimate”: Income does not establish willingness. Module 17.
Question 164Which source should define a current vaccine plan?
Best: Historical pandemic documents may be outdated. Why not the others: “A 2020 interim document alone”: Interim advice may no longer apply. “A generic social-media schedule”: It may not account for transplantation. “The donor’s preferences alone”: Preferences need informed clinical discussion. Module 17.
Question 165What confirms long-distance follow-up transfer?
Best: Responsibility must be explicit. Why not the others: “Giving the patient a phone number only”: Acceptance remains unconfirmed. “Closing the old file immediately”: This can create a gap. “Assuming any local clinic will know the regimen”: The plan needs a documented handoff. Module 17.
Question 166KDIGO’s living donor guideline and the DOH order both support:
Best: donor care continues for life. Short or conditional follow-up does not meet the framework. Module 17.
Question 167A donor misses two yearly check-ups. The respectful recall step is:
Best: respectful, private, practical. Pressure through the recipient breaches separation; closing the file abandons the duty. Module 17.
Question 168Which items are typically checked at a donor’s yearly visit?
Best: the donor framework centers on blood pressure, body mass, kidney function, and albuminuria. Donors take no immunosuppressants. Module 17.
Question 169A recipient’s graft is failing. Navigation should include:
Best: plan the transition early with the team. Waiting forces urgent dialysis; the donor’s information is separate. Module 17.
Question 170Which PhilHealth package includes living donor monitoring?
Best: the post-KT circulars list living donor monitoring among their services. Module 17.
Question 171A donor nods while a family member answers everything. What next?
Best: Nodding alone does not establish a free informed choice. Why not the others: “Treat the family’s answers as consent”: The donor’s own choice must be heard. “Speed up before the slot expires”: Time pressure increases risk. “Assume agreement because they are related”: Relatedness does not prove consent. Module 18.
Question 172Low literacy should lead to what?
Best: Communication support enables informed participation. Why not the others: “Automatic exclusion”: Literacy alone is not a blanket exclusion criterion. “A longer form without explanation”: More text may not help. “Consent by the coordinator”: The coordinator cannot consent for the person. Module 18.
Question 173Who performs specialist psychiatric assessment?
Best: The coordinator arranges the referral. Why not the others: “Any staff member with a checklist”: A checklist does not confer specialist competence. “The recipient”: This creates conflict and lacks qualifications. “A payer clerk”: Insurance work is not psychiatric assessment. Module 18.
Question 174A foreign insurance letter is presented. What next?
Best: One document does not satisfy every system. Why not the others: “Assume local transplant approval”: Jurisdiction and authorization still matter. “Guarantee full reimbursement”: Coverage is unverified. “Skip ethics review”: Ethics protections still apply. Module 18.
Question 175How should donor guilt be handled?
Best: Support should protect free choice. Why not the others: “Remind the donor they owe the recipient”: This is coercive. “Reveal private fears publicly”: This violates confidentiality. “Promise that surgery has no risk”: This is false reassurance. Module 18.
Question 176In a Canadian cohort, donors with higher expenses had:
Best: higher expenses were associated with worse mental health-related quality of life. The design cannot prove cause. Module 18.
Question 177A potential donor is an overseas Filipino worker who must return abroad in four weeks. You:
Best: honest planning, no pressure. Rushing undermines safety and choice, exclusion is unfair, and the recipient cannot decide for the donor. Module 18.
Question 178A recipient says, “I stopped one medicine because it is expensive.” Your best response is:
Best: safety and trust together. Scolding hides future problems; choosing medicines is not your role. Module 18.
Question 179A young woman asks about pregnancy after donating a kidney. You:
Best: this is a clinical counseling question for her team. Absolute statements either way are inaccurate. Module 18.
Question 180For a pediatric recipient, which PhilHealth circular covers post-transplant services?
Best: the children’s post-KT package is PC 2025-0011; the adult one is PC 2025-0012. Module 18.
Question 181Should donor psychiatric findings appear on a family dashboard?
Best: Shared milestones do not require private details. Why not the others: “Yes, because the family is involved”: Family involvement does not waive privacy. “Yes, if names are shortened”: Indirect identification may remain possible. “Yes, if the spreadsheet is convenient”: Convenience is not a lawful basis. Module 19.
Question 182What is needed for an interpretable follow-up rate?
Best: Without definitions rates can mislead. Why not the others: “A percentage alone”: The population and period are unclear. “Only the best-performing month”: Selective reporting distorts performance. “Excluding all unreachable donors silently”: This hides follow-up gaps. Module 19.
Question 183What should a misdirected record trigger?
Best: The responsible team assesses containment and notification. Why not the others: “Deletion of all evidence”: Evidence may be needed for investigation. “Posting the incident publicly with names”: This compounds the disclosure. “Ignoring it if accidental”: Accidental breaches can still cause harm. Module 19.
Question 184Which metric alone risks volume pressure?
Best: It needs safety, ethics and access balancing measures. Why not the others: “Donor understanding alongside safety review”: This helps assess quality. “Medicine-gap tracking”: This identifies continuity risks. “Barrier-resolution time with equity review”: This assesses navigation performance. Module 19.
Question 185Can the training website submit real national-registry records?
Best: The Academy is an educational site. Why not the others: “Yes, through a guessed endpoint”: Unverified integration is unsafe and unauthorized. “Yes, using shared passwords”: Credentials must remain controlled. “Yes, through quiz answers”: Training inputs must never become clinical submissions. Module 19.
Question 186Where should a donor’s private reason for withdrawing be recorded?
Best: record facts in a restricted record. Shared views and the recipient’s chart breach privacy; not recording loses accuracy. Module 19.
Question 187A quality measure reads “Donor follow-up completeness at 1 year.” Before counting, you must define:
Best: without a defined denominator and window, the rate means nothing. Targets and blame come after a valid measure. Module 19.
Question 188Of 20 donors due for a 1-year visit, 15 attended. Of 60 due in a larger program, 45 attended. Both are 75%. Which is more precise?
Best: same rate, more observations, narrower uncertainty. Report the interval with the rate. Module 19.
Question 189Equity review means:
Best: a fair average can hide unfair gaps. Compare groups and act on the differences. Module 19.
Question 190Under the Data Privacy Act, health information is treated as:
Best: health information is sensitive personal information with stricter processing rules. Module 19.
Question 191What is the capstone’s central standard?
Best: Timeliness must coexist with rights, safety and accurate approvals. Why not the others: “Completing surgery at any cost”: This can reward coercion or unsafe shortcuts. “Maximizing claims alone”: Financial completion is only one domain. “Avoiding all escalation”: Escalation is essential when authority or risk requires it. Module 20.
Question 192Does this course confer CCTC certification?
Best: Educational study is not a credential. Why not the others: “Yes, after all quizzes”: Quizzes cannot award an external credential. “Yes, after 60 study hours”: That is not the ABTC process. “Yes, after a hospital prints a badge”: A badge does not establish certification. Module 20.
Question 193Can US donor advocate rules be labeled Philippine law?
Best: Legal duties remain jurisdiction-specific. Why not the others: “Yes, because they protect donors”: A useful principle does not transfer jurisdiction. “Yes, if translated”: Translation does not enact law. “Yes, if a coordinator prefers them”: Preference does not override local requirements. Module 20.
Question 194A donor freely withdraws after support. Is navigation necessarily unsuccessful?
Best: Performance must not pressure donation. Why not the others: “Yes, because no transplant occurred”: Volume alone is an unsafe measure. “Yes, so the reason should be disclosed”: Confidentiality remains important. “Yes, so the donor should be billed as punishment”: Punitive pressure undermines free choice. Module 20.
Question 195What permits independent workplace responsibilities?
Best: The facility must assess actual practice and scope. Why not the others: “The website progress bar”: A progress bar measures study only. “A downloaded completion certificate”: This course does not issue a credential. “A high quiz score alone”: Knowledge is only one element of competence. Module 20.
Question 196In the United States, the independent living donor advocate (ILDA) is a useful idea for the Philippines because:
Best: borrow the principle, follow the Philippine definition. The US role is not local law, not automatic for coordinators, and not a replacement for the committee. Module 20.
Question 197Which foreign rule should not be applied in the Philippines without legal review?
Best: deadlines are jurisdiction-specific law. The other three are principles already consistent with Philippine safeguards and safety practice. Module 20.
Question 198In the capstone, which action is a critical failure?
Best: disclosure of the donor’s reason is a critical failure. The other three are correct actions. Module 20.
Question 199A recipient had a transfusion on Tuesday; an offer comes Thursday. The coordinator’s key step is to:
Best: a sensitizing event must reach the team and laboratory. Hiding, interpreting, or canceling are wrong. Module 20.
Question 200The UK Human Tissue Authority independent assessment is best used in this course as:
Best: it is a comparison. The Philippine safeguards are the DOH order and the hospital ethics committee. Module 20.
