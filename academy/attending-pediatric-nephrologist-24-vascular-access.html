Training track for attending pediatric nephrologists

This module is study material for attending pediatric nephrologists and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and it gives no doses. Doses, fill volumes and thresholds must come from current pediatric guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved pediatric protocols and a pediatric pharmacist. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Choose between a tunneled central venous catheter and an arteriovenous fistula for a child, using size, the expected time to transplant and a lifelong vein plan.
  • Troubleshoot catheter dysfunction and lead the response to suspected catheter-related bloodstream infection, including when the catheter must come out.
  • Write an access plan that protects veins from birth and that an adult hemodialysis unit can follow without you in the room.

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · the access plan for a whole childhoodA child who starts hemodialysis (HD) at 8 may need kidney replacement therapy for sixty years. The attending pediatric nephrologist owns the vascular access plan: which veins are protected, which access is placed and when, who is called when it fails, and how the plan follows the child to an adult HD unit 60 kilometers away. The surgeon creates the access, the interventionist rescues it, and the nurse uses it three times a week. You decide what the next access will be before the present one fails.
In plain wordsThink of a child’s veins as the few roads into a small town. Every needle, every intravenous line and every catheter is roadwork. Most roadwork is repaired. Some leaves a road closed for good, and the town cannot build new ones.

The picture has a limit. A closed road is visible; a closed vein often is not. A peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) or a temporary jugular line can narrow a central vein without any sign for years, until a fistula in that arm swells, or a new catheter will not pass. Veins also grow with the child, so a vein too small for a fistula at 15 kilograms may be ideal at 35. The plan has to look forward in time, not only at today’s anatomy.

Most children on HD start with a tunneled central venous catheter (CVC), because they are small, because they started urgently, or because a transplant is expected soon. A few will dialyze long enough, and grow enough, that a fistula would serve them better. Your work is to protect every vein that a future fistula or catheter will need, to keep the catheter working and uninfected while it is the lifeline, and to recognize the night when a fever on dialysis is sepsis from the line.

Lesson · part 1

Why a child’s veins are the scarce resource

An HD access must deliver blood to the circuit fast enough for the dialyzer to work, then take it back. Flow through a tube rises steeply with its radius: in laminar flow, halving the radius cuts flow to about a sixteenth at the same pressure. That physics is the whole problem of pediatric access. A small child’s veins and arteries are narrow, so the catheter that fits is narrow, and the fistula that is made may never carry enough flow to mature.

A catheter does damage while it works. Its tip and body rub the vein wall with every heartbeat and breath, and the endothelium responds with inflammation, smooth-muscle growth and a fibrin sheath. Over months this narrows the central vein (central venous stenosis), silently, until a fistula in that arm swells or a future catheter will not pass.

A fistula works differently. Joining an artery to a vein exposes the vein to arterial flow and pressure. Over weeks the vein dilates and its wall thickens, a process called maturation. It needs a vein of adequate diameter, an artery that can deliver flow, and an undamaged path back to the heart. Every venipuncture in the forearm and every PICC in the arm can remove one of those conditions.

On the ward, the PICC request · fictional

A pediatric resident messages you at 9 p.m. Elijah, 5, has chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 4 from renal dysplasia and needs two weeks of intravenous antibiotics for osteomyelitis. The resident has booked a PICC in the left arm for the morning.

You ask for a small-bore tunneled central line in the internal jugular vein instead, placed by the team that would one day place his dialysis catheter, and you write “No PICC, no blood draws above the wrist in either arm: future fistula” on the order sheet and the front of the chart.

What this shows. Vein preservation is decided by whoever writes the next order. Put the rule where that person will read it.

RememberEvery vein in a child with CKD belongs to the future fistula or the future catheter. Spend it only when nothing else will do.
Lesson · part 2

What the evidence shows about choosing an access

What adults taught us, and where it stops. For adults, “fistula first” changed practice. The Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) 2019 update moved the frame to an end-stage kidney disease “Life-Plan”: the right access for the patient, chosen with a view of every access that patient will need.1 Children fit that frame better than adults do, because their life-plan is longer and more often ends in a transplant.

The pediatric consensus. The European Society for Pediatric Nephrology (ESPN) Dialysis Working Group published its recommendations in 2019, graded with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system. Where evidence was thin, the group marked its expert opinion as such.2 Its statements most relevant to an attending are these:

The consensus calls central venous lines “CVLs”; this module uses CVC for the same device. It does not set a weight cutoff as a recommendation. It notes that earlier guidance suggested considering a fistula in children over 20 kilograms expected to wait more than a year for a transplant, and that fistulas have been created in children under 10 kilograms with microvascular technique.2 Read that as a signal about surgical skill, not a rule about weight.

The registry. The International Pediatric Hemodialysis Network (IPHN) followed 552 children from 27 countries prospectively between 2012 and 2017. Over 314 patient-years, 628 catheters, 225 fistulas and 17 grafts were placed. Infections were reported only with catheters, at 1.3 per 1,000 catheter-days, and 47% of those infections required the access to be replaced. Dysfunction ran at 2.5 per 1,000 catheter-days against 1.2 per 1,000 fistula-days. Catheters needed 82% more revisions and almost three times as many replacements at a new site. Fistulas delivered higher blood flow and higher urea clearance (Kt/V, a surrogate for dialysis dose). One-third of children with a fistula still needed a temporary catheter while it matured.3

The registry also shows why the comparison is not fair. Catheter use was associated with younger age and with the expectation of an early living related transplant, with a median time to transplant of 14 months against 20 months with fistulas, and a threefold higher rate of living related transplantation.3 The design was observational. It shows that catheters carry more infection and dysfunction; it does not show that placing a fistula in a given child would have improved survival or growth, because the children who got fistulas were different from the start.

The Philippine reality. Few surgeons in the country create fistulas in small children, and a transplant date is rarely certain, because donor work-up, cross-matching and costs move at the family’s pace. Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) covers HD sessions under PhilHealth Circular (PC) 2024-0023; that circular pays for sessions, not for a particular access, and it does not tell you which access a child should have.4 If the transplant is likely within months, a well-cared-for catheter is reasonable. If the work-up has stalled, the plan should change.

RememberChoose the access for the time the child will really spend on HD, not the time the family hopes for. Re-ask the question every three months.
Lesson · part 3

Keeping the catheter working

Where it goes. Most pediatric access teams prefer the right internal jugular vein for a tunneled catheter, because the path to the right atrium is short and straight. The subclavian route is avoided: stenosis there threatens every future access in that arm. Femoral catheters are a fallback when the neck veins are unusable, and they carry the problem of a diaper and a moving hip. The tip should sit where blood flows freely, usually near the junction of the superior vena cava and the right atrium; a tip that is too short draws against the vein wall, especially as the child grows taller.

Why it stops working. Early dysfunction, in the first days, usually means the catheter is kinked, malpositioned or too short. Late dysfunction usually means thrombus inside the lumen or a fibrin sheath around the tip. Growth matters in children: a tip that sat well at 9 may have pulled back into the superior vena cava by 11. The ESPN group suggests tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) both as a locking solution to prevent catheter thrombosis (2B) and as the thrombolytic for catheter thrombosis (2D); it notes that fibrin-sheath disruption and catheter exchange are rarely used in children and lack pediatric studies.2 Which lock your unit uses, and how much, comes from the unit’s protocol and pharmacy, not from this module.

Care that prevents trouble. The Standardizing Care to Improve Outcomes in Pediatric End Stage Renal Disease (SCOPE) collaborative introduced standardized catheter care bundles across North American pediatric dialysis centers. In 325 children with tunneled catheters at 15 centers, bundle compliance reached 87.6%, and the adjusted catheter-associated bloodstream infection (CA-BSI) rate fell from 3.3 per 100 patient-months before the bundles to 0.8 per 100 patient-months 48 months later.5 That is a before-and-after quality improvement design without a control group. Secular trends and attention may explain part of the fall, but the direction is consistent with what a careful connection and dressing technique should do.

RememberA catheter that fails early is in the wrong place. A catheter that fails late is clotting or sheathed. Find which before anyone changes the prescription.
Lesson · part 4

Fever on dialysis: catheter-related bloodstream infection

How organisms get in. In the first weeks, organisms from the skin track along the outside of the catheter through the tunnel. Later, most enter through the hub, travel down the lumen and settle in a biofilm on the catheter’s inner wall. When the circuit is connected and the pump starts, that biofilm sheds bacteria and endotoxin into the blood. That is why rigors that begin minutes after connection point to the catheter until proven otherwise.

The guideline. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) 2009 guideline grades its statements by strength (A to C) and quality of evidence (I to III). For patients on HD through a catheter, it recommends drawing peripheral blood cultures from vessels that will not be needed for a future fistula, such as hand veins (A-III); cultures drawn from the bloodlines connected to the catheter during dialysis are acceptable when no peripheral sample can be obtained (B-II). Empirical therapy should include vancomycin and gram-negative coverage chosen from the local antibiogram (A-II).6 Doses and drug choices come from your unit’s pediatric formulary and a pharmacist, weighed for the child’s size and for dialysis clearance.

When the catheter must come out. IDSA recommends always removing the infected catheter when HD catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI) is caused by Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas species or Candida species, and inserting a temporary catheter at another site (A-II); if no other site exists, exchange over a guidewire (B-II). For other organisms, therapy can start without immediate removal. If fever, chills, unstable blood pressure or altered mental status resolve within 2 to 3 days and there is no metastatic infection, the catheter can be exchanged over a guidewire or retained with an antibiotic lock after each session for 10 to 14 days (B-II). Persistent symptoms or metastatic infection mean removal (B-II).6

The guideline is frank about children. Small blood volumes reduce the yield of cultures, peripheral samples are hard to obtain, and losing access is a real harm, so treatment without removal is often attempted; such children should be watched closely and the device removed if they deteriorate or the infection recurs. Fungal infection treated without removal has a low success rate.6 The IDSA grades rest largely on adult data and on expert opinion, which is what the “II” and “III” mean.

What the SCOPE cohort adds. In 1,277 children from 35 pediatric dialysis centers, 79.7% dialyzed through a catheter. Among them, mupirocin at the exit site was associated with a higher CA-BSI rate (rate ratio 4.45), and centers that improved their compliance with care practices saw CA-BSI fall from 2.71 to 0.71 per 100 patient-months.7 The mupirocin signal is an observational association and may reflect which children received it; it is a reason to look at your own unit’s practice, not proof of harm.

Lesson · part 5

Fistulas in small arms, needles, and the written access plan

Making the fistula work. A fistula in a child is a team project. Before surgery, the ESPN group suggests a structured history, examination and duplex ultrasound (2C), and imaging of the central veins when stenosis is suspected (2D). It suggests placing the fistula in the non-dominant arm where possible (ungraded) and distally in the arm (2D). Once the fistula has matured adequately, cannulation should use the rope-ladder or buttonhole technique (2C), with aseptic technique (ungraded).2

Watching it. The ESPN group suggests routine structured physical examination of every fistula by nurses and physicians (2D), and urgent referral to the access surgeon when dysfunction is found (ungraded).2 A healthy fistula has a soft, continuous thrill and a vein that collapses when the arm is raised. Steal, where the fistula diverts so much blood that the hand becomes cold, pale or painful, is possible but rarely seen in children.2

Needles and fear. For a 10-year-old, two large needles three times a week can become the worst part of kidney failure. The ESPN text recommends local anesthetic creams to ease fistula cannulation, whatever technique is used.2 Add the simple things: the same experienced cannulator where possible, a child life or play approach that lets the child rehearse, and honesty about what will hurt. A child who dreads the needles will skip sessions as an adolescent.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The venipuncture in the antecubital fossa

What you see
A ward nurse draws daily blood from the left antecubital vein of a 9-year-old with CKD stage 5 who is about to start HD.
What is likely going on
No one told the ward which veins belong to the future fistula.
What to do
Ask for draws from the hand or the dialysis catheter per protocol, write the vein rule on the chart cover and the bedside sign, and tell the charge nurse why.
Why it matters
Each draw in the forearm or elbow can scar the vein a surgeon will need in two years.

The catheter that only works lying down

What you see
An adult unit reports that an 11-year-old’s catheter gives good flow only when he lies flat with his head turned left; he has grown 8 centimeters this year.
What is likely going on
The tip has pulled back as he grew and now draws against the vein wall.
What to do
Ask for a chest radiograph and refer to the access team for exchange before the catheter fails completely.
Why it matters
Growth moves the tip. A catheter that was perfect at insertion can become the wrong length.

The culture that grew yeast

What you see
Two days after a fever on dialysis, the blood culture from a 7-year-old’s catheter grows Candida. The fever has settled.
What is likely going on
Fungal catheter infection rarely clears with the catheter in place, even when the child looks better.
What to do
Arrange removal and a temporary catheter at another site, start antifungal therapy per protocol with the pediatric pharmacist, and look for metastatic infection.
Why it matters
Feeling better is not the test for fungal catheter infection; the organism decides.

The fistula nobody listens to

What you see
A 15-year-old’s fistula bleeds for 25 minutes after needles at three consecutive sessions; the unit has been adding pressure dressings.
What is likely going on
Prolonged bleeding suggests high venous pressure from an outflow stenosis.
What to do
Examine the fistula yourself, request duplex ultrasound and refer to the access surgeon this week.
Why it matters
A fistula that bleeds long is often a fistula about to clot.
Field notes
  • Put “Future fistula: no PICC, no draws above the wrist” on the chart cover of every child with CKD stage 4 or 5.
  • Keep pediatric and adult blood culture bottles in the unit, and a written note that adult bottles are acceptable when pediatric ones run out.
  • Ask the adult unit for the external catheter length at every monthly review; a change of more than a centimeter needs a look.
  • Give every access plan a review date, and put that date in your own calendar, not only in the chart.
Case review

Back to the case: Rigors at minute twenty

The next 30 minutes. Rigors and fever 20 minutes into a session through a catheter are catheter-related bloodstream infection until proven otherwise, and Carlo’s falling blood pressure and rising pulse say he is already septic. Paracetamol and Friday cultures is the wrong plan. Ask the nurse to stop fluid removal and the POD to draw blood cultures now, from the catheter or bloodlines and, if possible, from a hand vein, using adult bottles if pediatric ones are not stocked. The first dose of empirical antibiotics covering gram-positive and gram-negative organisms should be given within the hour, chosen from the unit’s pediatric protocol with a pharmacist. Stop the session; return the blood only if the circuit is intact. With a heart rate of 138 and a pressure below his baseline, he needs a pediatric bed today: call the pediatric center, speak to the accepting physician, and send a written endorsement with the vital-sign trend, the cultures drawn and the time of the first dose.

The catheter. Not necessarily, and not at the chair. IDSA recommends removal when the culture grows Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas or Candida, when symptoms persist beyond 2 to 3 days, when there is metastatic infection, or when the tunnel is infected. Otherwise the catheter can be treated in place with systemic antibiotics and an antibiotic lock, or exchanged over a guidewire, which matters for a child whose left arm has already lost a vein. The decision belongs to the attending with the access team, at the receiving hospital, once cultures are back and the child’s course is clear. Explain this to Aling Marites in plain words: pulling the line in the unit would leave Carlo with no way to dialyze and would not treat the infection faster.

Prevention and the plan. Carlo’s infection most likely started at the hub, so review how the adult unit connects and dresses his catheter, against a written bundle like the ones the SCOPE centers used. His left-arm PICC should have been avoided; write the vein rules on the front of his chart. Then face the real issue: a transplant that was expected within months has stalled on cost. Ask the transplant coordinator and the social worker to move the donor work-up, and set a date. If there is still no transplant date by then, order vein mapping and central venous imaging and refer him for a fistula in the right arm, because a catheter that has become a long-term access is now the riskier choice.

Question 1 of 5A 13-year-old dialyzing through a tunneled catheter develops rigors 15 minutes after connection. What is the most appropriate first step?
Rigors soon after connection suggest catheter-related bloodstream infection. Cultures first, then empirical therapy covering gram-positive and gram-negative organisms (IDSA A-II), chosen from the unit’s pediatric protocol.
Question 2 of 5Which culture result makes IDSA 2009 recommend removing an infected hemodialysis catheter?
IDSA recommends removal for S. aureus, Pseudomonas and Candida (A-II). Other organisms can be treated with the catheter in place if the child responds within 2 to 3 days.
Question 3 of 5What did the IPHN registry show about catheters and fistulas in children?
Catheter infections ran at 1.3 per 1,000 catheter-days; dysfunction 2.5 vs 1.2 per 1,000 days. The study was observational; catheter use was associated with age and with an expected early transplant.
Question 4 of 5A catheter’s arterial pressure alarm sounds at the start of each session and improves when the child turns the head. What is the most likely cause?
Positional, early-session inflow problems point to tip position. Growth can pull a tip back into the superior vena cava; a chest radiograph and access review follow if it recurs.
Question 5 of 5Which ESPN 2019 statement applies to a child with a central venous stenosis on the left?
A fistula drains through the central veins on its side. The ESPN group advises avoiding an AVF on the side of a central venous stenosis (ungraded) and imaging central veins when stenosis is suspected (2D).
