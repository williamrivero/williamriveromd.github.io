Study series for physicians on duty

This module is study material for physicians on duty in hemodialysis units. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol. At the chairside, follow your unit’s protocol and ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Doses and cut-off values are set by your unit. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Explain, with arithmetic, why a dialysis patient meets hundreds of liters of water each week, and why contaminants that are harmless to drink can harm that patient.
  • Describe the water treatment steps in outline, and match each major contaminant to its source and to the illness it causes.
  • Explain how dialysis fluid is mixed from water and two concentrates, what conductivity measures, and what goes wrong when the mix is wrong.
  • Recognize a cluster event and carry out the first steps in the order your unit’s protocol sets.
  • Read the water and dialysis fluid records a physician should understand, and name the standards they follow.

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsThink of the water you drink as a visitor who must pass three guarded gates before reaching the blood. The gut lining is the first gate. The liver is the second. The kidneys, which throw out whatever slips through, are the third. Dialysis fluid takes a back door. It skips all three gates and meets the blood across one thin membrane.

The picture has a limit. The membrane is not a gate that lets nothing through. It is built to let small molecules cross in both directions, because that is how dialysis cleans the blood. Anything small enough in the fluid, such as fluoride, aluminum, or fragments of bacteria, can cross into the blood too. The patient has no working kidneys to clear it afterward.

Figure 1 · Key terms for this module

Most shifts pass without any problem from the water or the dialysis fluid. A few bring one, and it looks like the nausea, headache, chills, or cramps you see every day. Your work is to notice when the same complaint appears in more than one chair, to stop the harm per protocol, and to ask the right questions of the right people.

Opening case · fictional

Case: Everyone on the afternoon shift is nauseated

It is 2:50 p.m. on a hot Tuesday in a 14-station unit in Marilao, Bulacan. The afternoon shift started forty minutes ago. Nurse Carmela Reyes calls you to the floor.

Mang Teodoro Santiago, 61, is nauseated and has a headache. So is Aling Pacita Navarro, 55, two stations away. A third patient, Jun-jun Ramos, 38, says his legs are cramping and he feels “hot inside.” Their blood pressures are 150/90, 146/88, and 162/96 mm Hg. These are illustrative values, not targets. No patient on the morning shift complained.

Two facts come up as you talk. The technician replaced the bicarbonate concentrate supply at the start of the afternoon shift. The water system was serviced yesterday. Nurse Reyes has stopped fluid removal on the first patient and is checking the machines.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What makes this a cluster rather than three unrelated complaints?
  • Which causes in the water, the concentrate, the machines, or the disposables fit these symptoms, and what would you ask to see?
  • What are your first steps in the next 10 minutes, and whom do you call?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

The hidden half: how much water meets the blood

Module 5 followed the blood through the circuit. This module follows the water and the concentrates that become dialysis fluid. You do not run the water system, but when it fails, you may be the first doctor to see the result.

Three gates, and the one that remains

In a healthy person, the gut absorbs only part of most metals in drinking water, the liver clears much of what is absorbed, and the kidneys excrete the rest. Each gate lowers the dose that reaches the tissues.

In a dialysis patient, the dialysis fluid bypasses the gut and the liver entirely. Only the membrane separates it from the blood. Small ions such as fluoride move across by diffusion toward the side with less of them. Aluminum crosses too, and once in the blood it binds to plasma proteins, so the free level stays low and more keeps moving in. The kidneys that would have excreted them are gone. What crosses tends to stay and build up.1

Bacteria are too large to cross an intact membrane, but small pieces of endotoxin can. High-flux membranes also let some fluid move from the dialysis fluid into the blood, a process called backfiltration.1 Purity matters even when the bacteria stay on the fluid side.

The volume is the second reason

The gate is thinner, and the volume is far larger. The arithmetic below shows the scale.

Worked scenario 1. Weekly water exposure (illustrative arithmetic, not a prescription).
Formula: dialysis fluid used per session (liters) = dialysis fluid flow (mL per minute) × session time (minutes) ÷ 1,000.
Illustrative values: 500 mL per minute × 240 minutes = 120,000 mL = 120 L per session.
Three sessions a week: 120 L × 3 = 360 L per week.
Comparison: a person who drinks 2 L a day drinks 2 × 7 = 14 L a week. 360 ÷ 14 ≈ 26 times more water meets the blood by dialysis fluid than a person drinks, and the drinking water passes the gut and the liver first.
What the number tells you: a concentration trivial in drinking water can deliver a meaningful dose across the membrane. What it does not tell you: how much of any one substance crosses, which depends on its size, its binding, and the gradient.

Flow and session length belong to the prescription (Module 7).

What the record of harm shows

A review from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) counted at least 592 cases and 16 deaths in the United States since the 1960s from chemical, bacterial, and endotoxin contamination, in outbreaks that still recur.2 These are reported outbreaks only, so the true number is likely higher.

RememberThe membrane is the only gate. Whatever is in the water is one membrane away from the blood.
Lesson · part 2

From tap to dialyzer: the water treatment train

Picture a line of guards at a series of gates, each catching a different kind of intruder, so that one tired guard does not let everything through. The picture has a limit: a guard who has caught too many intruders stops catching any, without warning. Carbon beds and softeners become exhausted in exactly this way, which is why they are tested and not trusted.

The technician track teaches how to run each step. You need to know what each one removes, so that a symptom in a patient points you to a step in the water room.2,1

Figure 4 · The steps and what each removes

Sediment filtersSand, rust, and particles.
SoftenerCalcium and magnesium, which would scale the reverse osmosis membrane.
Carbon bedsChlorine and chloramine. This step protects red blood cells.
Reverse osmosis (RO)The main barrier against dissolved salts, metals such as aluminum and fluoride, bacteria, and endotoxin.
Distribution loopPipes that carry product water to every station and back, kept moving so bacteria cannot settle.
Point-of-use ultrafiltersIn some units, a final filter at each station against bacteria and endotoxin.

Why the carbon step gets daily attention

Reverse osmosis has one important gap. Chloramine passes through it and through deionization, so it must be removed earlier, by activated carbon. A review of chloramine removal advised two carbon beds in series, so that breakthrough after the first bed is detected before the second one fails. It also advised testing more than once daily, because the chloramine level in city water can change without notice.3

This is why a total chlorine test is done before each shift. Section 6 shows where it is recorded.

The standards behind the system

The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) publishes the ISO 23500 series on fluids for dialysis. Part 1, in its second edition of 2024, sets the general requirements and replaced the 2019 edition.4 Part 3 sets the minimum chemical and microbiological quality of water used to make dialysis fluid and concentrates, and gives guidance on monitoring.5 Part 5 sets the minimum chemical and microbiological quality of the dialysis fluid itself.6 These are standards, not graded clinical recommendations. ISO has marked Parts 3 and 5 for revision, so check which edition your unit’s policy cites. Earlier European guidance on dialysis fluid purity preceded them.7

This module does not print chemical or microbial limits. The limits that apply are those in your unit’s water-quality policy, taken from the edition the unit and its regulator have adopted. Ask to see it in your first week.

Source water changes the design. City water usually carries chlorine or chloramine. Deep-well water may carry more iron and hardness, and no disinfectant. The site’s self-assessment, carbon tank contact-time, and capacity calculators show the checks a technician works through.

What the physician on duty does

Before your first shift ends, know where the water room is, who the responsible technician is and how to reach them, and where today’s chlorine check is written. You will rarely need them, and then within minutes.

RememberEach step catches one kind of contaminant. When a patient shows the calling card of that contaminant, ask about that step.
Lesson · part 3

What goes wrong: contaminants and the illnesses they cause

Think of the contaminants as a rogues’ gallery. Each one leaves a calling card that you can recognize at the chair. The picture has a limit. Calling cards overlap, and the same card can come from a cause outside the water. Use the gallery to choose your first question, not to make a diagnosis.

The evidence here comes from outbreak investigations. They are observational, from other countries and decades, and rarely give a precise dose. The mechanisms they point to are established.

Chloramine: the red cell breaker

Chloramine is an oxidant. In the blood it damages hemoglobin and the red cell membrane, and the damaged cells break, which is hemolysis.1 In one outbreak, the carbon filter in a recently modified water system failed. Forty-one patients needed transfusion for hemolytic anemia. The unit’s death rate rose in the following months, although no death could be attributed to the exposure.8 Copper released from pipes by acidic water can break red cells too. Nitrates in well water can turn hemoglobin into methemoglobin, a form that cannot carry oxygen.2,9

Aluminum: the brain

At autopsy, dialysis patients who had died of an unexplained neurologic syndrome had brain gray-matter aluminum of 25 parts per million. Dialysis patients who died of other causes had 6.5, and controls 2.2.10 These patients had also taken aluminum-containing phosphate binders, so the study does not say how much came from the water. This small tissue comparison suggested, without proving, that the syndrome was aluminum intoxication. A later outbreak supports the link. In Curaçao, distilled seawater ran through a new pipe lined with cement mortar, and aluminum leached into the dialysis water. Of 27 patients with similar exposure, 10 died of acute aluminum encephalopathy. Those who died were older, lighter, and had lower serum albumin.11

Fluoride: the itch and the heart

A temporary deionization system in one room of a university unit took up fluoride and then released it in a burst. Over five shifts, 12 of 15 patients in that room became acutely ill, with severe itching, many nonspecific symptoms, or fatal ventricular fibrillation in 3. None of 17 patients in the adjacent room became ill.12

Cyanobacterial toxins: the liver

At a center in Caruaru, Brazil, in 1996, water from a reservoir reached the unit without treatment, filtering, or chlorination. Toxins made by cyanobacteria, called microcystins, were found in the water and in patients’ blood and liver. Of 130 patients, 116 had visual disturbance, nausea, and vomiting; 101 met the case definition of acute liver failure, and 50 died.13

Bacteria and endotoxin: fever and slow inflammation

Bacteria grow in water loops, in stagnant branches, and in bicarbonate concentrate. Their fragments cause a pyrogenic reaction: chills, fever, and sometimes low blood pressure, often within the first hour of the session.1,9 Module 17 covers fever and bloodstream infection. Low-grade contamination may add to chronic inflammation without ever causing fever.

The standard endotoxin test, the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) test, detects intact endotoxin. In 269 samples tested with a cell assay that also responds to smaller bacterial fragments, 10.3% of samples meeting the European definition of pure fluid, and 9.1% of those meeting the ultrapure definition, still provoked an inflammatory response.14 This laboratory study shows that the standard test misses some inflammatory material. It does not show that this material harms patients.

A cluster is not always the water

In May 1998, 30 patients in three states of the United States developed hemolysis during dialysis. Twenty had high blood pressure and 18 had abdominal pain. Ten needed intensive care, and two died. The only common factor was one lot of blood tubing with a narrowed opening that damaged red cells as they were pumped through it.15 The water was sound. A cluster investigation checks the lot numbers of the disposables as well as the water (Module 16).

RememberMatch the calling card to the suspect: hemolysis to chloramine or the tubing, confusion to aluminum, itching and arrhythmia to fluoride, early chills to bacteria, liver failure to toxins.
Lesson · part 4

Mixing dialysis fluid: water, two concentrates, and conductivity

Making dialysis fluid is like making juice from concentrate: the machine adds exact amounts of two concentrates to treated water, minute by minute. Conductivity is the machine’s taste test for total dissolved salt. The picture has a limit. A taste test says the juice is too strong or too weak, not which ingredient is wrong, and it cannot taste a contaminant that carries no charge.

The two concentrates

The acid concentrate carries sodium chloride, potassium, calcium, magnesium, a small amount of acid, and in many formulas glucose. The bicarbonate concentrate carries sodium bicarbonate. The two are kept apart until the last moment, because calcium and magnesium would otherwise combine with bicarbonate and fall out of solution as scale.1 The machine draws each through its own pump in a fixed ratio and mixes them with product water. This is called proportioning.

Bicarbonate concentrate supports bacterial growth. A jug left open or mixed long before use can carry bacteria and endotoxin into every session it feeds.1 Your unit’s protocol states how long a mixed bicarbonate container may be used.

What conductivity measures, and what it misses

Conductivity rises with the concentration of charged particles. Sodium and its partner anions supply most of it, so conductivity is a close stand-in for the sodium in the fluid.1 If a pump fails, a line kinks, a jug runs empty, or the wrong jug is connected, conductivity moves. Out of range, the machine alarms and sends the fluid to drain, the bypass Module 5 described. A temperature sensor works the same way.

Two gaps remain. Conductivity cannot tell sodium from potassium, so a wrong acid concentrate with similar total salt may pass. It also misses bacteria, endotoxin, and uncharged toxins, which depend on the water system and testing.9

What the blood does when the fluid is wrong

Plasma equilibrates with dialysis fluid within minutes, so a wrong fluid changes the patient quickly. The chain below is established physiology. Alarm thresholds belong to the machine manual and the unit’s protocol.

Purity and long-term inflammation: what the trials show

Composition is part of the prescription (Module 7). Purity is a separate question. Ultrapure dialysis fluid is made to stricter bacterial and endotoxin limits, often with an ultrafilter at the station.

Susantitaphong and colleagues pooled 23 study arms with 2,221 patients, most of them not randomized. Ultrapure fluid lowered C-reactive protein (CRP) by 3.2 mg/L, raised serum albumin by 0.11 g/dL and hemoglobin by 0.40 g/dL, and lowered the weekly erythropoietin dose by 273 units. The results held when the analysis was limited to controlled trials. The authors called these surrogate markers and asked for a trial with hard outcomes.16

Asci and colleagues then randomized 704 patients in a two-by-two factorial trial of high-flux versus low-flux membranes and ultrapure versus standard fluid. The trial found no difference with ultrapure fluid in fatal and nonfatal cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.90, 95% confidence interval 0.61 to 1.32). A lower risk after more than 3 years on dialysis came from a post hoc analysis, which can only generate a hypothesis.17

Surrogates improve; in the one trial, hard outcomes did not. Whether to use ultrapure fluid is a decision for the unit and its Medical Director.

What the physician on duty does

A conductivity or temperature alarm with the patient connected is a stop-and-check event (Module 5). Sudden headache, chest tightness, cramps, or hemolysis early in the session prompts a check of the concentrate and the machine, not only the patient. Ask which jug is connected, when it was changed, and what the machine’s conductivity reads.

Stop and callStop and call when a conductivity or temperature alarm will not clear with the patient connected, or when any patient shows signs of hemolysis. Follow your unit’s protocol on whether the blood is returned, as Module 16 teaches; suspected hemolysis means it is not. Call the attending nephrologist at once.
Lesson · part 5

When several patients are sick from the same cause

A smoke alarm going off in one room may mean burnt toast. Alarms going off in three rooms at once point to the building. The picture has a limit. Three patients can fall ill on one afternoon for three reasons. A cluster is a hypothesis to test quickly, not a conclusion.

What counts as a cluster

A cluster is two or more patients with the same new symptom, linked by something they share. The link may be the shift, the room, the water loop, a group of machines, a batch of concentrate, or a lot of disposables. The symptom need not be dramatic. Nausea, itching, or chills in more than one chair within an hour is enough to ask.

Module 16 gives the circuit side of the cluster-event card. This section adds the water and the fluid, and the reasoning that finds the cause.

Comparison finds the cause

Every outbreak in Section 3 was solved by comparison. In the fluoride outbreak, illness was confined to one room served by one deionization unit: 12 of 15 patients in that room and none of 17 next door.12 In Caruaru, all 17 patients on the Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday night schedule became ill.13 In the tubing outbreak, the link was one manufacturing lot.15 The comparison, not the count, points to the cause.

Worked scenario 2. Attack rate: who is sick and who is not.
Formula: attack rate = patients who became ill ÷ patients who were exposed.
Published example: fluoride outbreak. Room A: 12 ÷ 15 = 80%. Adjacent room: 0 ÷ 17 = 0%. The difference pointed to the one thing room A had and the other room did not: its own deionizer.
Illustrative unit example: afternoon shift 5 ÷ 12 = 42%. Morning shift 0 ÷ 14 = 0%. The difference points to something that changed between shifts, such as a concentrate batch, a group of machines, a lot of disposables, or a water event.
What the number tells you: where to look first. What it does not tell you: the cause. With small numbers, chance differences are easy, so testing must confirm the comparison.

Split the patients by every exposure you can name, and hand that table to the Medical Director. The published figures above are from the fluoride report.12

Lesson · part 6

Reading the water log and asking the right questions

The water log is the daily vital-sign sheet for the unit’s largest patient. The picture has a limit. A water log describes samples taken at certain points and times, so a clean log can miss a problem that started an hour after the last test.

What the log contains

The ISO standards set the quality requirements and give guidance on monitoring. Each unit writes them into its own policy.5,6 The CDC review covers the monitoring methods.2

Expect pre-shift total chlorine results, daily reverse osmosis readings such as product water conductivity and salt rejection, bacterial culture and endotoxin results from set sample points, periodic chemical analyses, disinfection dates, and the corrective action taken whenever a result reached a set level.

Two terms matter on a microbiology report. A maximum level is the limit at which the system should be taken out of use until it is corrected and re-tested. An action level is set below it, as an early warning. Reaching it calls for corrective steps such as disinfection and a re-test.2 The same review advises testing total chlorine before each patient shift, after the first carbon tank.2 The numbers come from your unit’s policy.

The regulatory limit for the bacterial count in dialysis water in the Philippines is set by Department of Health (DOH) Administrative Order 2013-0003: less than 200 colony-forming units (CFU) per milliliter.18 Accredited water testing laboratories report results against this DOH value, so it is the number on the report you will read. The standards of the Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) give a stricter target of less than 100 CFU/mL.19 A unit may adopt the stricter target in its water policy, approved by the Medical Director; a result that meets it also meets the DOH limit.

Reading a log line by line

Worked scenario 3. A model water log (illustrative entries; the unit’s ISO-based levels apply).
Line 1. Today, 5:40 a.m.: total chlorine after the first carbon bed recorded as within the unit’s limit, initialed. Reading: the test was done and passed. No question needed.
Line 2. Last month: bacterial culture from the loop return marked “action level reached”; loop disinfected two days later; re-sample within limit. Reading: the system worked as designed. Confirm that the re-test result is on file.
Line 3. Reverse osmosis salt rejection: 98%, 97%, 96%, 95% over two weeks. Reading: a falling trend. Ask the technician today what is planned, and tell the Medical Director.
Line 4. Yesterday: “system serviced” with no detail of what was changed and no post-service chlorine test. Reading: a documentation gap. Ask what was serviced and whether a test was done before patients started.
What the walk-through tells you: which entries need a question and to whom. What it does not tell you: whether the water is safe now. Only a current test answers that.

You are not auditing the technician. You are confirming that today’s protective checks were done, and noticing trends the Medical Director should know.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The chlorine test that was not done

What you see
It is 6:10 a.m. in a unit in Quezon City. The first four patients are cannulated and waiting. The water log has no total chlorine entry for today. The technician on the night roster called in sick.
What is likely going on
No one knows whether the carbon beds are still removing chloramine. The test exists for exactly this moment.
What to do
Ask the charge nurse to hold the start of treatment per the unit’s water policy until a test is done by a trained staff member. Inform the Medical Director and the attending nephrologist, and follow the escalation list for a backup technician. Record the delay and the result.
Why it matters
Chloramine breakthrough causes hemolysis in every patient on the loop at once. A missed test is a missing guard.

Itching in one room

What you see
Two patients in the small back room of a unit in Pampanga develop sudden, severe itching and nausea an hour into the session. Patients in the main hall are well. The back room was recently connected to a separate water line.
What is likely going on
A water problem limited to one supply. Fluoride and other chemicals have caused exactly this pattern.
What to do
Declare a possible cluster to the charge nurse. Assess both patients, and treat per protocol with an electrocardiogram (ECG) and a cardiac monitor if available. Hold new starts in that room per protocol. Ask the technician what supplies the back room and to test it. Call the Medical Director and the attending nephrologists.
Why it matters
A room-by-room comparison is the fastest route to the cause. Fatal arrhythmia was part of the published fluoride outbreak.

Chills on the new jug

What you see
Three patients develop shivering and a temperature of 38.4 °C within forty minutes of starting. All three machines were connected to bicarbonate concentrate mixed the evening before and left uncovered.
What is likely going on
A pyrogenic reaction from bacteria or endotoxin in the bicarbonate concentrate is likely. Bloodstream infection must still be considered in each patient.
What to do
Treat each patient per the fever protocol and Module 17, including blood cultures as the protocol directs. Ask that the containers be kept, not discarded, and that fresh concentrate be used per protocol. Call the Medical Director and the attending nephrologists.
Why it matters
Discarding the jug destroys the evidence. Keeping it allows the cause to be confirmed and the mixing practice to be corrected.

The pink plasma

What you see
A man is found to have a hemoglobin far lower than last month. His blood sample after dialysis is pink-tinged. Two other patients on the same shift report back pain during their sessions this week.
What is likely going on
Low-grade hemolysis in several patients, possibly from chloramine breakthrough, a tubing lot, or fluid temperature.
What to do
Tell the charge nurse and the technician, and ask for the chlorine records and the disposables lot numbers. Call the Medical Director and the attending nephrologists. Follow the unit’s protocol for any patient with active hemolysis on the machine, including not returning the blood.
Why it matters
Hemolysis can be slow and spread over days. A falling hemoglobin in several patients at once is a cluster, even without drama.

The falling reverse osmosis trend

What you see
Reading the log on a quiet Saturday, you see that the reverse osmosis salt rejection has fallen a little each day for two weeks. No patient is ill.
What is likely going on
The main barrier is losing efficiency. Dissolved chemicals, including metals, may be passing in increasing amounts.
What to do
Ask the technician what is planned and by when. Inform the Medical Director in writing the same day. Note it in your shift record.
Why it matters
Aluminum and other metals harm slowly and silently. A trend is the warning that comes before any symptom.
Field notes
  • Read the chlorine entry in the water log before the first patient of your shift starts.
  • When two patients have the same new complaint, ask what else they share: room, loop, machine, concentrate batch, or lot.
  • Never let a concentrate jug, dialyzer, or blood line from a suspected cluster be thrown away.
  • Ask after every water service or concentrate change whether a test was done before patients started.
  • Keep the technician’s and the Medical Director’s numbers on your phone, not only on the wall.
Case review

Back to the case: Everyone on the afternoon shift is nauseated

What makes this a cluster? Three patients developed new symptoms within forty minutes of starting, on the same shift, in the same room. Their complaints overlap, and their pressures are higher than expected early in a session. The morning shift was well. Something changed between shifts, and two changes are known: a new bicarbonate concentrate supply and yesterday’s water service. Together they share an exposure, which makes this a unit problem until proven otherwise.

Which causes fit, and what would you ask to see? The picture fits fluid that is too concentrated: headache, nausea, thirst or a feeling of heat, cramps, and rising pressure. A proportioning fault or a wrong concentrate could produce it, and the concentrate change makes this the leading suspect. Early hemolysis also fits, so chloramine after yesterday’s service, overheated fluid, and a faulty tubing lot stay on the list. Contaminated bicarbonate would more often bring chills and fever, which no one has yet. You ask to see the concentrate containers on each affected machine with their batch numbers, the machines’ conductivity and temperature readings, an immediate total chlorine test, the record of yesterday’s service, and the dialyzer and tubing lots. You look at the blood in each venous line and ask for sodium, potassium, and hemoglobin per protocol.

Your first steps in the next 10 minutes, and whom you call. You tell the charge nurse aloud that this is a possible cluster. Each patient is assessed, and fluid removal is stopped per protocol. Because the concentrate is the leading suspect, treatment on the affected machines is paused per protocol until the conductivity and the containers are checked. If any line shows translucent, dark blood or a patient has chest or back pain, the blood pump is stopped, the lines are clamped, and the blood is not returned, as Module 16 teaches. Per protocol, no new patient is started on the machines or concentrate batch in question. You check the other eleven patients, because the attack rate matters. The containers, fluid samples, and disposables are kept. You call the Medical Director at once and each patient’s attending nephrologist. You record times, stations, machines, batch, lots, and whom you told.

🚨 Emergency quick cards: Port-wine blood, hemolysis · Fever, rigors, sepsis. One screen each, saved for use with no signal. Print a copy for the crash cart.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Reading the daily water log with the technician

Perform this at the start of a supervised shift, in the water room, with the technician and your preceptor present. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • Locates the water room, the water log, and the technician on duty at the start of the shift.
  • CriticalConfirms that today’s pre-shift total chlorine (or chloramine) test was done, recorded, and within the unit’s limit.
  • Identifies the sample point for each recorded result.
  • Reads the most recent microbiology and endotoxin results and states whether an action or maximum level was reached.
  • If a level was reached, confirms that the corrective action and the re-test are recorded.
  • Notes recent maintenance, disinfection, or concentrate changes that could explain new symptoms on the shift.
  • Reads the reverse osmosis performance trend and states whether it is stable or falling.
  • CriticalStates the unit’s rule for what happens to treatments when a chlorine test fails.
  • CriticalNames the escalation path: technician, charge nurse, attending nephrologist, Medical Director.
  • CriticalStates the first steps of the unit’s cluster protocol, including which samples and supplies are kept.
  • Records in the shift note any water-related concern, the time, and the person to whom it was reported.

Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 8 questions at random from this module’s 15-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 7 of 8 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 15Why can a contaminant that is harmless in drinking water harm a dialysis patient?
Three gates are lost, and the exposure runs to hundreds of liters a week.
Question 2 of 15A dialysis fluid flow of 500 mL per minute runs for 240 minutes, three times a week. About how much fluid meets the blood each week?
500 × 240 ÷ 1,000 = 120 L per session, and 120 × 3 = 360 L per week.
Question 3 of 15Which step of water treatment removes chloramine?
Chloramine passes through reverse osmosis and deionization. Carbon removes it, which is why the chlorine test is taken after the first carbon bed.
Question 4 of 15Why are two carbon beds placed in series?
A test between the beds catches exhaustion of the first while the second still protects.
Question 5 of 15Over two afternoon shifts, four patients in one room develop new anemia, and two have back pain during dialysis. The technician changed a carbon tank last week. Which contaminant is the leading suspect?
Hemolysis after a carbon change points to chloramine. A faulty tubing lot is the main mimic.
Question 6 of 15Several patients develop severe itching and nausea during dialysis, and one has ventricular fibrillation. All were treated in one room that uses a temporary deionizer. What is the likely cause?
This matches the published outbreak in which a temporary deionizer released fluoride: 12 of 15 patients in one room became ill.
Question 7 of 15What did the Caruaru outbreak in Brazil show?
Of 130 patients, 101 met the case definition of acute liver failure and 50 died. The water had not been treated.
Question 8 of 15What does the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) test miss, according to Glorieux and colleagues?
About 1 in 10 pure or ultrapure samples still provoked a cytokine response. The clinical meaning is unknown.
Question 9 of 15What does conductivity in dialysis fluid mainly reflect?
Conductivity rises with charged particles. It cannot detect bacteria, endotoxin, or uncharged toxins.
Question 10 of 15Twenty minutes into the session, a patient complains of intense thirst, headache, and nausea, and the blood pressure has risen. The bicarbonate jug was changed before the start. What should you suspect first?
High sodium pulls water out of cells and raises thirst and pressure. Check the concentrate, the conductivity, and the machine.
Question 11 of 15What did the factorial randomized trial by Asci and colleagues show about ultrapure dialysis fluid?
The hazard ratio was 0.90 with a confidence interval from 0.61 to 1.32. The subgroup finding needs confirmation.
Question 12 of 15The meta-analysis by Susantitaphong and colleagues found that ultrapure fluid raised hemoglobin and albumin and lowered C-reactive protein. How should you describe these findings?
These are surrogates. The authors called for a large trial with hard outcomes.
Question 13 of 15At 3 p.m., two patients in different rooms on the same water loop develop chills and fever within an hour of starting. Neither has a catheter. What is the best next step?
The same new symptom on one loop is a unit problem until proven otherwise. Early chills suggest endotoxin.
Question 14 of 15In a cluster, 6 of 10 patients on machines that received a new concentrate batch are ill, and 0 of 9 on the old batch are. What does this comparison tell you?
An attack rate of 60% against 0% points to the batch. It guides testing; it does not prove the cause.
Question 15 of 15At the start of your shift, the log shows no total chlorine result for today, and the first patients are about to start. What do you do?
The pre-shift chlorine test protects red cells. The policy states what happens when it is missing.

Terms in plain words

Dialysis fluid (dialysate)
The fluid on the other side of the dialyzer membrane, made from treated water and two concentrates.
Product water
Water after treatment, ready to make dialysis fluid and concentrates.
Reverse osmosis
A tight membrane process that rejects most dissolved salts, metals, and microbes. The main barrier in water treatment.
Chloramine
A disinfectant added to city water. It passes reverse osmosis, is removed by carbon, and breaks red cells.
Endotoxin
A part of the outer wall of certain bacteria that triggers fever and inflammation, even when the bacteria are dead.
Conductivity
How well the fluid carries an electric current. It reflects total dissolved salt, mostly sodium.
Proportioning
The machine’s mixing of treated water with acid and bicarbonate concentrates in a fixed ratio.
Ultrapure dialysis fluid
Dialysis fluid made to stricter bacterial and endotoxin limits than standard fluid.
Action level
A test result below the maximum level that calls for corrective action and a re-test.
Attack rate
The number of people who became ill divided by the number who were exposed.
