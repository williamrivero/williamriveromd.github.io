Before you begin

How this examination works

This examination covers the whole Physician on Duty in the Dialysis Unit series. Each sitting draws 30 questions at random from a bank of 135, in random order, with the answer choices shuffled. Choose the single best answer for each question, then select Check answers.

When to take itAfter you have finished every module and reviewed the questions you missed.
How to take itIn one sitting, without notes. Allow about one minute per question.
Proposed pass mark24 of 30 best answers (80 percent). Your trainer may set a different mark.
If you fall shortEach explanation names its module. Reread those modules, then select New set of questions.
What a pass meansA pass shows that you know the material. It does not show that you can do the work. Competence is demonstrated on your unit’s own equipment and signed off by a preceptor.

Examination

End-of-course examination

Question 1Which statement best describes the physician on duty in a hemodialysis unit?
The physician on duty is physically present and gives acute care during the shift. The prescription and long-term plan belong to the attending nephrologist. Module 1.
Question 2A nurse asks you to lower a patient’s dry weight. What is the best response?
Dry weight is part of the prescription. The nurse’s observation is useful evidence and should reach the nephrologist, who decides. Module 1.
Question 3When should you call the attending nephrologist about a patient with chest pain during dialysis?
In an emergency, call during the first steps, not after. Someone else can dial while you examine the patient. Module 1.
Question 4In SBAR, which part contains what you found on examination and what you think is happening?
Assessment gives your findings, with numbers, and your working diagnosis. Module 1.
Question 5What does the published evidence show about SBAR?
The evidence is moderate and comes mostly from before-and-after studies. It is strongest for telephone communication. Module 1.
Question 6At which points in a session are problems most likely?
Assessment before the start finds unstable patients. The last hour brings low pressure and cramps. Disconnection brings bleeding and dizziness on standing. Module 1.
Question 7Your unit has no written protocol for a situation you face. What is the safest approach?
Without a protocol, give emergency care as any physician would, and call. Do not assume that general ward practice fits a dialysis patient. Module 1.
Question 8Which of these may a physician on duty change without calling the attending nephrologist?
The dialysis fluid, dry weight, and treatment time are all part of the nephrologist’s prescription. Module 1.
Question 9A patient wants to stop the session one hour early. What should you do?
A patient may decline treatment. Your duties are to make sure the choice is informed, to record it, and to tell the nephrologist. Module 1.
Question 10You want to share an interesting electrocardiogram from a patient in a private chat group of physicians. What applies?
The Data Privacy Act of 2012 protects health information wherever it is stored or sent, including personal telephones. Module 1.
Question 11What is a near miss?
Near misses are reported because they show where the next harmful error could happen. Module 1.
Question 12Before you telephone the nephrologist about an overloaded patient, which set of facts should you have ready?
These five items answer most of the nephrologist’s first questions. Module 1.
Question 13A nurse says, “He is not himself today,” about a patient whose vital signs are normal. What should you do?
An experienced nurse’s impression is a clinical finding. Early confusion, infection, and low blood sugar can show before vital signs change. Module 1.
Question 14Which item is a critical part of the start-of-shift round?
You cannot look for emergency equipment during an emergency. Module 1.
Question 15You cannot reach the attending nephrologist during an emergency. What do you do?
Every unit should have an escalation list. Emergency care continues while someone else makes the calls. Module 1.
Question 16About how much fluid do two healthy kidneys filter each day?
The kidneys filter about 180 liters a day and take back about 99%, leaving 1 to 2 liters of urine. Module 2.
Question 17A patient who makes no urine is given one liter of intravenous saline. Where is that liter six hours later?
Without urine output, the fluid stays until dialysis removes it. Module 2.
Question 18Why does telling a patient to “drink less” often fail when it is the only advice?
Salt raises the blood sodium slightly and causes thirst. Cutting salt reduces thirst and weight gain. Module 2.
Question 19Fluid gained between sessions collects mainly in which compartment?
Salt and water expand the extracellular fluid. Module 2.
Question 20Why does blood pressure fall when fluid is removed too fast during dialysis?
Dialysis takes water from the plasma. If plasma refilling from the tissues cannot keep up, blood volume and pressure fall. Module 2.
Question 21A 50-kg patient needs 3,000 mL removed in 4 hours. What is the fluid removal rate?
3,000 ÷ 4 ÷ 50 = 15 mL per kg per hour. Module 2.
Question 22In the analysis of the HEMO Study, fluid removal rates above 13 mL per kg per hour were:
The association was observational (adjusted hazard ratio 1.59 compared with rates up to 10). No randomized trial has tested a specific limit. Module 2.
Question 23About what share of the body’s potassium is inside cells?
About 98% is inside cells. The blood test measures only the small amount outside. Module 2.
Question 24How do insulin with glucose and inhaled salbutamol lower the blood potassium?
They shift potassium into cells and buy time. In a patient without kidney function, dialysis is what removes it. Module 2.
Question 25A dialysis patient with possible hyperkalemia has a normal ECG. What does this mean?
The electrocardiogram (ECG) is an insensitive test. In a review of 90 patients with a potassium of 6.0 mmol/L or more, no ECG measurement reliably identified hyperkalemia. Module 2.
Question 26A dialysis patient fasts for a long time before a procedure. Which change is most likely?
With little insulin, potassium moves out of cells, and without kidneys it cannot be excreted. Module 2.
Question 27How does hemodialysis correct metabolic acidosis?
The dialysis fluid has a higher bicarbonate level than the blood, so bicarbonate crosses into the patient. Module 2.
Question 28When is the blood bicarbonate of a dialysis patient usually lowest?
Acid builds up between sessions and uses up bicarbonate. Module 2.
Question 29In the study of 32,065 patients dialyzing three times a week, what was found on the day after the two-day gap?
The death rate was 22.1 compared with 18.0 per 100 person-years on other days. Module 2.
Question 30A blood sample taken immediately after dialysis shows a low-normal potassium. What should you expect a few hours later?
Potassium rebounds after dialysis. The immediate post-dialysis level underestimates the level later that day. Module 2.
Question 31In a patient with a forearm fistula, what does the term “arterial line” mean?
The names describe direction in the circuit. Both needles are in the same vessel. Module 5.
Question 32A connection comes loose between the arterial needle and the blood pump. What is the main danger?
The pump pulls on this segment, so air is sucked in. Module 5.
Question 33Which process removes most of the urea and potassium in standard hemodialysis?
Small molecules cross quickly by diffusion. Convection adds little in standard hemodialysis. Module 5.
Question 34Why do blood and dialysis fluid run in opposite directions through the dialyzer?
Blood that is nearly clean meets the freshest fluid, so diffusion continues to the end. Module 5.
Question 35A colleague says that a high-flux dialyzer “gives a better urea clearance.” What is the most accurate reply?
Flux depends on pore size. Urea removal depends mostly on surface area and flows. Module 5.
Question 36What did the HEMO Study find when it compared high-flux with low-flux dialyzers in 1,846 patients?
The relative risk was 0.92, with a confidence interval that includes no effect. Module 5.
Question 37The machine goes into bypass after a conductivity alarm. What is happening?
Bypass keeps fluid of the wrong composition away from the blood. No dialysis takes place meanwhile. Module 5.
Question 38Why can a correct conductivity reading not confirm that the potassium level of the dialysis fluid is right?
The sensor cannot tell one salt from another, so staff check the concentrate label. Module 5.
Question 39Mrs. Aquino is dialyzing through a new fistula. The high venous pressure alarm has sounded twice, and her arm hurts near the venous needle. What do you do first?
This suggests infiltration. Look at the arm before anything on the machine is changed. Module 5.
Question 40Mr. Ocampo is asleep with his fistula arm under a blanket. The machine is quiet. Why is this unsafe?
Most of the venous pressure comes from the needle itself. A visible access is the safeguard. Module 5.
Question 41The arterial pressure becomes steadily more negative while the displayed blood flow is unchanged. What is the best interpretation?
With strong suction the pump tubing partly collapses and delivers less than the screen shows. Module 5.
Question 42The transmembrane pressure has climbed for an hour, and dark streaks are visible in the dialyzer. What is the most likely cause?
Clotted fibers reduce the working membrane, so more pressure is needed to remove the same water. Module 5.
Question 43Twenty minutes after a temperature alarm was overridden, Mr. Salonga reports back pain and breathlessness. The blood in his venous line looks translucent, like red wine. What applies to the blood in the circuit?
These signs suggest hemolysis. Dialysis is stopped, the blood is not returned, and the attending nephrologist is called at once. Module 5.
Question 44A blood-leak alarm sounds. No blood is visible in the fluid tubing, and a technician offers to override the alarm. What is the best response?
A small leak may be invisible. Torn fibers mean blood loss and contact with non-sterile fluid. Module 5.
Question 45Which statement about the randomized evidence for hemodiafiltration is accurate?
The hazard ratio for death was 0.77 in selected patients able to reach a high convection volume. Module 5.
Question 46What is the main difference between a fistula and a graft?
In a fistula the patient’s own vein is needled. In a graft a synthetic tube connects artery and vein and is needled. Module 9.
Question 47Why is the thrill of a healthy access continuous?
The access is a low-resistance path, so flow and vibration continue through diastole. Module 9.
Question 48You feel a hard, bounding pulse where a soft thrill used to be. What is the most likely cause?
Blood enters at arterial pressure and cannot leave easily, so pressure rises before the narrowing. Module 9.
Question 49You raise the arm of a patient with a forearm fistula. The lower half stays full and the upper half flattens. What does this suggest?
A normal fistula flattens along its length. With an outflow narrowing, only the part beyond it drains. Module 9.
Question 50In the pulse augmentation test, the pulse near the join barely strengthens. What does this suggest?
With normal inflow the pulse strengthens clearly against the occluding finger. Module 9.
Question 51Mr. Ramos has a looped forearm graft. Before his session the nurse finds no thrill and no bruit. He feels well. What do you do first?
No thrill and no bruit mean thrombosis. A trial needle adds harm, and earlier treatment is associated with better results. Module 9.
Question 52Which statement about graft infection does the KDOQI guideline support?
A graft is foreign material, and bacteria that reach it are hard to clear. Module 9.
Question 53Mrs. Aquino has a bulge on her fistula. Today the skin over it is shiny, cannot be pinched up, and has a small scab. The thrill is normal. What is the best action?
Thin shiny skin and a scab mean a wall at risk of rupture. Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) advises emergency surgical assessment for erosion. Module 9.
Question 54Why does repeated needling in one small area raise concern?
In an observational study of 7,058 patients, area puncture was associated with a higher risk of access failure than rope-ladder needling. Module 9.
Question 55Mr. Santiago has diabetes and a new upper-arm fistula. The hand on that side is cold, numb, and pale. The thrill is strong. What is the most likely problem?
Blood is taking the shortcut through the access, and diseased arteries cannot compensate. Module 9.
Question 56A needle site is still bleeding after the session. Which technique is correct?
Pressure belongs on the needle site. It should stop the bleeding without stopping the flow, and it should not be lifted early. Module 9.
Question 57A scab over an aneurysm gives way during dialysis, and blood pours from the arm. Which response is correct?
Direct pressure comes first and is not released. Blood in the circuit is never a reason to delay. Module 9.
Question 58In the review of 88 fatal access bleeds, where did most deaths with a known location occur?
The figure was 78%. Patients and families need to know the warning signs and how to press. Module 9.
Question 59A ward nurse is about to take a blood pressure on a fistula arm. What is the best explanation to give?
The rule rests on physiology and expert opinion, and it costs nothing to follow. Module 9.
Question 60What does KDOQI say about a narrowing that causes no clinical indicators?
KDOQI relies on regular examination and the treatment record to identify the narrowings that need action. Module 9.
Question 61What does the cuff on a tunneled catheter do?
The cuff sits inside the tunnel. Ingrown tissue holds the catheter and forms a barrier between skin and vein. Module 10.
Question 62The chart shows that a non-tunneled neck catheter was inserted five weeks ago. It flows well. What should you do?
Non-tunneled catheters are intended for short use because infection risk rises with time. The plan belongs to the nephrologist. Module 10.
Question 63Why is a catheter examined before every connection?
Once the pump starts, organisms at the exit site or in the lumen can be flushed into the blood. Module 10.
Question 64Before connection, a ring of white fabric is visible at the exit site of a tunneled catheter. What comes next?
A visible cuff means the catheter has moved outward. The exposed part is not sterile and the tip may be out of position. Module 10.
Question 65Which step, done before a cap is removed, protects most directly against air entry?
Central venous pressure can fall below room pressure during a breath in. Scrubbing and masks protect against bacteria. Module 10.
Question 66A ward nurse asks to run an overnight antibiotic through a patient’s dialysis catheter because the veins are difficult. What is the best answer?
Each extra opening of the hub adds risk of infection and air entry. Module 10.
Question 67What did the cluster-randomized study of catheter care compare?
Intervention facilities had fewer positive blood cultures per 1,000 catheter-days. Module 10.
Question 68Why can a lumen accept a saline flush and still fail to give blood back?
Pushing lifts the obstruction away and pulling draws it back, like a flap. Module 10.
Question 69A tunneled catheter placed three days ago flows poorly from the first session. Which cause is most likely?
Early poor flow is usually mechanical. Clot and fibrin sheath are typical after weeks of good function. Module 10.
Question 70A lumen resists a saline flush. Why should nobody push harder with a small syringe?
For the same push, a small syringe generates a higher pressure. Stop and call the attending nephrologist. Module 10.
Question 71In the study of 102 patients with tunneled catheters, what happened when the blood lines were reversed?
Recirculation above 10% was found in 86% with lines reversed and 3% in the usual position. Module 10.
Question 72Forty minutes into dialysis, a 67-year-old man with a tunneled catheter has shaking chills. His exit site is clean. How do you interpret this?
Bacteria inside the lumen are washed into the blood when the pump runs. The exit site may look normal. Module 10.
Question 73For the same patient, what should happen before the first antibiotic?
Guidelines give priority to cultures before antibiotics. Forearm veins are saved for a future fistula. Module 10.
Question 74At disconnection a hub is found open and unclamped. The seated patient coughs, becomes breathless, and her blood pressure falls. What do you do first?
This is suspected air entry. Returning blood or flushing could push more air into the patient. Module 10.
Question 75A patient eight months into catheter dialysis says a fistula operation is unnecessary because the catheter works. What is the most useful response?
Catheter use is associated with more infection and death than fistula use. The access plan belongs to the nephrologist. Module 10.
Question 76Why is “do not start” never a complete decision for a dialysis patient?
Not dialyzing carries its own risk. Module 11.
Question 77Mr. Ramos has a catheter. His blood pressure is far below that of his last six sessions, and he has felt cold and weak since yesterday. He has no fever. What fits best?
Fever is often absent in catheter bloodstream infection. Module 11.
Question 78What is the purpose of the first minute of the three-minute sequence?
Walking, breathing, alertness, and speech give the fastest information. Module 11.
Question 79A patient is 4 kilograms above dry weight and sleeps sitting up. Her chest is clear and her ankles are not swollen. What does the evidence suggest?
In 1,106 paired examinations, crackles and edema agreed poorly with lung congestion on ultrasound. Module 11.
Question 80Mrs. Bautista arrives for dialysis. Her speech has been slurred since breakfast and her right hand is clumsy. Vital signs are at her usual values. What is the best decision?
A new neurologic deficit needs urgent imaging, and heparin could worsen a brain bleed. Module 11.
Question 81Why is the outpatient dialysis chair the wrong place to begin for a patient with active bleeding?
The circuit usually needs a blood thinner. Module 11.
Question 82In the study of suspected infection in catheter-dependent patients, what was found about fever?
Fever was a presenting symptom in 47%, so its absence does not exclude infection. Module 11.
Question 83Which question bears most directly on the safety of heparin today?
Heparin can worsen any bleeding, including a slow bleed inside the skull after a fall. Report these answers before connection. The nephrologist decides the heparin plan. Module 11.
Question 84What do the large studies of missed dialysis sessions show?
The studies were observational, and most patients were on three sessions a week. Module 11.
Question 85Mr. Santiago missed his last session and arrives 6 kilograms above dry weight, comfortable at rest. The nurse suggests removing all of it today. What is the best response?
Fast fluid removal is associated with harm. Dividing the work across sessions is a prescription decision. Module 11.
Question 86A patient admits he skipped a session because he had no fare. Which response serves him best?
Patients who feel blamed hide missed sessions. Module 11.
Question 87Under the CDC recommendations, how is a patient positive for hepatitis B surface antigen dialyzed?
Hepatitis B survives on surfaces for at least seven days. Module 11.
Question 88A patient transfers to your unit this morning with no hepatitis B result in the chart. He says, “I am negative.” What should you do?
Unknown is not the same as negative. Module 11.
Question 89After dialysis, Mrs. Cruz has her usual seated blood pressure. On standing she is dizzy and her pressure falls. What is the best next step?
The fall on standing shows she cannot yet compensate for the fluid removed, so she is not safe to walk. Dry weight review belongs to the nephrologist. Module 11.
Question 90A patient’s needle sites have bled longer than usual at three sessions in a row. Why should the nephrologist be told?
The KDOQI guideline lists it among the clinical signs of a failing access. Other causes exist. Module 11.
Question 91Why do most patients tolerate the removal of several liters in one session?
The machine removes water only from the blood. The three defenses hold the pressure while it does. Module 13.
Question 92Why does tightening of the veins help to maintain pressure during fluid removal?
When veins tighten, the heart stays filled. When they relax, blood pools and output falls. Module 13.
Question 93A woman with long-standing diabetes has a pressure of 80/46 mm Hg in hour three. Her pulse is 64 per minute and regular. What does the pulse tell you?
Autonomic neuropathy and reflex loss of sympathetic tone can both keep the pulse from rising. Module 13.
Question 94In the analysis by Flythe and colleagues, which definition was most strongly associated with death?
Nadir-based definitions tracked mortality. Definitions built on symptoms or interventions did not. Module 13.
Question 95Published estimates of how often intradialytic hypotension occurs vary widely. What is the main reason?
A fall in pressure alone is very common. A fall with symptoms and an intervention is much less common. Module 13.
Question 96A patient’s pressure has fallen in hour three and the nurse has not yet acted. The patient responds and is breathing. What comes first?
These two steps take seconds. Assessment and the call follow immediately. Module 13.
Question 97How strong is the evidence for the head-down, legs-up position?
The European guideline grades it as opinion. It is used because it is quick, carries little risk in a patient who is breathing comfortably, and fits the physiology. Module 13.
Question 98A patient recovers after first steps. The nurse asks whether to restart fluid removal at the original rate. What is the best answer?
The remaining goal, the time, and the dry weight belong to the prescription. Module 13.
Question 99Ten minutes into a session, a man becomes itchy and flushed, begins to wheeze, and his pressure falls. What happens to the blood in the circuit?
Onset in the first minutes with itching and wheeze suggests a dialyzer reaction. That blood is not returned. Module 13.
Question 100A woman with a dialysis catheter has a pressure of 86/50 mm Hg forty minutes into her session. She is shivering and feels warm. What is the best interpretation?
An early drop with chills and a catheter does not fit fluid removal. Module 13.
Question 101Which finding makes a simple volume-related drop less likely?
A patient who is not overloaded has little fluid to lose. Look for another cause, or a dry weight set too low. Module 13.
Question 102What did the MyTEMP trial show?
The hazard ratio was 1.00. The trial tested a policy, not cooling for individual patients with frequent episodes. Module 13.
Question 103What did the echocardiography studies by Burton and colleagues show?
Stunning occurred in 64% of 70 patients and was associated with higher mortality. The studies were small and observational. Module 13.
Question 104A man has had low pressure in four of his last six sessions, each time after a full meal in hour two. What should the physician on duty do?
Moving the meal addresses the trigger and protects nutrition. The plan belongs to the nephrologist. Module 13.
Question 105During an episode, the blood in the venous line looks translucent, like red wine, and the patient has back pain. A second patient nearby is also unwell. What do you do?
These findings suggest hemolysis, possibly from the dialysis fluid. Hemolyzed blood is not returned. Module 13.
Question 106Ten minutes into a session with a new dialyzer type, a patient develops itching, wheeze, and a falling blood pressure. What is done with the blood in the circuit?
A severe early reaction may be caused by the dialyzer. Returning the blood delivers more of the trigger. Module 15.
Question 107Why is a single raised troponin a poor guide to heart attack in a dialysis patient?
Chronic elevation is common. A rise or fall with evidence of ischemia defines an acute heart attack. Module 15.
Question 108A 70-year-old woman with diabetes becomes breathless and sweaty in the third hour. Her blood pressure has fallen. She has no chest pain. After the first protocol steps, what comes next?
Fewer than half of dialysis patients with a heart attack had chest pain in a large registry. Treat these signs as possible ischemia. Module 15.
Question 109According to the ERC 2025 guidance, who operates the dialysis machine during a cardiac arrest?
Assigning the machine to trained staff leaves the leader free to direct the code. Module 15.
Question 110A patient collapses during dialysis, and the nurse sees foam in the venous line. What is correct for the circuit?
Air in the venous line is a do-not-return condition. Returning the blood would push more air into the patient. Module 15.
Question 111What does the ERC 2025 guidance say about the dialysis access during a cardiac arrest?
The access is already in the circulation and is faster than a new cannula. Module 15.
Question 112When must a patient be disconnected from the dialysis machine before a shock?
The ERC advises disconnection unless the machine is defibrillation-proof. Know your machines’ rating before a code. Module 15.
Question 113A man who missed a session arrests in the waiting area before dialysis. The monitor shows a slow, wide rhythm. Which reversible cause leads your list?
Arrest before a session after a long gap points to hyperkalemia. The other three need a running circuit. Module 15.
Question 114Why do arrhythmias cluster around the first session after the long gap?
Both the peak and the rapid correction stress the rhythm. Module 15.
Question 115Studies disagree on whether the first rhythm in dialysis unit arrests is usually shockable. What follows in practice?
Either type of rhythm may be present, so early rhythm analysis is needed alongside immediate compressions. Module 15.
Question 116A patient develops new atrial fibrillation in the second hour. He is stable and feels well. A colleague suggests starting a blood thinner today. What is the best response?
Evidence for long-term AF treatment in dialysis patients is limited. The decision belongs to the nephrologist and cardiologist. Module 15.
Question 117How do the ERC and AHA 2025 guidelines compare on intravenous calcium for cardiac arrest from high potassium?
The guidelines differ, and both rest on limited evidence. Your unit’s protocol decides. Module 15.
Question 118A patient arrests in a half-reclined dialysis chair. What is the first step for positioning?
Compressions start where the patient is, on the back, on as firm a surface as can be achieved without delay. Module 15.
Question 119A session is stopped for chest pain with new ST depression. The patient is stable. How does he travel to the emergency room?
For suspected acute coronary syndrome, the chest pain guideline recommends transfer by emergency medical services. Module 15.
Question 120Which statement about the debrief after a code is most accurate?
The AHA supports performance-focused debriefing, both immediate and delayed. Module 15.
Question 121Why is the circuit blood not returned when hemolysis is suspected?
Returning it can cause severe hyperkalemia in a patient who cannot excrete potassium. Module 16.
Question 122Twelve minutes into a first treatment with a new dialyzer, a patient has itching, a hoarse voice, wheeze, and a falling blood pressure. What happens to the blood in the circuit?
This is a severe early (type A) reaction. The circuit blood carries the trigger. Module 16.
Question 123Why may the venous pressure alarm stay silent when the venous needle comes out?
Tubing and needle resistance remain, so the pressure can stay within the alarm limits. Module 16.
Question 124Mr. Aquino, 78, has dementia and keeps pulling at his blanket. The machine is quiet. He is now pale and drowsy. What do you do first?
A covered access in a restless patient is the usual setting for a dislodged venous needle. A silent machine does not exclude it. Module 16.
Question 125A patient coughs and becomes breathless, and there is foam below the venous chamber. The pump is stopped and the line clamped. Which position do published reviews advise for venous air embolism?
The left lateral position tends to hold air in the right heart. Sitting upright lets air rise toward the brain. Module 16.
Question 126Why does an opening in the circuit before the blood pump draw air in?
Pressure there is below atmospheric, so an opening admits air instead of leaking blood. Module 16.
Question 127Which pair describes a type B dialyzer reaction?
Type B reactions come later and are usually mild. Dangerous causes of chest pain are excluded first. Module 16.
Question 128Three patients in one shift develop abdominal pain and high blood pressure within 30 minutes. What is the best next step?
The same new symptoms in several patients point to a shared cause. Blood is not returned if hemolysis is possible. Module 16.
Question 129The venous chamber holds a large clot and the dialyzer shows dark streaks. A staff member suggests pushing saline hard to return the blood. What do you advise?
Clot must never be pushed toward the patient. Module 16.
Question 130Which part of the water system removes chloramine?
Chloramine passes through reverse osmosis. Exhausted carbon has led to outbreaks of hemolysis. Module 16.
Question 131A conductivity alarm returns after each reset and the fluid is in bypass. A staff member proposes overriding it to finish the last hour. What is correct?
Fluid that is too dilute can break red cells. The bypass is protecting the patient. Module 16.
Question 132What did investigators find in the 1998 multistate outbreak of hemolysis?
The one common factor across three states was a single lot of tubing. Module 16.
Question 133Ms. Lim has her first dialysis with a very high urea. Near the end she has a headache and becomes confused. The lines and blood look normal. Which statement fits best?
New neurologic symptoms in a first treatment with a high urea suggest disequilibrium, which is not a circuit fault. Module 16.
Question 134What does a confirmed blood leak alarm mean?
The detector sits in the used dialysis fluid. Blood return then follows unit protocol and the nephrologist. Module 16.
Question 135You cannot tell whether sudden chest pain is hemolysis, a reaction, or cardiac. The pump is stopped and the lines clamped. What is the safest position on blood return?
Giving up the circuit blood costs less than returning air, broken cells, or an allergen. Module 16.
