How this examination works

This examination covers the whole Physician on Duty in the Dialysis Unit series. Each sitting draws 30 questions at random from a bank of 480, in random order, with the answer choices shuffled. Choose the single best answer for each question, then select Check answers.

When to take itAfter you have finished every module and reviewed the questions you missed.
How to take itIn one sitting, without notes. Allow about one minute per question.
Proposed pass mark24 of 30 best answers (80 percent). Your trainer may set a different mark.
If you fall shortEach explanation names its module. Reread those modules, then select New set of questions.
What a pass meansA pass shows that you know the material. It does not show that you can do the work. Competence is demonstrated on your unit’s own equipment and signed off by a preceptor.

Examination

End-of-course examination

Question 1Which statement best describes the physician on duty in a hemodialysis unit?
The physician on duty is physically present and gives acute care during the shift. The prescription and long-term plan belong to the attending nephrologist. Module 1.
Question 2A nurse asks you to lower a patient’s dry weight. What is the best response?
Dry weight is part of the prescription. The nurse’s observation is useful evidence and should reach the nephrologist, who decides. Module 1.
Question 3When should you call the attending nephrologist about a patient with chest pain during dialysis?
In an emergency, call during the first steps, not after. Someone else can dial while you examine the patient. Module 1.
Question 4In SBAR, which part contains what you found on examination and what you think is happening?
Assessment gives your findings, with numbers, and your working diagnosis. Module 1.
Question 5What does the published evidence show about SBAR?
The evidence is moderate and comes mostly from before-and-after studies. It is strongest for telephone communication. Module 1.
Question 6At which points in a session are problems most likely?
Assessment before the start finds unstable patients. The last hour brings low pressure and cramps. Disconnection brings bleeding and dizziness on standing. Module 1.
Question 7Your unit has no written protocol for a situation you face. What is the safest approach?
Without a protocol, give emergency care as any physician would, and call. Do not assume that general ward practice fits a dialysis patient. Module 1.
Question 8Which of these may a physician on duty change without calling the attending nephrologist?
The dialysis fluid, dry weight, and treatment time are all part of the nephrologist’s prescription. Module 1.
Question 9A patient wants to stop the session one hour early. What should you do?
A patient may decline treatment. Your duties are to make sure the choice is informed, to record it, and to tell the nephrologist. Module 1.
Question 10You want to share an interesting electrocardiogram from a patient in a private chat group of physicians. What applies?
The Data Privacy Act of 2012 protects health information wherever it is stored or sent, including personal telephones. Module 1.
Question 11What is a near miss?
Near misses are reported because they show where the next harmful error could happen. Module 1.
Question 12Before you telephone the nephrologist about an overloaded patient, which set of facts should you have ready?
These five items answer most of the nephrologist’s first questions. Module 1.
Question 13A nurse says, “He is not himself today,” about a patient whose vital signs are normal. What should you do?
An experienced nurse’s impression is a clinical finding. Early confusion, infection, and low blood sugar can show before vital signs change. Module 1.
Question 14Which item is a critical part of the start-of-shift round?
You cannot look for emergency equipment during an emergency. Module 1.
Question 15You cannot reach the attending nephrologist during an emergency. What do you do?
Every unit should have an escalation list. Emergency care continues while someone else makes the calls. Module 1.
Question 16About how much fluid do two healthy kidneys filter each day?
The kidneys filter about 180 liters a day and take back about 99%, leaving 1 to 2 liters of urine. Module 2.
Question 17A patient who makes no urine is given one liter of intravenous saline. Where is that liter six hours later?
Without urine output, the fluid stays until dialysis removes it. Module 2.
Question 18Why does telling a patient to “drink less” often fail when it is the only advice?
Salt raises the blood sodium slightly and causes thirst. Cutting salt reduces thirst and weight gain. Module 2.
Question 19Fluid gained between sessions collects mainly in which compartment?
Salt and water expand the extracellular fluid. Module 2.
Question 20Why does blood pressure fall when fluid is removed too fast during dialysis?
Dialysis takes water from the plasma. If plasma refilling from the tissues cannot keep up, blood volume and pressure fall. Module 2.
Question 21A 50-kg patient needs 3,000 mL removed in 4 hours. What is the fluid removal rate?
3,000 ÷ 4 ÷ 50 = 15 mL per kg per hour. Module 2.
Question 22In the analysis of the HEMO Study, fluid removal rates above 13 mL per kg per hour were:
The association was observational (adjusted hazard ratio 1.59 compared with rates up to 10). No randomized trial has tested a specific limit. Module 2.
Question 23About what share of the body’s potassium is inside cells?
About 98% is inside cells. The blood test measures only the small amount outside. Module 2.
Question 24How do insulin with glucose and inhaled salbutamol lower the blood potassium?
They shift potassium into cells and buy time. In a patient without kidney function, dialysis is what removes it. Module 2.
Question 25A dialysis patient with possible hyperkalemia has a normal ECG. What does this mean?
The electrocardiogram (ECG) is an insensitive test. In a review of 90 patients with a potassium of 6.0 mmol/L or more, no ECG measurement reliably identified hyperkalemia. Module 2.
Question 26A dialysis patient fasts for a long time before a procedure. Which change is most likely?
With little insulin, potassium moves out of cells, and without kidneys it cannot be excreted. Module 2.
Question 27How does hemodialysis correct metabolic acidosis?
The dialysis fluid has a higher bicarbonate level than the blood, so bicarbonate crosses into the patient. Module 2.
Question 28When is the blood bicarbonate of a dialysis patient usually lowest?
Acid builds up between sessions and uses up bicarbonate. Module 2.
Question 29In the study of 32,065 patients dialyzing three times a week, what was found on the day after the two-day gap?
The death rate was 22.1 compared with 18.0 per 100 person-years on other days. Module 2.
Question 30A blood sample taken immediately after dialysis shows a low-normal potassium. What should you expect a few hours later?
Potassium rebounds after dialysis. The immediate post-dialysis level underestimates the level later that day. Module 2.
Question 31Why is urea described as a marker of uremia rather than its main cause?
Urea is easy to measure and moves with the retained load, but it causes few of the symptoms itself. Module 3.
Question 32A man has a Kt/V above target but reports new night itch, poor appetite, and morning nausea. What is the best interpretation?
Kt/V is a surrogate that tracks urea, not middle or protein-bound solutes. Module 3.
Question 33Why does conventional hemodialysis remove indoxyl sulfate poorly?
The healthy tubule secretes protein-bound solutes. Dialysis cannot. Module 3.
Question 34What did the European Uremic Toxin Work Group find about the 90 retained solutes it cataloged?
Of 90 solutes, 68 were under 500 daltons, 22 were middle molecules, and 25 were protein-bound. Module 3.
Question 35What did the IDEAL trial show?
Deaths were 37.6% with early start and 36.6% with late start. Module 3.
Question 36Why must the IDEAL result be read with care?
It tested waiting with close follow-up, not waiting in a symptomatic patient. Module 3.
Question 37A referred woman with an estimated GFR of 9 has a pericardial rub, vomiting, and asterixis. What is the best next step?
A pericardial rub and asterixis are urgent uremic indications whatever the GFR, and the vomiting adds to them. Module 3.
Question 38How does KDIGO 2024 grade its practice points on when to start dialysis?
Practice points are ungraded. Do not borrow another system’s grades. Module 3.
Question 39A resident calculates an estimated GFR from the creatinine of a patient on dialysis for a year. What is wrong?
Dialysis removes creatinine, so the blood level no longer reflects kidney function. Module 3.
Question 40What did the CHOICE cohort find about urine output at one year on hemodialysis?
The hazard ratio was 0.70 in an observational cohort, so confounding is possible. Module 3.
Question 41A timed collection gives a much lower clearance than before. The patient forgot to collect two voids. What is the best interpretation?
An incomplete collection lowers the calculated clearance. Record it honestly. Module 3.
Question 42A man who passes a liter of urine a day is scheduled for a non-urgent contrast CT scan. What should you do?
Residual function is worth protecting, and the decision belongs to the nephrologist. Module 3.
Question 43What did Moist and colleagues find about ACE inhibitor use and loss of residual function?
The adjusted odds ratio was 0.68 in an observational cohort, which does not prove protection. Module 3.
Question 44A woman on twice-weekly dialysis has rising weight gains and says her urine has fallen. What do you do?
Rising gains and falling urine are triggers to step up. The schedule and the fluid goal belong to the nephrologist. Module 3.
Question 45What have the completed randomized trials of incremental dialysis shown so far?
The British and Spanish trials found no protection. Two smaller trials suggested benefit. TwoPlus is in progress. Module 3.
Question 46Why does the left ventricle thicken in kidney failure?
Each load raises wall stress, and the ventricle thickens in response. Module 4.
Question 474D and AURORA lowered LDL cholesterol by more than 40% in hemodialysis patients. What happened to the main cardiovascular outcome?
The surrogate moved, but events did not. This heart disease is not only atherosclerosis. Module 4.
Question 48A man who has taken a statin for years starts hemodialysis. What does the KDIGO lipid guideline suggest?
KDIGO suggests not starting statins on dialysis (2A) but continuing them (2C). The nephrologist decides. Module 4.
Question 49In stable hemodialysis patients tested with an hs-cTnT assay, how many were above the general-population 99th percentile?
Nearly all were, so one value means little. A troponin I assay was raised in 13%. Module 4.
Question 50A woman on dialysis has epigastric discomfort. Her troponin is 85 ng/L; her last stable value, same assay, was 80. A repeat two hours later is 84 and her ECG is unchanged. What does this suggest?
Acute infarction needs a rise or fall with ischemia. Her symptom still needs a report. Module 4.
Question 51Why are cardiac markers drawn before the session when possible?
In one study, hs-cTnT fell from 69 to 61 ng/L after a session. Module 4.
Question 52Why are BNP and NT-proBNP hard to interpret on dialysis?
Compare with the patient’s usual level and the clinical picture. Module 4.
Question 53A man’s hemoglobin fell from 10.8 to 9.2 g/dL in a month. He feels well. What comes first?
Anemia is expected; a new fall is not. Look for bleeding and report. Module 4.
Question 54Why do dialysis patients bleed easily with a normal platelet count?
The defect is in function, not number. Heparin adds to it. Module 4.
Question 55A patient’s calcium is 8.0 mg/dL with an albumin of 2.8 g/dL, so the corrected calcium is 9.2. She has tingling around her mouth. What should you do?
Correction does not track ionized calcium on dialysis, and she has symptoms. Module 4.
Question 56A woman with a tunneled catheter is quiet and slightly confused. Her temperature is 36.2 °C and her pressure is lower than usual. What is the best interpretation?
Uremia blunts fever. Examine the catheter site and call. Module 4.
Question 57A man with diabetes on dialysis has an HbA1c of 6.0%, but his home glucose is mostly above 200 mg/dL. What best explains this?
HbA1c underestimates glycemia on hemodialysis. Report it; do not change treatment at the chair. Module 4.
Question 58A dialysis patient has a raised D-dimer. What does it tell you?
D-dimer keeps its sensitivity but loses specificity. A normal value still helps. Module 4.
Question 59Which drug is least likely to be removed by dialysis?
Only free drug in the plasma reaches the membrane. Module 4.
Question 60A nurse asks you to prescribe an antibiotic for a dialysis patient’s skin infection. What comes first?
Dosing depends on renal clearance and dialytic removal. eGFR is not valid on dialysis. Module 4.
Question 61In a patient with a forearm fistula, what does the term “arterial line” mean?
The names describe direction in the circuit. Both needles are in the same vessel. Module 5.
Question 62A connection comes loose between the arterial needle and the blood pump. What is the main danger?
The pump pulls on this segment, so air is sucked in. Module 5.
Question 63Which process removes most of the urea and potassium in standard hemodialysis?
Small molecules cross quickly by diffusion. Convection adds little in standard hemodialysis. Module 5.
Question 64Why do blood and dialysis fluid run in opposite directions through the dialyzer?
Blood that is nearly clean meets the freshest fluid, so diffusion continues to the end. Module 5.
Question 65A colleague says that a high-flux dialyzer “gives a better urea clearance.” What is the most accurate reply?
Flux depends on pore size. Urea removal depends mostly on surface area and flows. Module 5.
Question 66What did the HEMO Study find when it compared high-flux with low-flux dialyzers in 1,846 patients?
The relative risk was 0.92, with a confidence interval that includes no effect. Module 5.
Question 67The machine goes into bypass after a conductivity alarm. What is happening?
Bypass keeps fluid of the wrong composition away from the blood. No dialysis takes place meanwhile. Module 5.
Question 68Why can a correct conductivity reading not confirm that the potassium level of the dialysis fluid is right?
The sensor cannot tell one salt from another, so staff check the concentrate label. Module 5.
Question 69Mrs. Aquino is dialyzing through a new fistula. The high venous pressure alarm has sounded twice, and her arm hurts near the venous needle. What do you do first?
This suggests infiltration. Look at the arm before anything on the machine is changed. Module 5.
Question 70Mr. Ocampo is asleep with his fistula arm under a blanket. The machine is quiet. Why is this unsafe?
Most of the venous pressure comes from the needle itself. A visible access is the safeguard. Module 5.
Question 71The arterial pressure becomes steadily more negative while the displayed blood flow is unchanged. What is the best interpretation?
With strong suction the pump tubing partly collapses and delivers less than the screen shows. Module 5.
Question 72The transmembrane pressure has climbed for an hour, and dark streaks are visible in the dialyzer. What is the most likely cause?
Clotted fibers reduce the working membrane, so more pressure is needed to remove the same water. Module 5.
Question 73Twenty minutes after a temperature alarm was overridden, Mr. Salonga reports back pain and breathlessness. The blood in his venous line looks translucent, like red wine. What applies to the blood in the circuit?
These signs suggest hemolysis. Dialysis is stopped, the blood is not returned, and the attending nephrologist is called at once. Module 5.
Question 74A blood-leak alarm sounds. No blood is visible in the fluid tubing, and a technician offers to override the alarm. What is the best response?
A small leak may be invisible. Torn fibers mean blood loss and contact with non-sterile fluid. Module 5.
Question 75Which statement about the randomized evidence for hemodiafiltration is accurate?
The hazard ratio for death was 0.77 in selected patients able to reach a high convection volume. Module 5.
Question 76Why can a contaminant that is harmless in drinking water harm a dialysis patient?
Three gates are lost, and the exposure runs to hundreds of liters a week. Module 6.
Question 77A dialysis fluid flow of 500 mL per minute runs for 240 minutes, three times a week. About how much fluid meets the blood each week?
500 × 240 ÷ 1,000 = 120 L per session, and 120 × 3 = 360 L per week. Module 6.
Question 78Which step of water treatment removes chloramine?
Chloramine passes through reverse osmosis and deionization. Carbon removes it, which is why the chlorine test is taken after the first carbon bed. Module 6.
Question 79Why are two carbon beds placed in series?
A test between the beds catches exhaustion of the first while the second still protects. Module 6.
Question 80Over two afternoon shifts, four patients in one room develop new anemia, and two have back pain during dialysis. The technician changed a carbon tank last week. Which contaminant is the leading suspect?
Hemolysis after a carbon change points to chloramine. A faulty tubing lot is the main mimic. Module 6.
Question 81Several patients develop severe itching and nausea during dialysis, and one has ventricular fibrillation. All were treated in one room that uses a temporary deionizer. What is the likely cause?
This matches the published outbreak in which a temporary deionizer released fluoride: 12 of 15 patients in one room became ill. Module 6.
Question 82What did the Caruaru outbreak in Brazil show?
Of 130 patients, 101 met the case definition of acute liver failure and 50 died. The water had not been treated. Module 6.
Question 83What does the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) test miss, according to Glorieux and colleagues?
About 1 in 10 pure or ultrapure samples still provoked a cytokine response. The clinical meaning is unknown. Module 6.
Question 84What does conductivity in dialysis fluid mainly reflect?
Conductivity rises with charged particles. It cannot detect bacteria, endotoxin, or uncharged toxins. Module 6.
Question 85Twenty minutes into the session, a patient complains of intense thirst, headache, and nausea, and the blood pressure has risen. The bicarbonate jug was changed before the start. What should you suspect first?
High sodium pulls water out of cells and raises thirst and pressure. Check the concentrate, the conductivity, and the machine. Module 6.
Question 86What did the factorial randomized trial by Asci and colleagues show about ultrapure dialysis fluid?
The hazard ratio was 0.90 with a confidence interval from 0.61 to 1.32. The subgroup finding needs confirmation. Module 6.
Question 87The meta-analysis by Susantitaphong and colleagues found that ultrapure fluid raised hemoglobin and albumin and lowered C-reactive protein. How should you describe these findings?
These are surrogates. The authors called for a large trial with hard outcomes. Module 6.
Question 88At 3 p.m., two patients in different rooms on the same water loop develop chills and fever within an hour of starting. Neither has a catheter. What is the best next step?
The same new symptom on one loop is a unit problem until proven otherwise. Early chills suggest endotoxin. Module 6.
Question 89In a cluster, 6 of 10 patients on machines that received a new concentrate batch are ill, and 0 of 9 on the old batch are. What does this comparison tell you?
An attack rate of 60% against 0% points to the batch. It guides testing; it does not prove the cause. Module 6.
Question 90At the start of your shift, the log shows no total chlorine result for today, and the first patients are about to start. What do you do?
The pre-shift chlorine test protects red cells. The policy states what happens when it is missing. Module 6.
Question 91Which change in the order raises small-solute removal without a ceiling set by the dialyzer?
Flow and dialyzer size have diminishing returns. Every minute adds dose. Module 7.
Question 92A man on thrice-weekly dialysis has a pre-dialysis potassium of 3.3 mmol/L after three days of vomiting. His order lists a very low potassium bath. What should you do?
A steep gradient would lower his potassium fast. The bath belongs to the nephrologist. Module 7.
Question 93What did Karnik and colleagues find about potassium baths in dialysis-unit cardiac arrests?
17.1% of arrest patients were on a 0 or 1 mEq/L bath, against 8.8% of the dialysis population. The study was observational. Module 7.
Question 94Why does digoxin make a low-potassium bath more dangerous?
Hypokalemia increases digoxin toxicity. This is established pharmacology. Module 7.
Question 95What did the MyTEMP trial show about cooler dialysis fluid?
In 84 centers and 15,413 patients, the adjusted hazard ratio was 1.00. Module 7.
Question 96What does Kt/V measure?
Kt/V is a surrogate for small-solute removal. It does not measure middle molecules, fluid, or nutrition. Module 7.
Question 97In the HEMO Study, what happened when the dose was raised above the standard?
A single-pool Kt/V of 1.71 against 1.32 gave a relative risk of death of 0.96, with a confidence interval crossing 1. Module 7.
Question 98Pre-dialysis BUN is 80 mg/dL and post-dialysis BUN is 24 mg/dL. What is the urea reduction ratio?
(80 − 24) ÷ 80 = 0.70, or 70%. Module 7.
Question 99A nurse draws the post-dialysis sample from the arterial line with the pump at full speed through a fistula with recirculation. How does this affect the result?
Cleaned blood lowers the post blood urea nitrogen (BUN), so the dose looks better. Module 7.
Question 100Why is equilibrated Kt/V lower than single-pool Kt/V?
Urea rebound from slower compartments raises blood urea after the session. Equilibrated Kt/V accounts for it. Module 7.
Question 101Mr. Ramos’s spKt/V has fallen from 1.45 to 1.15. His log shows full time and no alarms. On the adequacy day, a new nurse drew the post sample 45 minutes after the session ended. What is the most likely explanation?
A late draw catches rebound, so the dose looks low. Report it and ask whether to repeat. Module 7.
Question 102A patient lost 40 of 240 prescribed minutes in each session this month. Her usual spKt/V is 1.41. Using the simplified proportional estimate, what is her approximate spKt/V?
1.41 × 200 ÷ 240 ≈ 1.18. The real relation is not linear, so a measured value is still needed. Module 7.
Question 103What did the FHN Daily Trial show for six-times-weekly in-center dialysis?
Hazard ratios were 0.61 and 0.70 for the two composites. Left ventricular mass is a surrogate. Module 7.
Question 104In CONVINCE, death occurred in 17.3% with hemodiafiltration and 21.9% with hemodialysis over about 2.5 years. What is the approximate number needed to treat to prevent one death?
The absolute difference is 4.6 percentage points, and 1 ÷ 0.046 ≈ 22. This number was calculated from published proportions, not reported by the trial. Module 7.
Question 105Ms. Lim is on online hemodiafiltration. Her fistula has been difficult and her blood flow is well below the order. The machine shows a low convection volume each session. What should you do?
Convection volume depends on blood flow, and the access may be failing. Module 7.
Question 106Which statement best describes dry weight?
No device measures dry weight directly. It is a clinical estimate, and it must be revised when the patient gains or loses flesh. Module 8.
Question 107A consensus group described pre-dialysis volume overload as the sum of two parts. What are they?
The scale sees the gain. Overload left behind because the target is too high hides under the target. Module 8.
Question 108In the LUST auscultation substudy, how well did crackles reflect lung water on ultrasound?
Crackles shared about 12% of the variance with B-lines and edema about 4%. Their absence does not exclude lung water. Module 8.
Question 109A man arrives 3.2 kilograms above target for a 4-hour session. His unit adds 0.2 liters for the rinse-back and normalizes to a target weight of 64 kilograms. What is his UF rate?
Goal 3,400 mL. 3,400 ÷ (4 × 64) = 3,400 ÷ 256 = 13.3 mL/kg/h. The numbers are illustrative. Module 8.
Question 110What is the strongest evidence currently available that UF rates above 13 mL/kg/h are harmful?
The HEMO secondary analysis and a cohort of 118,394 patients found associations. No randomized trial has tested a ceiling. Module 8.
Question 111According to the Mermelstein cohort, how does a fixed ceiling in mL/kg/h behave across body sizes?
Risk levels followed 3W plus a constant, so the same risk sits at a higher mL/kg/h in a small patient and a lower one in a heavy patient. A fixed ceiling restricts small patients more and heavy patients less. Module 8.
Question 112Why does the blood pressure tend to fall late in a session in a patient near target?
Refilling depends on an expanded tissue space. Near true dry weight it slows, and removal outruns it. Module 8.
Question 113A nurse proposes removing the remaining 1.5 liters in the last hour because the patient was dizzy earlier. What is the problem with this plan?
The late session is when refilling is slowest. Fast removal there invites a sharp drop. Call the attending about the remaining goal. Module 8.
Question 114What did the LUST randomized trial show?
Congestion was relieved in 78% versus 56%, but the primary outcome did not differ (hazard ratio 0.88). Post hoc benefits are hypotheses. Module 8.
Question 115What was the main finding of the BISTRO trial?
The hazard ratio was 0.751 with a confidence interval crossing 1. The authors credit the standardized clinical protocol. Module 8.
Question 116In the DRIP trial, probing the dry weight down by about 1 kilogram did what?
DRIP showed a blood pressure benefit, a surrogate, over 8 weeks. It did not test hard outcomes. Module 8.
Question 117A woman has had cramps in the last hour of most sessions for three weeks. She is dizzy at home after dialysis, and her urine output has fallen. She has been eating well since a hospital stay. What is the most likely issue?
Weight gained as flesh makes the old target too low. Report the pattern; the attending decides. Module 8.
Question 118A man’s post-dialysis weights reach target every session, but his edema persists and his pre-dialysis pressure is climbing. He lost his appetite after pneumonia a month ago. What should you do?
Lost flesh may have been replaced by water. Proposing a review is your role; writing a new target is not. Module 8.
Question 119A relative asks you to lower her father’s target weight because his ankles are swollen. What do you do?
Any proposed change in target weight, from any source, goes to the attending. Edema has causes other than overload. Module 8.
Question 120A patient arrives 4.5 kilograms over target for a 3-hour session, and the rate would exceed your unit’s ceiling. What is the best action?
A goal that does not fit the ceiling is a prescription question. The attending chooses among the options. Module 8.
Question 121What is the main difference between a fistula and a graft?
In a fistula the patient’s own vein is needled. In a graft a synthetic tube connects artery and vein and is needled. Module 9.
Question 122Why is the thrill of a healthy access continuous?
The access is a low-resistance path, so flow and vibration continue through diastole. Module 9.
Question 123You feel a hard, bounding pulse where a soft thrill used to be. What is the most likely cause?
Blood enters at arterial pressure and cannot leave easily, so pressure rises before the narrowing. Module 9.
Question 124You raise the arm of a patient with a forearm fistula. The lower half stays full and the upper half flattens. What does this suggest?
A normal fistula flattens along its length. With an outflow narrowing, only the part beyond it drains. Module 9.
Question 125In the pulse augmentation test, the pulse near the join barely strengthens. What does this suggest?
With normal inflow the pulse strengthens clearly against the occluding finger. Module 9.
Question 126Mr. Ramos has a looped forearm graft. Before his session the nurse finds no thrill and no bruit. He feels well. What do you do first?
No thrill and no bruit mean thrombosis. A trial needle adds harm, and earlier treatment is associated with better results. Module 9.
Question 127Which statement about graft infection does the KDOQI guideline support?
A graft is foreign material, and bacteria that reach it are hard to clear. Module 9.
Question 128Mrs. Aquino has a bulge on her fistula. Today the skin over it is shiny, cannot be pinched up, and has a small scab. The thrill is normal. What is the best action?
Thin shiny skin and a scab mean a wall at risk of rupture. Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) advises emergency surgical assessment for erosion. Module 9.
Question 129Why does repeated needling in one small area raise concern?
In an observational study of 7,058 patients, area puncture was associated with a higher risk of access failure than rope-ladder needling. Module 9.
Question 130Mr. Santiago has diabetes and a new upper-arm fistula. The hand on that side is cold, numb, and pale. The thrill is strong. What is the most likely problem?

Question 438The power fails at 2:15 p.m. with ten patients on the machines. What do you do first?
Patients come before machines. Generator status is the second question. Module 30.
Question 439Why does the blood become harder to return the longer the power is off?
Moving blood resists clotting. A clotted circuit is not returned. Module 30.
Question 440During manual blood return without power, what replaces the venous air detector?
Without power the detector and clamp may not act. The operator’s eyes do their job. Module 30.
Question 441Mrs. Cruz’s session is being ended by hand after a power failure. The nurse sees foam in the venous line. What is right?
Air in the venous line means no return. Clamp and keep the circuit. Module 30.
Question 442Smoke enters the treatment area and the incident lead orders immediate evacuation. How are patients disconnected?
When the building must be left at once, there is no time to return the blood. Module 30.
Question 443Two patients in one room report back pain and chest tightness at 9:20 a.m.; one patient’s plasma looks pink. Both share a water loop. What do you do?
This is a water event until proven otherwise. Possible hemolysis means no return. Module 30.
Question 444Why can a substance harmless in drinking water poison a dialysis patient?
The patient meets a very large volume of fluid each session, separated from the blood by a thin membrane. Module 30.
Question 445Over two weeks, hemoglobin falls in many patients in one unit, with no change in medicines. What should you consider?
Anemia across a unit is a pattern. A chloramine outbreak showed exactly this. Module 30.
Question 446A monthly water test fails. Under Department of Health Administrative Order 2013-0003, what must the facility do?
The order requires a temporary stop, disinfection, and notification of patients. Module 30.
Question 447A water laboratory report shows a bacterial count of 150 CFU/mL in product water. How does it read?
The DOH order sets the regulatory limit at less than 200 CFU/mL, and testing laboratories report against it. The PSN standard gives a stricter target of less than 100 CFU/mL, which a unit may adopt in its water policy; the physician on duty does not choose. Module 30.
Question 448Only half the chairs can run tomorrow. Mr. Lim has no urine, last dialyzed four days ago, and had a high potassium. Mrs. Uy dialyzed two days ago and passes urine. What is best?
The longest gap, no residual function, and high potassium usually come first. The list and the nephrologist set the order. Module 30.
Question 449A patient kept away by a flood asks what to eat until the unit reopens. What do you give?
Numeric limits come from the unit dietitian and the nephrologist. Module 30.
Question 450Why does a unit review its response after a typhoon closure?
Disaster reviews name the after-event review as a core step that feeds back into the plan. Module 30.
Question 451Which list matches what DOH AO 2012-0001 expects of a physician on duty?
These are the order’s requirements. Module 31.
Question 452The PSN standard and the DOH order differ on a staffing point. How should the unit handle the difference?
The order is law. The standard is good practice. The Medical Director sets the unit’s rule, and it cannot fall below the order. Module 31.
Question 453At 6:30 p.m., a nurse hands you a medical certificate stating that a patient “completed dialysis today.” The patient left at hour two. What do you do?
Every statement on a certificate must be true. Module 31.
Question 454In Li v. Spouses Soliman, which element must a patient prove in an informed-consent claim?
Duty to disclose, failure to disclose, consent otherwise not given, and injury. Module 31.
Question 455Mr. Reyes, alert and oriented, wants to stop his session at hour one to attend a wake. His potassium this week was high. What comes first after you explain the risks?
A competent adult may refuse. The reason is often solvable. Module 31.
Question 456Which refusal note best holds up?
It has the time, explanation, the patient’s words, witnesses, call, and plan. Module 31.
Question 457A nurse posts a photo of a treatment sheet with the patient’s name in a staff group chat that includes a supplier. What do you do?
Report at once so the breach team has its full time to decide on notification. Module 31.
Question 458Under the Data Privacy Act, how is a dialysis patient’s hepatitis B status classed?
Health information is sensitive personal information. Module 31.
Question 459You forgot to write a note about a 2:15 p.m. episode and remember at 5:00 p.m. What is correct?
Inserting it to look contemporaneous makes the record untrue. Module 31.
Question 460A unit of 50 patients had 2 bloodstream infections in one month. What is the rate per 100 patient-months?
2 ÷ 50 × 100 = 4.0. Module 31.
Question 461Monthly catheter percentages for 12 months show the last six points all below the median. What does this mean?
Six or more consecutive points on one side of the median is a shift. Module 31.
Question 462A unit’s standardized hospitalization ratio is 1.2. Which statement is correct?
Observed divided by expected. The interval decides. Module 31.
Question 463What did the study of the U.S. End-Stage Renal Disease Quality Incentive Program show?
No association was found across 5,830 centers. Module 31.
Question 464After a medication omission, a review recommends “remind staff to check orders.” Why do critics call this a weak fix?
Reviews often settle for administrative fixes instead of changing system causes. Module 31.
Question 465A team wants to cut catheter infections. Which plan best fits a well-run PDSA cycle?
Small tests and data over time are what published Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) projects most often lack. Module 31.
Question 466In a descriptive report from four hemodialysis units, which medication error was most common?
Omission accounted for 24 of 35 medication errors. Module 32.
Question 467At 2 a.m. you order a medicine across the floor during an emergency. What makes this verbal order safe?
Read-back catches mishearing; writing makes it part of the record. Module 32.
Question 468Why should “units” be written in full on an order?
A misread U turns a dose into ten times the intended amount. Module 32.
Question 469A nurse asks you to lower a patient’s fluid goal for the rest of the session because his pressure keeps falling. What do you do?
The fluid goal belongs to the prescription. Module 32.
Question 470What did the I-PASS handoff study by Starmer and colleagues show?
Errors fell from 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions, in a before-after design. Module 32.
Question 471A patient with a left arm fistula is transferred for chest pain. Which letter item most directly protects the access?
A blood pressure cuff or an intravenous line on the fistula arm can damage or clot the access. Module 32.
Question 472A patient collapses in cardiac arrest at the chair. When do you call the attending nephrologist?
Calling yourself first would delay compressions. Module 15 has a team member call the attending nephrologist while you lead. Module 32.
Question 473What is a Critical step on a station scoring sheet?
Critical steps are gates, not points. Module 32.
Question 474A learner completes 11 of 12 steps (92%) at the hypotension station but does not screen for a dangerous cause, a Critical step. The unit’s standard is 80%. What is the result?
A missed Critical step means the station is not passed, whatever the percentage. Module 32.
Question 475What did the meta-analysis by Cook and colleagues find for simulation compared with no training?
Pooled effect sizes were about 1.1 to 1.2 for knowledge and skills and 0.50 for patient outcomes. Module 32.
Question 476What is the main limitation of the study by Barsuk and colleagues on central line infections?
The 74% reduction is an association within one site, not proof of cause. Module 32.
Question 477Four events arrive together: a coughing patient with foam in the venous line, a falling pressure, a feverish catheter patient, and a stable patient who wants to leave. Where do you go first?
Possible air embolism is an immediate threat to life. Module 32.
Question 478In the same shift, for which patient must the blood not be returned?
Returning blood from a line that may contain air could send more air into the patient. Module 32.
Question 479A colleague scores 95% on the written test. What does this show?
Entrustment is a separate judgment by people who watched the work. Module 32.
Question 480An in situ practice code in your unit finds that the suction unit on the emergency cart does not work. How should you view this?
Finding latent safety threats is one purpose of in situ simulation. Module 32.
