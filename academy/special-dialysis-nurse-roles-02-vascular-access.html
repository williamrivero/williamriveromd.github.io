Role subtrack for registered nurses

This subtrack prepares Philippine registered nurses who are assigned a special portfolio in a hemodialysis unit: vascular access, infection prevention, education, quality, safety, coordination, anemia, transplant liaison, records, water and equipment liaison, or adequacy and outcomes. It teaches assessment, coordination, teaching and escalation within nursing scope. Prescriptions, dose changes, access procedures, transplant listing, water-system repair and technical release belong to the physician, the authorized team and the technical staff. All cases are synthetic.

Learning objectives · Special Dialysis Nurse Roles (SDNR)-01 · about 10 hours · week 1

After this module you should be able to

  • Explain fistula maturation, outflow stenosis, steal and thrombosis as a causal chain from vessel to bedside finding.
  • Sort access findings into act now, today and this week, and route each to the right decision-maker.
  • Match cannulation difficulty to demonstrated cannulator skill and stop after a failed attempt limit.
  • Run an access pathway and referral tracker, and audit outcomes without creating incentives for unnecessary procedures.

Time plan. 3 h study · 2 h workshop · 5 h supervised practice. The supervised hours are arranged by your unit and recorded in its own competency system; this page records self-study only.

Intuition

A river that must keep flowing

Think of an arteriovenous fistula as a river dug by a surgeon. An artery, which carries blood at high pressure, is joined to a vein, which normally carries blood at low pressure. The vein is suddenly asked to carry several hundred milliliters a minute. Over weeks it widens and its wall thickens, the way a riverbank shapes itself to a stronger current. That is maturation. When the river is healthy you can feel it as a soft, continuous buzz under your fingers, the thrill, and hear it as a low continuous rush, the bruit.

The picture has a limit. A river is pushed by gravity; a fistula is pushed by the heart, and it can steal blood from the hand beyond it. A river silts up slowly and visibly; a fistula narrows silently, usually where the vein turns or meets a valve or where needles enter again and again, and it can clot in an hour. Your work as access nurse is to read the river every session and to make sure that a change reaches the person who can act before the river stops.

In plain wordsThe fistula is the patient's lifeline. You are its daily inspector and its coordinator. You do not repair it, but you make sure the repair happens in time.

Most access problems announce themselves days or weeks ahead: harder cannulation, longer bleeding after the needles come out, a pulse that becomes hammering, a swollen arm. A few are emergencies: a fistula with no thrill, a cold painful hand, bleeding that will not stop, a spreading red infection. Your work is to catch the first group early and never to cannulate through the second.

Physiology

From vessel to bedside finding

Maturation. When the vein receives arterial flow, the shear stress on its inner lining rises. The endothelium responds by releasing nitric oxide and other signals, the vein dilates, and its wall remodels. A usable fistula needs enough flow, a wide enough vein, and a vein shallow enough to reach with a needle. A common teaching rule of thumb ("rule of sixes": flow above about 600 mL/min, diameter about 6 mm, depth under about 6 mm, by about 6 weeks) is a rough guide only; many fistulas mature on a different timeline, and the decision to use a new fistula belongs to the nephrologist and surgeon.

Stenosis. The same remodeling can overshoot. Smooth-muscle cells grow inward at turbulent points, especially near the joining point and in the outflow vein, and the lumen narrows (neointimal hyperplasia). What you see depends on where the narrowing is:

Where it narrowsWhat happens to the riverWhat you may notice
Outflow (vein beyond the needles)Blood backs up; pressure in the access risesHammering pulse instead of a soft thrill; fistula stays full when the arm is raised; prolonged bleeding after needle removal; high venous pressure readings; arm swelling
Inflow (artery or near the joint)Too little blood entersWeak, flat fistula; collapses easily; poor arterial pressure and blood-flow alarms; difficult cannulation
Central veins (chest)Whole-arm drainage is blockedSwollen arm, face or breast on that side; visible chest-wall veins; often a history of catheters on that side

Recirculation. When outflow is narrowed or needles are too close or reversed, cleaned blood returning through the venous needle is pulled straight back into the arterial needle. The machine cleans the same blood twice, and the patient receives less treatment than the screen suggests (Module 13).

Steal. The fistula is a low-resistance shortcut. In some patients, especially older people with diabetes or arterial disease, too much blood takes the shortcut and the hand beyond it is starved. Mild coolness is common; pain, numbness, pallor, ulcers or a cold, painful hand during dialysis are not. This is ischemia, and nerves are damaged within hours.

Thrombosis. A narrowed access with slow flow can clot, often after a long hypotensive session or overnight. The thrill and bruit disappear. Salvage by thrombectomy or angioplasty is time-sensitive, which is why "no thrill this morning" is a same-day problem, not a next-clinic problem.

RememberLook, listen and feel before every cannulation. A change from the patient's own baseline matters more than any single finding.
Evidence

Why access type matters, and what surveillance can and cannot do

Catheters carry the most risk. A systematic review of 62 cohort studies with 586,337 participants found that, compared with people dialyzing through a fistula, people using a central venous catheter had a higher risk of death (relative risk 1.53), of fatal infection (2.12) and of cardiovascular events (1.38). Grafts sat in between.1 These are observational data with a high risk of selection bias: sicker people are more likely to be on catheters. The review shows that catheters are associated with worse outcomes; it does not prove that switching any individual to a fistula would save that person's life.

Plan the whole life, not just the next access. The 2019 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) vascular access guideline replaced the old "fistula first" slogan with a patient-centered end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) Life-Plan: each patient has a plan that considers their goals, life expectancy, vessels, likely future modalities (peritoneal dialysis, transplant) and the access sequence that keeps future options open.2 For a 30-year-old awaiting a living-donor transplant, a fistula may be worth months of preparation; for a frail 85-year-old with poor veins, a graft or even a catheter may be the right choice. The nurse's job is to make sure the plan exists, is written, and is being followed.

Surveillance has limits. Clinical monitoring means the physical examination and the treatment signals you see every session. Surveillance means device measurements such as access flow. A meta-analysis of 14 randomized trials (1,390 participants) found that finding and correcting stenosis early, before the access failed, lowered the risk of thrombosis (relative risk 0.79) but did not clearly reduce loss of the access (0.81, confidence interval 0.65 to 1.02). Effects looked larger in fistulas than in grafts, but the trials were small.3 In practice, this means a well-done clinical examination, recorded every session and acted on when it changes, is the backbone; device surveillance, where available, adds information but is not a reason for a procedure by itself.

Cannulation technique. In buttonhole cannulation, blunt needles enter the same track every time; in rope-ladder cannulation, sites rotate along the vein. Systematic reviews have found buttonhole cannulation associated with more access infections than rope-ladder cannulation.4,5 Where a unit uses buttonholes, strict scab removal and site disinfection are not optional.

How certain are we?Strong: catheters are associated with more infection and death (consistent observational data). Moderate: early stenosis correction reduces clotting, not clearly access loss (small randomized trials). Practice point: examine every session and act on change.
Practice

Turn findings into timely action

Most of the harm in access care comes from good findings that reach the decision-maker too late. Agree three urgency tiers with your nephrologist and write them into your unit's access pathway. The examples below are typical; your unit's protocol decides the details.

TierExamplesWhat the nurse does
Act now (before cannulation or immediately)No thrill or bruit; cold, pale, painful or numb hand; bleeding that will not stop with pressure; rapidly enlarging swelling; spreading redness with fever or pus; aneurysm with thin shiny skin or ulcerationDo not cannulate. Apply pressure for bleeding. Notify the physician at once. Arrange same-day surgical or interventional review through the physician. Document times.
TodayNew hammering pulse with fistula staying full on arm elevation; prolonged bleeding after needles on two or more sessions; repeated high venous pressure or low blood flow; new arm swellingSituation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (SBAR) to the physician this shift. Ask for the decision and the referral to be written; record the owner and due date.
This weekGradually harder cannulation; slow maturation of a new fistula; catheter-dependent patient without a dated access planAdd to the referral tracker, raise at the access meeting, confirm a dated plan.
Station 4, 6:40 a.m. · fictional

Mang Tonyo, 68, has diabetes and a left forearm fistula. His last two cannulations were difficult. This morning Nurse Aileen feels no thrill and hears no bruit. His left hand is cooler than his right, and he says it has ached since last night. His surgical follow-up is in three weeks.

Aileen does not cannulate. She checks capillary refill and sensation in both hands, records the times, and calls Dr. Reyes: "Situation: Mang Tonyo's left forearm fistula has no thrill or bruit, and his left hand is cool and painful since last night. Background: two difficult cannulations this week, follow-up in three weeks. Assessment: I think the fistula may have clotted, and I'm concerned about his hand. Recommendation: please review now; he needs a same-day surgical decision and a plan for today's dialysis."

What this shows. No thrill plus a symptomatic hand is an act-now finding. The three-week appointment is irrelevant to today's decision.

The bleeding that kept getting longer · fictional

Nurse Ivy tells Aileen that Aling Rosa's needle sites have bled for 25, 30 and 35 minutes after her last three sessions. Her fistula now feels pulsatile rather than buzzing, and it stays full when she raises her arm.

Aileen recognizes an outflow pattern. She sends an SBAR to Dr. Reyes that shift, who orders a fistulogram referral. Aileen writes it on the tracker with the date sent, the receiving unit and a check date.

What this shows. A trend across sessions is the signal. One long bleed is an event; three are a pattern.

Practice

Match the access to the cannulator

Not all accesses are equally easy, and not all nurses are equally skilled. Matching the two prevents most infiltrations and lost fistulas. Keep an access map for each patient: a simple drawing of the arm showing the arterial-to-venous direction, the usable segments, sites to avoid (aneurysms, recent infiltration, thin skin), the cannulation method, and needle direction. Update it when anything changes.

StandardMature, straight, superficial segment; any signed-off cannulator.
ComplexNew fistula on first uses, deep or tortuous vein, short segment, recent infiltration; only cannulators the unit has signed off for complex access.
Not by nurses without separate privilegeUltrasound-guided cannulation, catheter insertion or removal; only with a formal training pathway approved by the clinical head.

The attempt limit. Agree a maximum number of attempts per nurse and per session (many units use two per nurse) before handing over to a more experienced cannulator or calling the physician. Each failed attempt raises the risk of hematoma, infiltration and a lost access. Record every attempt, not just the successful one; the access nurse reviews these as a pattern.

StopA swelling that appears during cannulation or when the pump starts means infiltration. Stop the pump, remove or reposition as your protocol says, apply gentle pressure, and do not keep running through a swelling arm.

Catheters. For patients with a central venous catheter (CVC), your portfolio adds two jobs: making sure every catheter patient has a dated plan for a permanent access or a documented reason why not, and checking catheter care against the unit's aseptic procedure (Module 3).

Coordination

Review outcomes and close the loop

The access referral tracker is the heart of this portfolio. It is a list, kept in the unit's approved system, of every open access action: what was found, what was requested, who was asked, when, the receiving service, the date the plan came back, and the date it was carried out. It answers the question every patient's family eventually asks: "We were told he needed a procedure. What happened?"

  1. FindingDated, with tier.
  2. RequestPhysician decision and written referral.
  3. ReceiptReceiving service confirms.
  4. ProcedureDate done, or reason not done.
  5. Returned planReport back in the chart; access map updated.
  6. ClosedPatient told; first cannulation after the procedure supervised.

Measure without creating bad incentives. Useful measures include the share of referrals completed within the agreed time for their tier, the percentage of patients dialyzing with a catheter (with the reason recorded), infiltrations per 1,000 cannulations, and the patient's own experience of cannulation. Avoid measures that reward procedures for their own sake, such as "number of angioplasties ordered." An access nurse who sends every patient for imaging will improve a process number while adding cost, contrast exposure and missed sessions.

Worked example · access ratesOver three months, the unit's 60 patients had 4 catheter-related bloodstream infections over 54 catheter patient-months and none over 192 fistula and graft patient-months. Catheter rate: 4 / 54 × 100 = 7.4 per 100 patient-months. Fistula and graft rate: 0. All-access rate: 4 / 246 × 100 = 1.6. Reporting only 1.6 would hide that every infection came from catheters. The Portfolio Toolkit (tool 2) and the Access Infection Rate calculator show these separately.
Local practice

In a Philippine unit

Many Filipino patients start hemodialysis urgently through a catheter, and the wait for a surgeon, a fistulogram or an angioplasty can be long and expensive, especially outside Metro Manila. That makes the coordinator's work more valuable, not less. Practical steps that units find useful: a shared list of vascular surgeons and interventional services with their real waiting times and costs; a standard referral letter with the access history and map attached; a contact person at each receiving hospital; and a habit of telling the patient and family, in plain words, what was found and what happens next. Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) coverage for access procedures follows current circulars and the facility's accreditation, which change; check with the unit's PhilHealth liaison before telling a family what will be covered.

Vein preservation starts long before dialysis. Patients with advanced chronic kidney disease should be told to protect the arm planned for access: no blood pressure cuffs, drips or blood draws there if it can be avoided. Teaching this in the chronic kidney disease (CKD) clinic is part of the ESKD Life-Plan.2

Practice case · synthetic

Case: The fistula that went quiet

Thursday, 6:30 a.m., station 7. Mr. Arnel Santos, 54, has diabetes and a right upper-arm fistula created eight months ago. Last session his blood-flow alarms sounded repeatedly and the nurse needed three attempts to cannulate. He dialyzed for 3 hours 20 minutes of a 4-hour prescription.

This morning you feel a faint, pulsatile thrill near the joining point that disappears 4 centimeters up the arm. The bruit is short and high-pitched. His right hand is warm with normal sensation. He is anxious: "Ma'am, my last access clotted after something like this. I can't lose another one."

The vascular surgeon's next clinic is in 12 days. The charge nurse says, "Just cannulate below the weak part and run him slower."

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, whom you would contact and what you would say, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the debrief below.

Assess

Compare with his access map and recent sessions: weak segment, high-pitched short bruit and loss of thrill suggest a significant narrowing with impending thrombosis. Hand perfusion is normal, so this is not steal. Note the delivered time shortfall.

Communicate

Do not proceed on the charge nurse's suggestion alone. SBAR to the nephrologist now with the findings, last session's alarms, attempts and short treatment; request a decision on today's cannulation and an urgent (today or tomorrow) interventional referral rather than the 12-day clinic.

Teach

Tell Mr. Santos what you found, what you are doing, and what to do tonight: check the thrill morning and evening and call if it disappears or the arm swells or hurts.

Escalate

If the physician cannot be reached, follow the unit's escalation route to the clinical head. Enter the referral on the tracker with owner and check date; supervise the first cannulation after any procedure.
How it played out · fictional

Dr. Reyes reviewed him at 7:00, allowed cannulation of the lower segment by the most experienced nurse with a lower blood-flow target for that session, and arranged an angioplasty the next day. Two days later the thrill was continuous again. Aileen updated the access map and closed the tracker entry after the first supervised cannulation.

Documentation task. Write the tracker entry for this case (finding, tier, request, receiving service, owner, check date) and the SBAR you would have used, in no more than six lines each.
Second round · what changesTwo weeks later Mr. Santos's right hand is pale and painful during the second hour of dialysis and improves after he goes home. His thrill is now strong.
Submit: An act-now SBAR that names possible steal, what you checked (color, temperature, capillary refill, sensation, both hands), and the decision you are asking for.

Supervised practice

With your preceptor

Use synthetic or de-identified examples, or observe with the patient's permission. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own protocols, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record.

  • Perform and record look, listen and feel on ten accesses, including arm elevation, and compare with the preceptor's findings.
  • Draw and update access maps for five patients.
  • Sort twelve written access findings into act now, today and this week, and justify each.
  • Run the referral tracker for one month; present open items with owners at the access meeting.
  • Observe complex cannulations and record attempts against the unit's attempt limit.
