Study series for dialysis technicians

This module is study material for dialysis technicians. It explains why safe practice works the way it does. It does not replace the physician’s orders, the supervising nurse’s direction, your unit’s approved procedures, or the manufacturer’s instructions for use. What a technician may do differs between countries and between facilities, so do only the tasks you are trained, assigned, and signed off to do. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Verify the patient’s identity, the current treatment order, and the pre-treatment measurements, and escalate any discrepancy before the affected setup goes ahead.
  • Describe the monitoring you are assigned during treatment, and report clinical or equipment changes promptly.
  • Explain your assigned responsibilities at the end of treatment and during station turnover.
  • Record observations, changes, and notifications, and give a structured handoff with check-back.

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsEvery dialysis session has the same three parts, like a jeepney trip: boarding, the ride, and getting off. Before, you make sure that the right patient, the right order, and a ready machine have come together. During, you watch the patient and the machine and report changes. After, you record what happened, hand over, and reset the station.

The picture has a limit. A trip you take every day invites shortcuts, and a dialysis session does not forgive them. Most errors happen when a step is skipped because the shift is busy. The sequence below exists so that a busy shift and a quiet one are run the same way.

Figure 1 · One session at a glance

  1. ArriveIdentify the patient with two identifiers.
  2. MeasureWeight and the assigned vital signs. Ask about new symptoms.
  3. VerifyThe order, the dialyzer, the dialysate, and the machine checks.
  4. TreatObservations on schedule. The access and lines stay in view.
  5. CompleteThe assigned measurements and records.
  6. Turn overClear, clean, disinfect, and release the station.

Vital signsBlood pressure, pulse, temperature, and breathing rate.

Opening case · fictional

Case: An unexpected pre-treatment weight

It is Friday morning. Ms. Rosario Ramos, 66, steps on the scale before her session, and it reads 71.8 kg. Her recent comparable pre-treatment weights were 68.2–69.0 kg, and the chart shows her last post-treatment weight as 67.9 kg. You check her identity, the scale units, and the measurement conditions, and weigh her again on the assigned scale. It again reads 71.8 kg. Ms. Ramos says she feels well and has no new symptoms. The current order needs the nurse’s verification before setup continues.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What does the repeat measurement confirm, and what does it leave unanswered?
  • How will you report the finding to the dialysis nurse?
  • Which decisions belong to the authorized clinical staff, and not to you?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

One session, three phases

Think of a session as a jeepney trip. Before the jeep moves, the right passengers board and the route is confirmed. During the ride, someone watches the road and passengers call out when something is wrong. At the end, everyone gets off safely, and the seat is cleaned before the next passenger. The picture has a limit. On a jeepney one driver decides everything. In a dialysis unit the decisions are divided, and knowing which ones are yours is half of the work.

The session starts with identification and readiness checks, continues with monitoring during treatment, and ends with a completion review, a handoff, and safe station turnover. Philippine dialysis facility standards and the unit’s job descriptions set who is assigned to what and under whose supervision. The clinical team verifies the order, assesses the patient, decides whether treatment can go ahead, and handles any change in the prescription or in the patient’s condition. As a trainee, you practice the workflow in simulation and perform only verified, authorized tasks under supervision.1,2

PhaseTechnician’s assigned workSupervising nurse and clinical team
Pre-treatmentCheck identifiers, confirm the assigned measurements and that equipment is ready, and report any difference.Assess the patient and access, reconcile the current order, and authorize the next steps.
IntradialyticMake and record the assigned observations, and report symptoms, trends, hazards, or machine messages.Evaluate each concern, direct the patient’s care, and confirm any change to the treatment plan.
Post-treatmentTake the assigned observations and measurements when it is appropriate, give the handoff, and complete turnover.Judge whether treatment is complete and the patient is stable, and decide any follow-up or discharge needs.
Lesson · part 2

Pre-treatment · Verify before relying on the record

A record is only as good as its match to the person in the chair. Each step below closes one way the match can fail.

  1. Identify the patient. Match two facility-approved identifiers that belong only to this person against the record. Ask the patient to state them when possible, and use the approved alternative procedure when they cannot. A chair or station number tells you a location, not a person.
  2. Verify readiness. Within your assignment, check the station, the equipment, and any required release status. Confirm through the unit’s verification process that the patient record, the current order, and the approved setup agree. Time, dialyzer, dialysate, and fluid removal are examples of settings that may need verification.
  3. Collect assigned observations. Take the current weight and the assigned vital signs with approved technique. Ask about new symptoms and note what has changed. When a measurement is unexpected, confirm the patient’s identity, the units, the type of measurement, the equipment, and the conditions. Repeat it as directed, and report both the first and the repeat results under the unit procedure.
  4. Hand off exceptions. Tell the supervising nurse promptly about any unresolved concern with the order, weight, symptoms, access, or equipment. Pause the affected setup until you are directed. Do not copy a previous treatment’s settings into today’s session without verification.

The pre-treatment weight is an observation. Comparing it with the last post-treatment weight helps the clinical team judge how much fluid has been gained. The prescribed ultrafiltration (UF) goal reflects the authorized plan for today’s session.3,4

How to report it. Give the nurse the first and repeat readings, their times, the earlier weights, what Ms. Ramos said about her symptoms, and the checks you made. Then make a clear request: ask the nurse to assess the discrepancy and to verify the current order before setup continues. The cause and the fluid removal plan belong to the clinical team.

RememberNever copy the last session’s settings into today’s treatment. Orders change, and the patient in front of you may not weigh the same, or may not even be the same patient.
Lesson · part 3

Intradialytic · Compare readings with what you see

Follow the facility’s observation schedule and your authorized role. At each assigned check, note what the patient says and how the patient looks, the vital signs, whether the access and lines are in view, and the machine readings or messages. Compare each with the earlier observations, and note the time and how far into the session you are. A worrying change needs a spoken report now, even if the charting is not yet due.1,5

ObservationAction within assigned scope
New dizziness or nausea, especially with a falling blood pressureTell the dialysis nurse promptly, report the symptom and the readings, and follow the nurse’s directions.
Chest pain, severe breathlessness, unresponsiveness, heavy bleeding or visible dislodgementCall for help at once and activate the unit emergency pathway as required. Report the specific signs and where they are.
Newly changed machine reading or alarmLook at the patient and the circuit from your assigned position, tell the nurse, and report the exact message. Follow the machine model’s and the unit’s procedures. Leave every protective function in place.
Unexplained timing, undocumented change or interruptionBring it to the nurse’s attention, and record the actual events and times in the approved record.

Example. If Ms. Ramos later says she feels dizzy, tell the nurse when it began, her exact words, the assigned readings, and any change you can see. Do not try to explain the cause beyond your role. Dialysis pressure alarms can miss some blood loss outside the circuit, so keep the access in view as directed, and report what you see whether or not an alarm has sounded.4,5

Lesson · in practice

A chairside guide to machine alarms

In plain wordsThink of a doorbell. It tells you that someone is at the door. It does not tell you who it is, or whether they are welcome. A machine alarm is the same: it tells you which sensor noticed a change, not what caused it and not whether the patient is safe. It is the machine asking a person to come and look.

The picture has a limit. You can ignore a doorbell. You cannot ignore an alarm, and you cannot answer it from across the room. The order is always the same: patient first, access and lines second, machine screen third.

Figure 10 · Every alarm, every time

  1. Look at the patientAwake? Breathing comfortably? In pain? Bleeding?
  2. Look at the access and linesNeedles or catheter in place, connections tight, no kinks, no blood on the sheet or the floor.
  3. Read the alarmSay the exact words on the screen.
  4. Mute, do not reset blindlyMuting the sound gives you time. Resetting before you find the cause restarts the hazard.
  5. Call the nurseAt once for any alarm on the list of urgent alarms below, and for any alarm you cannot explain.
  6. RecordThe time, the alarm, what was found, what was done, and by whom.

Lesson · part 4

Completion, turnover and handoff

  • Completion. Follow the nurse’s directions and your assigned unit steps. Handling the patient’s access, returning blood, disconnecting, and other clinical tasks belong to authorized staff with validated competencies. This module rehearses observation, measurement, and communication. It does not teach those procedures.
  • Patient status. Report new symptoms, bleeding that has not stopped, or difficulty standing. Take the assigned post-treatment observations only when it is appropriate and safe to do so. The nurse confirms the patient’s condition and whether the patient may leave.
  • Turnover. After the patient leaves, follow Module 5 and the approved checklist. Send used equipment and waste by the correct route. Confirm cleaning, disinfection, contact time, and station release before you lay out clean supplies for the next patient.
  • Handoff. Report where the treatment stands, the relevant readings and trends, any events or interruptions, what was done, who has been told, and what follow-up is still open. Confirm who will take the next step.1,6,7
  • Situation. “Ms. Ramos’s measured pre-treatment weight is higher than her recent trend. I have paused setup.”
  • Background. “Her last post-treatment weight was 67.9 kg. Her recent pre-treatment weights were 68.2 to 69.0 kg.”
  • Assessment within role. “Today’s weight and the repeat on the assigned scale were both 71.8 kg. I confirmed her identifiers, the scale units, and the measurement conditions. She reports no new symptoms.”
  • Request. “Please assess the discrepancy and confirm the current order before setup continues.”

Notice what the handoff contains: the numbers with units, the checks already made, what has been paused, and one clear request. Use check-back to confirm the nurse’s instructions. Record the measured readings with units and times, the notification, and the directions you confirmed. Keep entries factual. Use the unit’s correction process; never replace an original entry without an audit trail. An incident report, when one is required, follows a separate facility process.8

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

Venous pressure alarm

What you see
In the second hour, the venous pressure alarm sounds at station 5. The patient looks comfortable and is watching television.
What is likely going on
Often the patient has bent the access arm or is lying on the line. Sometimes the needle has moved, or a clot is forming.
What to do
Look at the patient, the access, and the line from the needle to the machine. If a kink is obvious and correcting it is one of your assigned tasks, correct it; otherwise call the nurse. Never reset first and look later.
Why it matters
The alarm tells you where to look. The cause may be harmless, or it may be the start of blood loss.

“I feel hot and I am yawning.”

What you see
Two hours into treatment, a patient yawns again and again, says she feels warm, and looks pale.
What is likely going on
These are common early signs of a falling blood pressure.
What to do
Call the nurse now and stay with the patient. Do not change the machine settings.
Why it matters
Acting at the yawning stage prevents a collapse a few minutes later.

A weight that cannot be right

What you see
At 07:00 the scale shows a patient 6 kg heavier than three days ago.
What is likely going on
The patient may be holding a bag, wearing boots, or sitting in a different wheelchair. Or the weight may be real.
What to do
Check identity, remove the extras, weigh again as directed, and report both readings.
Why it matters
A wrong weight entered into the record can lead to far too much fluid being removed.

Yesterday’s settings

What you see
On Wednesday morning a coworker suggests copying Monday’s treatment settings because “her orders never change.”
What is likely going on
Orders do change, and the saved settings may belong to a different patient.
What to do
Verify today’s order with the nurse before anything is entered.
Why it matters
Errors from copying old settings forward are a known cause of wrong-treatment events.
Field notes
  • On every round, look at the patient first, then the access, then the lines, then the machine, then the record.
  • Chart at the time you take the reading. Numbers written from memory at the end of the shift are guesses.
  • A patient who has gone quiet or sleepy needs a closer look, not less attention.
  • End every handoff with what is still open and who owns it.
Case review

Back to the case: An unexpected pre-treatment weight

Case debrief. What the repeat confirms. The repeat shows that today’s pre-treatment weight is reproducible under the conditions you checked. It does not tell you why her weight is higher, and it does not authorize any change to fluid removal. The measured difference from her last post-treatment weight is 3.9 kg.

🚨 Emergency quick card: Blood leak alarm. One screen each, saved for use with no signal. Print a copy for the crash cart.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Simulated session and exception handoff

Materials: Mock treatment order, weight record, approved setup and turnover checklists, simulated vital signs, and a mock treatment record. The observer introduces a pre-weight difference and, later, a symptom during treatment. Use a model or an inactive station. Rate each step satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • CriticalMatches two approved patient identifiers and the correct record.
  • Verifies pre/post timing, measurement conditions, units and the recent weight trend.
  • CriticalReports the persistent difference and holds the affected setup pending clinical verification.
  • Identifies assigned checks during treatment and reports changes with times and values.
  • CriticalAlerts the nurse promptly to the simulated concerning symptom and explains the emergency route.
  • Identifies assigned post-session observations and reports unresolved concerns before patient departure.
  • Verifies turnover, wet contact time and station clearance before new supplies are staged.
  • Gives a concise handoff with next-step ownership and repeats instructions for check-back.
  • Documents objective facts, observations, notifications and the agreed action.
  • CriticalIn a simulated alarm, looks at the patient and access first, reads the alarm aloud, and calls the nurse without resetting blindly.
Placement activity. Under supervision, watch how the unit verifies readiness, handles a discrepancy, and records the follow-up. Write down one example of check-back, or complete an equivalent simulation. Use approved de-identified information.
Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 5 questions at random from this module’s 20-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 4 of 5 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 20Which is an appropriate pair for verifying identity before dialysis when approved by the facility?
Use two person-specific identifiers and match them to the order and care record. The station and schedule identify a location and time.
Question 2 of 20Which source defines the treatment parameters to be verified before the session?
The current order is the source of the prescribed treatment. Verify any discrepancy through the authorized clinician.
Question 3 of 20Today’s repeated pre-treatment weight differs markedly from the patient’s recent trend. What does a consistent repeat measurement establish?
Confirm scale, units, patient identity and measurement conditions as assigned. The verified current weight is a data point for clinical review, not a new prescription.
Question 4 of 20You confirm a verified pre-treatment weight remains unexpectedly high. What should you do before applying treatment settings?
The nurse or authorized clinician reviews the unexpected result and confirms the treatment plan. A technician reports the measurement and avoids independent changes.
Question 5 of 20A medication listed on a previous record is absent from today’s order. Someone asks you to prepare it as usual. What should you do?
Medication preparation, administration and changes depend on a verified order, legal scope and facility authorization.
Question 6 of 20What is the term for observations made while the dialysis treatment is running?
Intradialytic observations are made during treatment, such as assigned vital signs, symptoms and equipment or access observations.
Question 7 of 20Why record the actual time of an assigned vital sign and the treatment phase?
Timing gives the value clinical context and supports comparison across the treatment record.
Question 8 of 20The machine has not alarmed. The patient reports dizziness and appears pale. Which interpretation is most appropriate?
Machine alarms respond to monitored device conditions. They cannot replace patient observation or timely reporting of symptoms.
Question 9 of 20A patient becomes lightheaded and an assigned blood pressure reading is markedly lower than the initial value. What should you do?
Symptoms with a substantial blood pressure change warrant prompt nursing assessment. Relay facts and follow authorized instructions.
Question 10 of 20You are authorized to assist with specimen checks. A specimen label has the correct name but a different record number. What should you do?
A mismatched identifier makes the specimen identity uncertain. Reconcile it through policy before sending or relying on it.
Question 11 of 20Which measurements should be distinguished when documenting a session?
The pre- and post-treatment weights are measurements. The target is a clinical goal and should remain identifiable as such.
Question 12 of 20A session ended early. Why should the record show the actual treatment duration and the reason reported to the nurse?
The difference between planned and delivered treatment is relevant to clinical review, continuity and documentation.
Question 13 of 20The patient has left and visible soil was removed. The routine disinfectant wet contact time is still underway. Is the station ready?
The station is released only after the approved cleaning and disinfection requirements are completed.
Question 14 of 20After disconnecting, the patient reports dizziness while seated. The next patient has arrived. What should you do?
A new symptom requires nursing assessment. Patient safety takes priority and routine turnover waits until the station is vacated safely.
Question 15 of 20Which end-of-treatment handoff is most useful?
State what happened, when, who responded and what remains outstanding. The receiving clinician can confirm the pending action.
Question 16 of 20An alarm sounds. What do you look at first?
Patient first, access and lines second, screen third.
Question 17 of 20The venous pressure reading drops suddenly during treatment. What must you do at once?
A fall in venous pressure can mean the line has separated or the needle has come out.
Question 18 of 20The air detector alarms and the venous clamp closes. Which action is correct?
The clamp is what protects the patient from air.
Question 19 of 20Three machines alarm for low water supply at the same time. What does that suggest?
One machine or several is the question that separates a machine fault from a supply fault.
Question 20 of 20A venous pressure alarm keeps repeating. A colleague suggests widening the limits. Why is that unsafe?
Limits are a protection; the cause of the alarm has to be found.
