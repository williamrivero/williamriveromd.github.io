Study series for physicians on duty

This module is study material for physicians on duty in hemodialysis units. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol. At the chairside, follow your unit’s protocol and ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Doses and cut-off values are set by your unit. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Define dry weight (target weight) and explain why it is a clinical estimate that drifts with illness, appetite, and muscle loss.
  • Perform a bedside volume assessment and state which signs are unreliable in dialysis patients.
  • Calculate the session fluid goal, the hourly ultrafiltration rate per kilogram, and the minimum session time that keeps the rate within your unit’s ceiling.
  • Explain plasma refilling and why removing fluid faster than the tissues can refill drops the blood pressure.
  • Describe lung ultrasound and bioimpedance in outline, and summarize what randomized trials of each have and have not shown.
  • Recognize the clues that a target weight is set too high or too low, and escalate them with a structured report.

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsPicture a water tank with a fill line painted on its side. Every session, a drain empties the tank down to that line. The target weight is the line, and the drain is the dialysis machine.

The picture has a limit. Nobody can see the line. It is drawn by clinical judgment and redrawn when the patient changes, and the tank itself changes size as the patient loses or gains flesh. Dry weight is an estimate that drifts, not a fixed property of the patient.

Figure 1 · Key terms for this module

Dry weight (target weight)The lowest weight after dialysis that the patient tolerates without symptoms or low pressure, and without signs of overload between sessions. It is a clinical estimate.
Interdialytic weight gain (IDWG)The weight gained between the end of one session and the start of the next. It is mostly the salt and water taken in.
Ultrafiltration (UF)Removal of water from the blood by pressure across the dialyzer membrane.
UF rateThe volume removed per hour, usually divided by body weight and written in milliliters per kilogram per hour (mL/kg/h).
Plasma refillingMovement of fluid from the tissue spaces back into the blood vessels while the machine removes water from the blood.
Extracellular waterThe water outside the cells: plasma plus the fluid between the cells. Overload lives here.
B-linesVertical bright streaks on lung ultrasound that appear when lung tissue holds extra water.
Bioimpedance spectroscopyA bedside test that passes a tiny current through the body to estimate extracellular water.

Most sessions run on a fluid goal that fits the patient and the booked time. A few do not: the goal is too large for the hours booked, or the patient has changed and the target has not. Your work is to check the arithmetic, notice the mismatch, and bring it to the attending nephrologist with numbers. The target belongs to the prescription, not to you.

Opening case · fictional

Case: Four kilos over, three hours booked

Monday, 6:30 a.m., the first shift after the long weekend. Mang Teodoro “Teddy” Villanueva, 58, has diabetes and has been on dialysis for three years through a left forearm fistula. Nurse Liza Ramos tells you that his transport must leave early today, so the session is booked for 3 hours instead of his usual 4.

His pre-dialysis weight is 68.4 kilograms. The target weight on the order is 64.5 kilograms. His blood pressure is 168/94 mm Hg sitting, pulse 88 per minute, oxygen saturation 95% on room air, and respiratory rate 18 per minute. He has mild ankle edema and no crackles. He sleeps on two pillows “as always.” His post-dialysis weights over the last month reached the target each time. He says his appetite has been poor since a fever two weeks ago.

Ms. Ramos has entered a fluid goal of 4.2 liters: 3.9 kilograms of weight gain plus 0.3 liters for the rinse-back and his drinks during the session. She asks whether she may start. All values are illustrative.

  • Which findings support the current target weight, and which undercut it?
  • What do you write, whom do you call, and which options belong to the attending nephrologist?

Lesson · part 1

What dry weight is, and what it is not

Healthy kidneys match salt and water output to intake, hour by hour. Eat a salty meal, and the kidneys excrete the extra sodium and the water that travels with it. A patient on dialysis has little or no output. Every gram of salt eaten holds water in the extracellular space until the next session. The weight climbs between sessions, and the machine must remove it.

The fluid the patient carries before a session has two parts. The first is today’s gain, the interdialytic weight gain (IDWG). The second is any overload left after the last session because the target was set too high. A consensus group of 18 authors put it this way: pre-dialysis volume overload is the sum of the weight gain and the overload left behind after dialysis.1 The scale sees only the first part. The second part hides under the target.

A working definition

For this module, dry weight is the lowest weight after dialysis that the patient tolerates without symptoms or low pressure during or after the session, and without signs of overload between sessions.2 This is a clinical definition, not a measured quantity. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) Controversies Conference on blood pressure and volume in dialysis named the lack of an objective measure of extracellular volume as a central problem in this field.3

Why the hidden part matters

Chronic overload is associated with death. Wizemann and colleagues measured hydration with bioimpedance in 269 patients in three European centers and followed them for 3.5 years. Relative overhydration before dialysis was an independent predictor of death, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 2.10, second only to diabetes. Patients more than 15% above normal extracellular water, about 2.5 liters, had a gross mortality of 14.7%. The whole group’s annual figure was 8.5%.4

The weight gain itself carries a smaller signal. Kalantar-Zadeh and colleagues studied 34,107 patients in the United States. A gain of 4.0 kilograms or more, compared with 1.5 to 2.0 kilograms, was associated with cardiovascular death (hazard ratio 1.25).5 The consensus group concluded that chronic overload carries more than twice the mortality risk, while the risk linked to the gain alone is much smaller and is seen mainly with very large gains.1

All three sources are observational. Sicker and frailer patients may carry more water for reasons that also shorten life. These studies show an association. They do not show that lowering the target prolongs life.

For you, the target on the order is the attending nephrologist’s current estimate. Use it for the arithmetic, never change it, and notice when the patient no longer fits it.

RememberThe scale reading is a fact. The target weight is an estimate. Your job is to notice when the two stop agreeing.
Lesson · part 2

Assessing volume at the bedside, and its limits

Think of driving in fog with weak headlights. Each sign lights a little of the road, and none lights all of it. You combine weak signs and compare them with the last few sessions.

Each sign has its own physiology. Pitting edema appears only after a large expansion of the tissue spaces. Crackles need fluid in the air spaces, which comes late; water in the lung tissue is silent. The jugular venous pressure reflects right heart filling, which other heart disease can raise. Low albumin, heart failure, venous disease, and calcium channel blockers all cause edema without excess body water.

The Lung Water by Ultra-Sound Guided Treatment (LUST) investigators compared crackles and edema with lung ultrasound in 1,106 paired examinations from 79 high-risk patients. Crackles shared only 12% of their variance with lung water, and edema only 4%. Kappa values, which measure agreement beyond chance, ranged from 0.00 to 0.16. The area under the curve was 0.61 to 0.68 for crackles and 0.56 or lower for edema, where 0.5 is a coin toss.6

Lung water can also rise with no symptoms. In a multicenter study of 392 patients, 45% had moderate to severe lung congestion on ultrasound. Of those, 71% had no or only slight symptoms of heart failure.7

What you do at the chair

Run a structured assessment when a fluid goal looks large or the patient looks different. Module 11 covers the three-minute pre-dialysis check; this is the volume part of it, done in more depth.

  1. Review the last four to six sessions: pre- and post-dialysis weights, pre-dialysis pressure, and any cramps or low pressure.
  2. Ask about breathlessness, orthopnea and any change in it, cramps, dizziness at home, appetite, and urine output.
  3. Examine for edema, jugular venous pressure, and crackles.
  4. Check the standing pressure if the patient has been dizzy after sessions.
  5. Write what you found, not a verdict. “Edema 1+ to the ankles, unchanged from last week” helps the attending. “Euvolemic” does not.
RememberNo single bedside sign measures dry weight. Trends across sessions tell you more than one examination.
Lesson · part 3

The fluid goal and the hourly rate

Picture draining a pool through one pipe. Drain the same volume in three hours instead of four, and the water must run faster. The fluid goal is the volume. The ultrafiltration (UF) rate is the speed. Module 5 explained how the machine creates the flow; this section is the arithmetic.

The arithmetic

The formulas are definitions. The session goal in milliliters is the pre-dialysis weight minus the target weight, in kilograms, times 1,000, plus any fluid your unit’s protocol adds: the rinse-back, saline flushes, or drinks on the chair. Units differ on what they add, so learn your unit’s rule.

The UF rate in mL/kg/h is the goal divided by the session hours times the body weight. Studies differ on which weight they use; one large cohort used the post-dialysis weight.8 Follow your unit’s convention every time.

Figure 3 · From the scale to the rate

  1. Weight gainPre-dialysis weight minus target weight. In the case, 68.4 minus 64.5 = 3.9 kg.
  2. Session goalWeight gain in milliliters plus the fluid your unit’s protocol adds. In the case, 3,900 + 300 = 4,200 mL.
  3. Hourly volumeGoal divided by booked hours. 4,200 ÷ 3 = 1,400 mL per hour.
  4. Rate per kilogramHourly volume divided by the weight your unit uses. 1,400 ÷ 64.5 = 21.7 mL/kg/h.
  5. CompareSet the rate beside your unit’s ceiling. If it is above, call before the session starts.

What this tells you: the booked time cannot remove this goal within the ceiling. What it does not tell you: whether the goal or the target is right.

Why the rate matters: the evidence

Every study linking the rate to harm is observational. No randomized trial has yet shown that keeping the rate under any ceiling improves survival.

The KDIGO conference listed a slower fluid removal rate among the measures to consider for patients who drop their pressure. Its conclusions are conference statements, not graded recommendations.3 Your unit’s protocol sets the ceiling. Learn it, and learn which weight it divides by.

Lesson · part 4

Plasma refilling: why fast removal drops the pressure

Picture the machine drinking from a small glass that is refilled from a large jug. The glass is the plasma, about 3 liters in an adult. The jug is the fluid between the cells, several times larger. If the machine drinks faster than the jug pours, the glass empties, however full the jug is.

The picture has a limit. The jug does not pour by gravity. Fluid moves back into the capillaries because of forces across the capillary wall, and those forces change during the session.

The physiology

Ultrafiltration removes water only from the plasma. Proteins, mainly albumin, stay behind. As water leaves, the protein in the plasma becomes more concentrated, and its oncotic pressure rises. That pressure pulls fluid from the tissue spaces into the capillaries. At the same time, an expanded tissue space has a higher pressure of its own, which pushes fluid toward the vessels. These are the Starling forces.11

Refilling is fast when the tissue spaces are full and the albumin is normal. It slows as the patient approaches true dry weight, because the tissue pressure falls. It also slows with low albumin, with a warm dialysis fluid and opened skin vessels, and with posture changes.12 When removal outruns refilling, blood volume falls. Less blood returns to the heart, and the heart pumps less. The pressure falls if the vessels and the heart cannot compensate. Module 13 covers those defenses and the response when they fail.

This is why an overloaded patient often tolerates removal early, and why the pressure tends to fall late, as refilling slows near the target. A plan to “take off the rest in the last hour” puts the fastest removal exactly where refilling is slowest. That is the most dangerous moment of the session.

What monitoring can and cannot do

Some machines track the relative change in blood volume from the concentration of the blood. A review of intradialytic hypotension judged absolute blood volume measures more promising than relative ones for predicting episodes, and found that feedback systems that change the UF rate automatically have not yet improved outcomes.13 A falling blood volume curve tells you that removal is outrunning refilling. It does not tell you where the true dry weight lies.

RememberFluid leaves the blood quickly and returns from the tissues slowly. Speed, as much as volume, decides whether the pressure holds.
Lesson · part 5

Lung ultrasound and bioimpedance in outline

Two tools try to see what the stethoscope misses. Lung ultrasound counts the “comet tails” that wet lung tissue throws on the screen. Bioimpedance estimates extracellular water from how easily a tiny current passes through the body. Both measure fluid. Neither is an outcome.

A normal, air-filled lung reflects the ultrasound beam as horizontal repeating lines, called A-lines. When the thin walls between the small air units of the lung thicken with water, the beam produces vertical bright streaks, called B-lines. The more B-lines across the chest, the more water in the lung.7

Bioimpedance spectroscopy uses the fact that cell membranes block low-frequency current. At low frequency the current travels around the cells, through the extracellular water. At high frequency it also passes through the cells. Comparing the two lets the device estimate extracellular and intracellular water, and from them an overhydration value.

Lung ultrasound: what the evidence shows

The prognostic signal is strong. In the 392-patient cohort, very severe congestion was associated with a 4.2-fold risk of death (hazard ratio 4.20) and a 3.2-fold risk of cardiac events (3.20), adjusted for heart failure class and other risk factors.7

The treatment signal is weaker. The LUST trial randomized 367 high-cardiovascular-risk patients; in the active arm, the pre-dialysis lung scan guided UF and drug treatment. Over a mean of 1.49 years, congestion was relieved more often with ultrasound guidance (78% versus 56%). The primary composite of death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and decompensated heart failure did not differ (hazard ratio 0.88, 95% confidence interval 0.63 to 1.24). Nor did hospitalization or patient-reported outcomes. A post hoc analysis suggested fewer recurrent heart failure episodes (0.37) and cardiovascular events (0.63).14 Post hoc results generate hypotheses. They do not prove benefit.

In a LUST substudy of 71 patients, ultrasound-guided dry weight reduction lowered 48-hour central systolic pressure by 6.3 mm Hg, against 0.5 mm Hg in controls, over 8 weeks.15 That is a surrogate. In 250 low-risk patients, a combined lung ultrasound and bioimpedance protocol did not reduce death or cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 1.09). It reduced dyspnea before dialysis (rate ratio 0.81) and increased cramps (rate ratio 1.26).16

Bioimpedance: what the evidence shows

In 156 patients, bioimpedance guidance reduced left ventricular mass index by 10.2 g/m² more than usual care over one year, a surrogate.17 A single-center pilot of 131 patients reported an unadjusted mortality hazard ratio of 0.100. Events were few, caregivers were not masked, and the authors asked for a larger trial.18 A review of 8 randomized trials with 1,443 patients found no significant difference in mortality or cardiovascular events. Overhydration and blood pressure fell, and urine output fell faster in the two trials with the largest change in fluid status.19

The largest recent trial, BioImpedance Spectroscopy to maintain Renal Output (BISTRO), randomized 439 incident patients with residual kidney function in 34 United Kingdom centers. Time to anuria did not differ (cause-specific hazard ratio 0.751, 95% confidence interval 0.459 to 1.229), and neither did blood pressure or symptoms. Both arms used a standardized clinical fluid assessment, which the authors concluded preserved residual function well without bioimpedance. The trial recruited 85% of its target.20

Lesson · part 6

When the target is too high or too low

Dry weight is a moving target. An illness melts muscle, so the same scale number holds more water. Recovery adds flesh, so the old target drains too much. The order stays the same until someone notices.

Two ways to be wrong

When the target is too high, the patient leaves every session still overloaded. The blood pressure rises, and water collects in the lungs, often silently.7 When the target is too low, the patient is underfilled at the end of each session, with cramps, late drops in pressure, and hours of exhaustion afterward. In patients who still pass urine, repeated underfilling may hasten the loss of that urine. Faster loss of residual function is associated with higher mortality in new dialysis patients.21 In the bioimpedance trials, urine output fell faster in the two trials that removed the most fluid.19

What happens when the target is pushed down

The Dry-weight Reduction In hypertensive hemodialysis Patients (DRIP) trial randomized 150 hypertensive patients, 100 to dry weight probing and 50 to control. Without changing session time, the target fell by about 1 kilogram, and interdialytic ambulatory systolic pressure fell by 6.9 mm Hg at 4 weeks and 6.6 mm Hg at 8 weeks against control. Symptoms of low pressure during dialysis increased; quality of life did not worsen.22 DRIP shows a fall in blood pressure, a surrogate, over 8 weeks. It did not test death, cardiovascular events, or residual urine.

The trade-off is unsettled. A lower target lowers the pressure but may cost residual function and comfort, by amounts unknown for the individual patient.19,20

What you do

Document the pattern across sessions, not a single reading. Report it to the attending nephrologist in the situation, background, assessment, recommendation (SBAR) format. Proposing a review is your role. Writing a new target is not.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist for any proposed change in target weight, from any source: a nurse, the patient, a relative, or another doctor. Report the pattern and the trend. Do not alter the target or the goal yourself.
RememberA target weight is reviewed on a pattern, not on one session. Bring the pattern to the attending.
Lesson · part 7

When the goal cannot be reached safely

Think of a bridge rated for a certain load. If the truck is too heavy, you do not drive faster. You split the load. When a fluid goal cannot be removed within the ceiling in the booked time, the answer is never to run the machine harder.

The limits are refilling speed and the reserve of the heart and vessels. Module 13 covers what happens when they are crossed.

Options belong to the attending nephrologist

You list the options. The attending chooses. The usual choices are these:

  • Accept a partial goal today and add a session in the next day or two.
  • Extend today’s session, if the patient and the schedule allow.
  • Isolated UF, before or after the diffusion part of the session (Module 12).
  • Review salt intake and the dialysis fluid sodium (Module 7). Salt and fluid counseling with Filipino foods is in Module 23.
  • Revise the target weight, if the clues point that way.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The early pickup

What you see
Mrs. Corazon Dela Cruz, 47 kilograms, arrives 3.0 kilograms above target on a Monday. Her son must fetch her early, and the nurse has booked 3 hours instead of 4.
What is likely going on
A goal of about 3 liters in a 47-kilogram patient is a fast rate per kilogram even over 4 hours. The shorter session makes it faster still.
What to do
Calculate the goal and the rate with your unit’s weight and ceiling. If the rate is above the ceiling, call the attending nephrologist before the session starts with the weights, goal, hours, rate, and minimum time. Ask the family whether the pickup can move.
Why it matters
Small patients reach a high rate per kilogram with modest volumes. A schedule problem should not become a pressure problem.

Same weight, thinner arms

What you see
Mr. Rodel Santos had two weeks in the hospital for pneumonia. His post-dialysis weight still reaches target, but his arms are thin, his ankles are swollen, and his pre-dialysis pressure has risen over four sessions.
What is likely going on
He has likely lost muscle and fat, and water now fills the same scale weight. The target may be too high.
What to do
Document the four-session trend, the edema, and the illness history. Report the pattern to the attending nephrologist in SBAR form and suggest a target review.
Why it matters
The scale cannot tell flesh from water. A target that was right before an illness can be wrong after it.

Cramps every Thursday

What you see
Ms. Analyn Garcia has cramps and a low pressure in the last hour of most sessions. She feels dizzy on standing after dialysis, and her urine output has fallen. She has been eating well since she started a new job.
What is likely going on
She may have gained flesh, and the old target now dries her too far. Late cramps and dizziness fit a target set too low.
What to do
Manage today’s symptoms by your unit’s protocol and Module 13. Report the pattern, the standing pressures, and the urine history to the attending nephrologist, and suggest a target review.
Why it matters
Repeated underfilling is uncomfortable and may cost residual urine. A pattern is a prescription question.

The relative’s request

What you see
The daughter of Mang Ernesto asks you to lower her father’s target by one kilogram because his ankles are swollen. He takes amlodipine and his albumin was low last month.
What is likely going on
Edema has causes other than overload, including calcium channel blockers and low albumin. It may or may not mean the target is too high.
What to do
Thank her, assess him, and record the findings. Tell her that the attending nephrologist decides the target, and call the attending with the request and your findings.
Why it matters
Any proposed change in target weight goes to the attending, from any source. A rushed change can cause cramps and low pressure.

The silent lung

What you see
Mr. Benjamin Reyes has no crackles and no edema. His pre-dialysis pressure is rising, and he admits he now sleeps sitting up. A visiting nephrology fellow mentions many B-lines on a quick lung scan.
What is likely going on
Lung water can rise without crackles. New orthopnea and B-lines both point toward overload.
What to do
Check oxygen saturation and breathing at rest. If he is breathless at rest or desaturating, follow your unit’s protocol and Module 14. Otherwise, report the findings to the attending nephrologist before the session.
Why it matters
A clear chest on the stethoscope does not exclude lung congestion. New orthopnea deserves a call.
Field notes
  • Calculate the UF rate yourself at every chair where the goal looks large. It takes less than a minute.
  • Ask what changed: appetite, illness, a hospital stay, a new medicine. Weight that stays the same can hide a change in body makeup.
  • Learn your unit’s ceiling and which weight it divides by before your first shift.
  • Write findings, not verdicts. “Edema 1+ to the ankles, unchanged” helps the attending more than “euvolemic.”
  • When a goal does not fit the booked time, call before the session starts, not after the pressure falls.
Case review

Back to the case: Four kilos over, three hours booked

What rate does the goal require? Using the target weight of 64.5 kilograms to normalize, 4,200 mL over 3 hours is 21.7 mL/kg/h. Over his usual 4 hours it is 16.3 mL/kg/h. Against an illustrative ceiling of 13 mL/kg/h, both are above it. Staying at 13 would take about 5 hours. Even his usual session would not fit this goal within that ceiling. In the cohorts that studied it, a rate above 13 was associated with higher mortality.9,10 Use your unit’s own ceiling and weight convention.

Which findings support the target, and which undercut it? In favor: his post-dialysis weights reached the target every session last month, he has no crackles, and his oxygen saturation is acceptable. Against: his pressure is 168/94 mm Hg, he has ankle edema, and his appetite has been poor since a fever two weeks ago. A man who eats less loses flesh. If his weight still reaches 64.5 kilograms, water may have taken the place of that flesh, and the target may now be too high. Ask whether the two pillows are new. Absent crackles do not settle the question.6 His gain of 6.0% of target tells you what he took in over the long gap, not what hides under the target.1

What do you write, whom do you call, and whose options are they? You write the weights, the goal, the two rates, the minimum time, and your bedside findings, including the appetite history. You call the attending nephrologist before the session starts, in SBAR form. The options belong to the attending: a partial goal today with an extra session this week, a longer session if transport can be rearranged, isolated UF, and a review of the target weight given the recent illness. You do not shorten the goal, extend the session, or change the target on your own.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Volume assessment and fluid goal calculation at the chair

Perform this assessment at a real dialysis chair while your preceptor observes. Use illustrative numbers if the patient’s goal is routine, and talk through each step aloud. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • Confirms the patient’s identity and reads the target weight and UF instructions on the current order.
  • Checks that the scale was zeroed and that the patient was weighed in the usual clothing.
  • CriticalReviews the last four to six sessions: pre- and post-dialysis weights, pre-dialysis pressure, and any low pressure or cramps.
  • Asks about orthopnea and any change in it, breathlessness, cramps, dizziness at home, appetite, and urine output.
  • Examines for edema, jugular venous pressure, and crackles, and states the limits of each sign.
  • CriticalCalculates the session goal, including the fluids your unit’s protocol adds.
  • CriticalCalculates the hourly UF rate per kilogram with the unit’s chosen weight and compares it with the unit’s ceiling.
  • CriticalIf the rate exceeds the ceiling, calls the attending nephrologist before the session starts, with the numbers.

  • CriticalDoes not change the target weight or the goal without an order.

Placement activity. For two weeks, calculate the UF rate for every patient whose goal looks large on your shifts. Keep a log of the rate, the booked time, and any symptoms. Review the log with your preceptor and pick one patient whose target you would ask the attending nephrologist to review, with the clue pattern that supports it.
Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 8 questions at random from this module’s 15-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 7 of 8 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 15Which statement best describes dry weight?
No device measures dry weight directly. It is a clinical estimate, and it must be revised when the patient gains or loses flesh.
Question 2 of 15A consensus group described pre-dialysis volume overload as the sum of two parts. What are they?
The scale sees the gain. Overload left behind because the target is too high hides under the target.
Question 3 of 15In the LUST auscultation substudy, how well did crackles reflect lung water on ultrasound?
Crackles shared about 12% of the variance with B-lines and edema about 4%. Their absence does not exclude lung water.
Question 4 of 15A man arrives 3.2 kilograms above target for a 4-hour session. His unit adds 0.2 liters for the rinse-back and normalizes to a target weight of 64 kilograms. What is his UF rate?
Goal 3,400 mL. 3,400 ÷ (4 × 64) = 3,400 ÷ 256 = 13.3 mL/kg/h. The numbers are illustrative.
Question 5 of 15What is the strongest evidence currently available that UF rates above 13 mL/kg/h are harmful?
The HEMO secondary analysis and a cohort of 118,394 patients found associations. No randomized trial has tested a ceiling.
Question 6 of 15According to the Mermelstein cohort, how does a fixed ceiling in mL/kg/h behave across body sizes?
Risk levels followed 3W plus a constant, so the same risk sits at a higher mL/kg/h in a small patient and a lower one in a heavy patient. A fixed ceiling restricts small patients more and heavy patients less.
Question 7 of 15Why does the blood pressure tend to fall late in a session in a patient near target?
Refilling depends on an expanded tissue space. Near true dry weight it slows, and removal outruns it.
Question 8 of 15A nurse proposes removing the remaining 1.5 liters in the last hour because the patient was dizzy earlier. What is the problem with this plan?
The late session is when refilling is slowest. Fast removal there invites a sharp drop. Call the attending about the remaining goal.
Question 9 of 15What did the LUST randomized trial show?
Congestion was relieved in 78% versus 56%, but the primary outcome did not differ (hazard ratio 0.88). Post hoc benefits are hypotheses.
Question 10 of 15What was the main finding of the BISTRO trial?
The hazard ratio was 0.751 with a confidence interval crossing 1. The authors credit the standardized clinical protocol.
Question 11 of 15In the DRIP trial, probing the dry weight down by about 1 kilogram did what?
DRIP showed a blood pressure benefit, a surrogate, over 8 weeks. It did not test hard outcomes.
Question 12 of 15A woman has had cramps in the last hour of most sessions for three weeks. She is dizzy at home after dialysis, and her urine output has fallen. She has been eating well since a hospital stay. What is the most likely issue?
Weight gained as flesh makes the old target too low. Report the pattern; the attending decides.
Question 13 of 15A man’s post-dialysis weights reach target every session, but his edema persists and his pre-dialysis pressure is climbing. He lost his appetite after pneumonia a month ago. What should you do?
Lost flesh may have been replaced by water. Proposing a review is your role; writing a new target is not.
Question 14 of 15A relative asks you to lower her father’s target weight because his ankles are swollen. What do you do?
Any proposed change in target weight, from any source, goes to the attending. Edema has causes other than overload.
Question 15 of 15A patient arrives 4.5 kilograms over target for a 3-hour session, and the rate would exceed your unit’s ceiling. What is the best action?
A goal that does not fit the ceiling is a prescription question. The attending chooses among the options.

Abbreviations

IDWG
Interdialytic weight gain — Weight gained between dialysis sessions; almost all fluid.
KDIGO
Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes — The international nephrology guideline body.
SBAR
Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation — A structure for clinical reports.
UF
Ultrafiltration.
UFR
Ultrafiltration rate.

Terms in plain words

Dry weight (target weight)
The lowest weight after dialysis that the patient tolerates without symptoms or low pressure, and without signs of overload between sessions. A clinical estimate.
Interdialytic weight gain
The weight gained between the end of one session and the start of the next.
Session fluid goal
The volume to remove in one session: the weight above target plus the fluids the unit’s protocol adds.
Ultrafiltration rate
The volume removed per hour, usually divided by body weight and written in mL/kg/h.
Plasma refilling
Movement of fluid from the tissue spaces back into the blood vessels during fluid removal.
Starling forces
The pressures across the capillary wall, from fluid and from protein, that decide which way fluid moves.
Extracellular water
Water outside the cells, in the plasma and between the cells.
B-lines
Vertical bright streaks on lung ultrasound that indicate extra water in the lung tissue.
Bioimpedance spectroscopy
A bedside test that estimates extracellular and intracellular water from how the body conducts current at different frequencies.
Surrogate outcome
A measurement, such as blood pressure or heart muscle mass, used in place of an outcome the patient feels, such as death or hospitalization.
