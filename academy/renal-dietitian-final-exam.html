Before you begin

How this examination works

This examination covers the whole Renal Dietitian series. Each sitting draws 40 questions at random from a bank of 240, in random order, with the answer choices shuffled. Choose the single best answer for each question, then select Check answers.

When to take itAfter you have finished every module and reviewed the questions you missed.
How to take itIn one sitting, without notes. Allow about one minute per question.
Proposed pass mark32 of 40 best answers (80 percent). Your trainer may set a different mark.
If you fall shortEach explanation names its module. Reread those modules, then select New set of questions.
What a pass meansA pass shows that you know the material. It does not show that you can do the work. Competence is demonstrated on your unit’s own equipment and signed off by a preceptor.

End-of-course examination

Question 1An eGFR of 34 mL/min/1.73 m² with a UACR of 420 mg/g, both confirmed over four months, is classified as:
An estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 30 to 44 is G3b, and a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) above 300 mg/g is A3. Module 1.
Question 2What establishes that kidney disease is chronic?
Chronicity requires abnormality of structure or function for at least three months. One result may reflect a temporary problem. Module 1.
Question 3A patient has an eGFR of 75 and a normal urine test and ultrasound. Does she have CKD?
G1 and G2 are CKD only when a marker of kidney damage, such as albuminuria, is also present. Module 1.
Question 4Why can a frail, thin patient have a misleadingly good creatinine-based eGFR?
Creatinine is produced by muscle. With little muscle, serum creatinine is low and the formula overestimates kidney function. Module 1.
Question 5During a call, a patient reports new palpitations, and the laboratory flags her potassium as critical. What is the first action?
A critical result with symptoms needs immediate medical assessment. Diet teaching comes after stabilization. Module 1.
Question 6Which referral should be seen within days, not at the next routine clinic?
A new dialysis start with falling intake is a high-risk transition and should be expedited. Module 1.
Question 7A patient asks whether she can take two phosphate binder tablets with a large meal. The prescription says one. The dietitian should:
Changing a dose belongs to the prescriber. The dietitian reports the question and reinforces the written instruction. Module 1.
Question 8In SBAR, where does “spironolactone was added last month” belong?
Background holds the relevant history, including recent medicine changes. Module 1.
Question 9A handoff is “closed” when:
Closing the loop means read-back and acceptance. A message nobody acknowledged has not been delivered. Module 1.
Question 10Which document requires Philippine hospitals to record nutrition care as assessment, diagnosis, intervention, and monitoring?
The Department of Health order sets out the Nutrition Care Process in hospitals. Module 1.
Question 11Why did guidelines move away from fixed lists of forbidden foods?
Restriction for everyone risks wasting, and non-diet causes are common. Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) 2020 recommends adjusting intake to the individual’s serum levels. Module 1.
Question 12Which item is NOT one of the six things to collect before a first consult?
The six are the referral question, stage or modality, recent admissions, weight and intake trend, key results with dates, and medicines. Module 1.
Question 13The UACR mainly tells you:
Albuminuria reflects damage to the filtering barrier and predicts progression and cardiovascular risk. Module 1.
Question 14You cannot reach the attending physician about a critical result. What next?
The escalation list exists for this. Move to the next contact within the time your policy sets. Module 1.
Question 15After an urgent handoff, what should the note contain?
The record must let another clinician see what was found, who was told, and who took responsibility. Module 1.
Question 16Which nutrient is the source of urea?
Urea is made by the liver from the nitrogen in amino acids. Module 2.
Question 17A patient with CKD eats less for two weeks, and his urea rises. The most likely explanation is:
With low intake, a rising urea points to catabolism or another nitrogen source. Module 2.
Question 18After a salty meal, what does the body defend first?
Thirst and antidiuretic hormone restore concentration by retaining water, which expands volume. Module 2.
Question 19Why does telling a hemodialysis patient to “drink less” often fail on its own?
Sodium raises the blood concentration and triggers thirst. Lowering sodium lowers the drive to drink. Module 2.
Question 20In the Cochrane review of salt reduction in CKD, the blood pressure fell by about:
Lowering sodium by about 1,700 mg a day reduced blood pressure by about 7/4 mm Hg; trials were short. Module 2.
Question 21About how much of the body’s potassium is inside cells?
Nearly all potassium is intracellular, so small shifts change the blood level. Module 2.
Question 22Which hormone moves potassium into cells within minutes of a meal?
Insulin, with adrenaline, drives potassium into cells. Fasting and insulin deficiency weaken this. Module 2.
Question 23Which of these lowers kidney potassium excretion?
Spironolactone blocks aldosterone, which the kidney needs to excrete potassium. Module 2.
Question 24Why does constipation matter for potassium in advanced CKD?
Colonic excretion becomes more important as eGFR falls, and constipation reduces it. Module 2.
Question 25Which source of phosphorus is absorbed most completely?
Additives are nearly fully absorbed; plant phytate is the least available. Module 2.
Question 26In early CKD, serum phosphorus is usually normal because:
Hormonal compensation keeps the level normal for years, at the cost of high PTH and low active vitamin D. Module 2.
Question 27Which foods add the most acid load?
Animal protein and grains produce acid; fruits and vegetables supply alkali. Module 2.
Question 28Chronic metabolic acidosis in CKD contributes to:
Acid is buffered by bone and promotes muscle protein breakdown. Module 2.
Question 29The Maroni formula estimates protein intake from:
It uses urine urea nitrogen plus a weight-based estimate of non-urea nitrogen, in a stable patient. Module 2.
Question 30In a large cohort on hemodialysis, higher reported dietary potassium was:
The association was null; the study was observational, so it informs but does not settle practice. Module 2.
Question 31A patient’s post-dialysis weight was 62 kg six weeks ago and is 58 kg now. Today’s pre-dialysis weight is 61 kg. The percent loss is:
Compare post-dialysis with post-dialysis: (62 − 58) ÷ 62 × 100 = 6.5%. Module 3.
Question 32Why is the pre-dialysis weight a poor measure of tissue change?
Interdialytic fluid can add several kilograms and hide tissue loss. Module 3.
Question 33A stable weight with rising blood pressure and new ankle edema may mean:
Fluid can replace lost tissue at the same scale weight. Report it to the nephrologist. Module 3.
Question 34Which intake method does KDOQI prefer for estimating energy and protein intake?
A three-day record, including a dialysis day and a non-dialysis day, reduces day-to-day error. Module 3.
Question 35The nPCR is unreliable as a measure of protein intake when the patient is:
Urea then reflects tissue breakdown or building, not intake alone. Module 3.
Question 36Which is NOT a cause of low serum albumin?
Albumin falls with inflammation, dilution, losses, and liver disease. Module 3.
Question 37Which tool does KDOQI firmly recommend (grade 1B) for assessing nutritional status in adults on dialysis?
The 7-point Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) is recommended as valid and reliable in CKD stage 5 on dialysis. Module 3.
Question 38On which arm should handgrip be measured in a hemodialysis patient with a fistula?
Protect the access arm; measure the other side the same way each time. Module 3.
Question 39How many of the four ISRNM categories must be met to diagnose PEW?
At least one item in three of the four categories. Module 3.
Question 40The GLIM diagnosis requires:
Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) combines a phenotype (weight loss, low body mass index, or low muscle) with a cause (reduced intake or inflammation). Module 3.
Question 41Which etiology makes a PES statement actionable?
The etiology should be something the nutrition plan can change. Module 3.
Question 42Unintentional weight loss that meets the ISRNM body-mass criterion is:
These thresholds are part of the protein-energy wasting (PEW) body-mass category. Module 3.
Question 43A small-framed, well, active patient has a stable body mass index of 21. On the PEW body mass index item alone you should:
PEW needs three categories. The 23 kg/m² cut-off is applied with judgment in small-framed adults. Module 3.
Question 44Intake has halved and weight has fallen 6% in six weeks. Besides starting a plan, you should:
Combined intake decline and weight loss needs prompt team review. Module 3.
Question 45A good monitoring plan states:
A plan another person could carry out names the measure, the time, and the owner. Module 3.
Question 46Under KDIGO 2024, the suggested protein intake for an adult with CKD G4 who weighs 60 kg is:
0.8 g/kg/day × 60 kg = 48 g/day (a weak suggestion, grade 2C). Module 4.
Question 47Under KDOQI 2020, the low-protein range for a metabolically stable 60 kg adult with CKD stage 4 without diabetes is:
0.55 to 0.60 g/kg/day × 60 kg = 33 to 36 g/day, under close clinical supervision (a firm recommendation, grade 1A). Module 4.
Question 48The KDOQI energy recommendation for metabolically stable adults with CKD is:
25 to 35 kcal/kg/day, individualized (a firm recommendation, grade 1C). Module 4.
Question 49Why must energy be adequate before protein is lowered?
Protein is spared for tissue maintenance only when energy needs are met. Module 4.
Question 50Which patient should NOT be offered a low-protein diet?
Frailty with weight loss calls for protecting intake; KDIGO suggests considering higher targets in such patients. Module 4.
Question 51In the ketoanalogue trial by Garneata and colleagues, what limits applying the result widely?
The trial shows what is achievable in highly selected patients. Module 4.
Question 52The Cochrane review of low-protein diets in adults without diabetes concluded that:
The benefit was seen for very low-protein diets in stages 4 to 5; mortality was probably not affected. Module 4.
Question 53An observational study finds that older adults with CKD who eat more protein die less often. The best reading is:
Association is not causation; confounding by health status is likely. Module 4.
Question 54“Metabolically stable” excludes a patient who:
A recent hospital stay for acute illness means the patient is not stable. Module 4.
Question 55KDIGO’s advice to avoid more than 1.3 g/kg/day in adults at risk of progression is a:
It is an ungraded practice point. Module 4.
Question 56One cup of cooked rice provides about how much protein?
About 4 g per cup; three cups add about 12 g a day. Module 4.
Question 57Which first change addresses protein, sodium, and phosphate additives together?
Processed meats carry all three; fresh sources lower them without lowering energy. Module 4.
Question 58A patient on a supervised low-protein diet develops pneumonia and eats poorly. You should:
Restriction is for stable patients; illness raises needs and the risk of wasting. Module 4.
Question 59The plant-dominant low-protein diet is best described as:
It rests on physiology; outcome trials have not yet been done. Module 4.
Question 60For an adult with diabetes and CKD not on dialysis, the American Diabetes Association and KDIGO Diabetes guideline advise aiming for:
Both advise 0.8 g/kg/day; lower intakes are not recommended in diabetes. Module 4.
Question 61A label lists 720 mg of sodium per serving and two servings per packet. A patient eats the whole packet. Sodium eaten is:
Two servings × 720 mg = 1,440 mg. Module 5.
Question 622 g of sodium is about how much salt?
Sodium × 2.5 = salt; 2 g of sodium is about 5 g of salt, one level teaspoon. Module 5.
Question 63KDIGO 2024 suggests a sodium intake for people with CKD of:
Less than 2 g of sodium a day (a weak suggestion, grade 2C). Module 5.
Question 64The KDOQI 2020 sodium statement is:
Less than 2.3 g (100 mmol) a day; grade 1B in CKD stages 3 to 5 and 1C on dialysis. Module 5.
Question 65What did the Cochrane review of salt reduction in CKD establish with high certainty?
Blood pressure fell by about 7/4 mm Hg. Trials were too short for hard outcomes. Module 5.
Question 66Pre-dialysis weight 66.5 kg; previous post-dialysis weight 64.0 kg. The gain is:
66.5 − 64.0 = 2.5 kg; 2.5 ÷ 64.0 × 100 = 3.9%. Module 5.
Question 67Why should sodium be taught before fluid?
Thirst follows sodium intake. Module 5.
Question 68A patient on twice-weekly dialysis has the same daily intake as one on thrice-weekly dialysis. His weight gain per interval will be:
Gain accumulates over more days. Module 5.
Question 69A salt substitute lists potassium chloride. For a patient with CKD G4 on losartan, you should:
Potassium chloride is fully absorbed, and the trial evidence excluded people with serious kidney disease. Module 5.
Question 70The large salt-substitute trial showed fewer strokes in:
The benefit applies to the population studied, which excluded those at risk of hyperkalemia. Module 5.
Question 71Which patient most likely does NOT need a fluid limit?
Patients who pass urine normally and have no fluid retention should drink to thirst. Module 5.
Question 72Very low weight gains with falling post-dialysis weight suggest:
Little food means little sodium and fluid; check the food record. Module 5.
Question 73Which counts toward a fluid allowance?
Liquids and foods that are liquid at room temperature count. Module 5.
Question 74Which finding makes a visit urgent?
Resting breathlessness may be pulmonary edema and needs immediate assessment. Module 5.
Question 75Who sets the dry weight and the fluid prescription?
The dietitian contributes intake and weight data; the nephrologist decides. Module 5.
Question 76A stable patient has a mildly high potassium on two checks. What comes before changing whole-food intake?
Hyperkalemia is usually multifactorial, and diet is seldom the only cause. Module 6.
Question 77Which ingredient most deserves attention on a “low-sodium salt” label?
Potassium chloride is absorbed almost completely. Module 6.
Question 78Why is a glass of buko juice a different load from a serving of vegetables with similar potassium?
Liquids deliver potassium rapidly; whole foods bring fiber and alkali. Module 6.
Question 79A hemolyzed blood sample can cause:
Potassium leaks from broken red cells: pseudohyperkalemia. Module 6.
Question 80Which over-the-counter medicine can raise potassium and harm kidney function?
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs reduce kidney potassium excretion. Module 6.
Question 81A patient asks whether to stop her angiotensin receptor blocker because of potassium. You should:
renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors are protective; the dietitian helps remove other causes. Module 6.
Question 82How does constipation raise potassium in advanced CKD?
The colon is an important exit route when eGFR is low. Module 6.
Question 83KDOQI’s statement on dietary potassium is:
It is an opinion-based statement that calls for individualization. Module 6.
Question 84In the trial of fruits and vegetables for acidosis in stage 4 CKD, potassium did not rise. An important limit is that:
The result applies to selected, monitored patients. Module 6.
Question 85KDIGO suggests considering treatment to prevent acidosis with clinical consequences at a bicarbonate below about:
A practice point gives below 18 mmol/L in adults as an example. Module 6.
Question 86Which change is most likely to lower potassium at the least nutritional cost?
Removing concentrated, fully absorbed sources costs little nutritionally. Module 6.
Question 87Boiling cut root crops in plenty of water and discarding the water:
Leaching lowers potassium content, with loss of water-soluble vitamins. Module 6.
Question 88In which patient is potassium restriction usually wrong?
Low potassium is common in peritoneal dialysis and should be reported, not restricted. Module 6.
Question 89An old potassium restriction should be lifted when:
Restrictions should end when the reason ends. Module 6.
Question 90The observational finding that dietary potassium was not associated with serum potassium on hemodialysis means:
It weakens the case for universal restriction without proving intake is irrelevant. Module 6.
Question 91Why do most patients on hemodialysis need binders despite dialysis?
A session removes on the order of 800 mg, less than typical absorption over the days between. Module 7.
Question 92Which source of phosphorus is absorbed most completely?
Inorganic additives are about 90% absorbed or more. Module 7.
Question 93Which ingredient indicates a phosphate additive?
Look for “phos” in the ingredient list. Module 7.
Question 94Which protein food has the lowest phosphorus-to-protein ratio?
Egg white has about 1 to 2 mg of phosphorus per gram of protein. Module 7.
Question 95A patient with low protein intake and high phosphorus should first:
Remove phosphorus that carries no protein; protect protein intake. Module 7.
Question 96When should a phosphate binder be taken?
A binder acts on the food that is in the gut with it. Module 7.
Question 97A patient asks to take an extra binder with a fiesta meal. You should:
Dose changes belong to the prescriber. Module 7.
Question 98KDIGO suggests that decisions about phosphate-lowering treatment be based on:
Serial measurements and the three values together guide treatment. Module 7.
Question 99The HiLo trial showed that:
Enrollment and phosphate separation were insufficient. Module 7.
Question 100How much elemental calcium is in a 500 mg calcium carbonate tablet?
Calcium carbonate is 40% elemental calcium. Module 7.
Question 101KDOQI suggests a total elemental calcium intake in CKD stages 3 to 4 (not on active vitamin D) of about:
800 to 1,000 mg a day from all sources (a weak suggestion, grade 2B). Module 7.
Question 102Which is a medicine for secondary hyperparathyroidism and not a nutritional supplement?
Calcitriol is active vitamin D; it raises calcium and phosphorus absorption. Module 7.
Question 103Phosphorus rises after a dose increase of an active vitamin D analog. The likely explanation is:
Active vitamin D raises absorption of calcium and phosphorus. Module 7.
Question 104Three sachets of 3-in-1 coffee a day matter for phosphorus because:
Non-dairy creamers often contain phosphate additives. Module 7.
Question 105Boiling meat and discarding the liquid:
Some phosphorus moves into the cooking liquid. Module 7.
Question 106Why does the risk of hypoglycemia rise as CKD advances?
Slower insulin clearance and less kidney glucose release both lower glucose. Module 8.
Question 107A patient received an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent two months ago. Her HbA1c may be:
Young red cells carry less glucose, so HbA1c reads low. Module 8.
Question 108KDIGO’s HbA1c target in diabetes with CKD is:
A firm recommendation (grade 1C) to individualize the target. Module 8.
Question 109Which data best explain recurrent lows before dinner?
Times link each low to a missed meal or a dose. Module 8.
Question 110Two symptomatic lows on insulin this week. The dietitian should:
Recurrent hypoglycemia is a safety issue for the prescribing team. Module 8.
Question 111One cup of cooked rice contains about how much carbohydrate?
About 40 to 45 g; keeping the portion steady helps with fixed insulin doses. Module 8.
Question 112Why is a cola a poor routine drink but sometimes used to treat a low?

Question 113An SGLT2 inhibitor causes glucose to be:

Question 114Who gives sick-day instructions for pausing an SGLT2 inhibitor?

Question 115In the FLOW trial, semaglutide reduced the main kidney and heart outcome by about:

Question 116What should the dietitian watch for in a patient starting a GLP-1 receptor agonist?

Question 117A patient cuts rice for glucose, meat for her kidneys, and fruit for potassium. The main risk is:

Question 118Sarcopenic obesity means:
The scale can hide low muscle. Module 8.

European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism — Publishes the hospital kidney nutrition and micronutrient guidelines.

ICU
Intensive care unit.

Intradialytic parenteral nutrition — Nutrients given into the dialysis circuit during hemodialysis.

International Society of Renal Nutrition and Metabolism — The professional society for kidney nutrition.
KDIGO
Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes — The international nephrology guideline body.

Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative — The National Kidney Foundation guideline program; publishes the 2020 nutrition guideline.
KRT
Kidney replacement therapy — Dialysis in any form, or a transplant.
PD
Peritoneal dialysis.
PDSA
Plan-Do-Study-Act — A small test-of-change cycle used in quality improvement.
PES
Problem, etiology, signs and symptoms — The format of a nutrition diagnosis.
PEW

PhilHealth
Philippine Health Insurance Corporation.
PTH
Parathyroid hormone.

Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system — Hormones that control blood pressure and potassium; blocked by several kidney-protective medicines.
