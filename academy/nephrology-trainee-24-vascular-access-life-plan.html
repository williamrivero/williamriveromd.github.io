Training track for nephrology fellows, residents and medical interns

Study material for medical interns, residents and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and gives no drug doses. Doses, rates and order sets come from current guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved protocols and your supervising physician. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Intern: Protect the arm veins of every patient with advanced chronic kidney disease, recognize an access that has no thrill, is bleeding or shows signs of infection, and report it at once.
  • Resident: Examine an arteriovenous fistula by looking, feeling and listening, recognize inflow and outflow dysfunction and catheter dysfunction or infection, and write a clear referral.
  • Fellow: Plan dialysis access with each patient through the end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) Life-Plan of the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) 2019 guideline, choose between fistula, graft, catheter and peritoneal dialysis, and lead monitoring and timely referral.

Start here

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · guardian of the veinsThe intern protects veins: no blood draws or intravenous lines in the arm chosen for a fistula, and no peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) without asking the nephrology team. The resident examines the access at every dialysis round and admission, and knows a failing fistula before it clots. The fellow plans the access years ahead, with the patient, the surgeon and the family, and keeps the plan written where everyone can see it.
In plain wordsThink of vascular access as the road to a town. A fistula is a paved road that took months to build and lasts for years if it is maintained. A catheter is a temporary bridge put up overnight: useful in an emergency, but it washes out, it lets in trouble, and each one built damages the riverbank so the next one is harder to place.

The picture has a limit. A fistula is not built once and finished. After a surgeon joins an artery to a vein, the vein must dilate and thicken under arterial pressure until it carries enough blood for dialysis. Many never do. And the road can narrow anywhere along its length: at the arterial inflow, at the anastomosis, in the needling segment, or far away in the central veins of the chest, often because of catheters placed months or years before. So the access is a lifelong project that depends on veins that were protected long before anyone thought of dialysis.

Most Philippine patients start hemodialysis through a catheter, often in an emergency, because they reached a nephrologist late or could not afford surgery in time. A few keep that catheter for years and lose central vein after central vein to infection and stenosis. Your work is to plan the access before it is needed, examine it every time you see the patient, and refer early when it begins to fail.

Opening case · fictional

Case: The second catheter this year

It is Wednesday afternoon on the medical ward of a provincial hospital in Batangas City. Mrs. Lourdes Dimaculangan, 54, a sari-sari store owner with diabetes, started hemodialysis 11 months ago through a temporary femoral catheter after she came to the emergency room breathless and uremic. A tunneled right internal jugular catheter followed. No fistula was created: the family could not pay for surgery then, and she was afraid of “having her arm cut.”

Three months ago she was admitted with fever and chills that began during dialysis, with blood cultures growing Staphylococcus aureus. The right catheter was removed and a tunneled left internal jugular catheter placed. Today she is admitted again because the unit could not keep the blood flow up and two sessions ended early. She has a healed PICC scar on her left arm from a pneumonia admission two years ago, before she knew she had kidney disease. Her left arm and left face are a little puffy, and there are dilated veins over her left upper chest.

The fellow asks you, the resident, to examine her access and her arms and to draft a plan for the attending nephrologist.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What does Mrs. Dimaculangan’s course say about her ESKD Life-Plan, and where did it fail?
  • What do you look for in her arms, neck and chest, and what do the swelling and chest veins suggest?
  • What should the fellow propose for her access now and over the next months?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

The ESKD Life-Plan: access as part of a life, not a procedure

The KDOQI 2006 guideline promoted “fistula first.” Fistula rates rose, but so did fistulas that never matured, interventions to rescue them and months spent on catheters waiting. The 2019 update replaced a single rule with a plan for each person. KDOQI considers it reasonable for every patient with chronic kidney disease (CKD) heading toward kidney failure to have an individualized ESKD Life-Plan that is regularly reviewed, updated and documented in the medical record, with an annual review (expert opinion).1 The Life-Plan maps the modalities a person is likely to use over a lifetime (peritoneal dialysis, hemodialysis, transplant, conservative care) and the access each needs, in the order that preserves the most future options.

The reasoning is about sequence. A forearm fistula placed first leaves the upper arm for later. A catheter in the left internal jugular can narrow the left brachiocephalic vein and ruin every future access in the left arm. A patient who starts on peritoneal dialysis keeps both arms untouched for years. A patient with a living donor ready in six months may reasonably skip fistula surgery. KDOQI considers an arteriovenous (AV) access, fistula or graft, reasonable when it is consistent with the Life-Plan and the person’s goals (expert opinion), and lists situations where a tunneled catheter is reasonable: short term, for example while a newly created AV access is not yet ready, and long term, for example when life expectancy is limited (expert opinion).1

Why the access type matters. In a systematic review of 62 cohort studies including 586,337 people, catheter users, compared with fistula users, had higher all-cause mortality (risk ratio 1.53, 95% confidence interval 1.41 to 1.67), fatal infections (2.12) and cardiovascular events (1.38); graft users also had higher mortality than fistula users (1.18).2 The authors rated the risk of selection bias as high: sicker patients are more likely to have catheters, so these numbers describe association, not the effect of switching a given patient.

Why the fistula is not automatic. A meta-analysis of 46 studies found a primary failure rate of 23% for new fistulas; primary patency, counting those failures, was 60% at 1 year and 51% at 2 years, and secondary patency 71% and 64%.3 A fistula is the best access when it works, and a fistula plan must include what happens when it does not.

KDOQI suggests referral for access assessment and creation when the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is 15 to 20 mL/min/1.73 m² in patients with progressive CKD, and selective preoperative ultrasound vessel mapping in patients at high risk of access failure (conditional, low-quality evidence); the referral threshold itself is expert opinion.1 In the Philippines, where cost delays surgery and many patients first meet a nephrologist in the emergency room, the referral often comes too late. The Life-Plan conversation belongs in the CKD clinic, at the first visit with an eGFR under 30.

What you do at your stage. The intern writes “protect left arm: no needles” on the chart of a CKD patient when asked. The resident asks every new dialysis patient what the access plan is. The fellow writes the Life-Plan in the chart: current modality and access, the next access and its timing, transplant status, and the date of the next review.

RememberEvery patient heading to dialysis needs a written plan for this access, the next access and the one after. A catheter without a plan is how central veins are lost.
Lesson · part 2

Look, feel, listen: examining a fistula

A fistula is a high-flow, low-resistance circuit. Blood leaves the artery at the anastomosis, rushes into the vein and returns to the heart. A healthy fistula is soft, with a continuous thrill that you feel and a continuous low-pitched bruit that you hear through systole and diastole. A narrowing changes the physics. Upstream of a narrowing the vein is under higher pressure, so it becomes pulsatile and does not collapse; downstream it is flat. A narrowing in the inflow, on the arterial side, gives a weak, flat fistula that fills poorly.

Physical examination is accurate enough to matter. In 142 patients referred for fistula dysfunction, examination before angiography identified outflow stenosis with a sensitivity of 92% and a specificity of 86%, and inflow stenosis with 85% and 71%, against an independent, blinded reading of the angiogram.4 This was a referred population with suspected problems, examined by experienced interventionists, so the accuracy in routine rounds by trainees is likely lower. But the skill is learnable, cheap and available in every province.

Figure 3 · Fistula examination at the chair

  1. LookArm swelling, collateral veins on the chest or shoulder, aneurysms, shiny or thin skin over needling sites, redness, discharge, scabs that do not heal. Compare both arms.
  2. FeelA continuous thrill at the anastomosis. Strong pulsation instead of a thrill suggests a downstream (outflow) narrowing. A weak, flat vessel suggests an inflow problem.
  3. Arm elevation testRaise the arm above the heart. A healthy fistula collapses. One that stays distended suggests outflow stenosis.
  4. Augmentation testOcclude the fistula a few centimeters above the anastomosis. The pulse below should strengthen. A weak augmentation suggests inflow stenosis.
  5. ListenA continuous low-pitched bruit is normal. A high-pitched or systolic-only bruit suggests a narrowing.
  6. Check the handColor, warmth, capillary refill, sensation and pain, especially during dialysis.

KDOQI 2019 recommends regular physical examination or check of the fistula by a knowledgeable and experienced practitioner to detect dysfunction, and treats clinical monitoring as primary and surveillance findings as supplementary.1 For grafts, KDOQI does not suggest routine surveillance by flow measurement, pressure monitoring or imaging in addition to clinical monitoring (conditional, low-quality evidence); for fistulas it found insufficient evidence to make a suggestion.1 A new fistula should be evaluated for maturation by an appropriate member of the access team by 4 to 6 weeks after creation (expert opinion).1

What you do at your stage. The intern checks for a thrill and reports its absence at once. The resident examines the fistula in this order at every round and documents findings in words, not “arteriovenous fistula (AVF) functioning.” The fellow correlates the examination with the dialysis flow sheet and refers for imaging or intervention when findings and alarms agree.

RememberExamine the fistula the same way every time: look, feel, raise the arm, augment, listen, check the hand. A narrowing is usually felt before it clots.
Lesson · part 3

Catheters: the bridge, its course and its dangers

A tunneled cuffed hemodialysis catheter enters a central vein, usually the right internal jugular, runs down through the brachiocephalic vein and superior vena cava, and ends with its tip in the right atrium or at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium. The other end travels under the skin of the chest to an exit site several centimeters away, where a cuff in the tunnel becomes fixed by scar tissue and blocks bacteria from tracking along the outside. Two lumens carry blood out and back. KDOQI lists the internal jugular as the preferred site, followed by external jugular, femoral, subclavian and lumbar, and considers the right side preferable to the left because the course to the atrium is straighter (expert opinion).1

The dangers follow from the anatomy. The catheter is a foreign body in the bloodstream with a direct path from the skin. Infection can start at the exit site, track along the tunnel, or colonize the lumen and seed the blood every time it is opened. The catheter rubs the vein wall and provokes thrombosis and stenosis of the central veins, which may not show until a fistula on that side swells the arm. Fibrin sheath and thrombus at the tip reduce flow, so K falls and the dose with it.

KDOQI defines catheter dysfunction as failure to maintain the prescribed extracorporeal blood flow required for adequate hemodialysis (expert opinion), and suggests locking solutions chosen to help prevent infection and dysfunction where feasible (conditional, low-quality evidence); the lock and its concentration come from the unit’s protocol.1 The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) 2009 guideline describes how to diagnose catheter-related bloodstream infection with paired blood cultures and how to decide on catheter removal by the infecting microbe and course; Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia is among the infections in which a hemodialysis catheter is usually removed and complications such as endocarditis must be sought.5 Antibiotic choice and duration come from that guideline, the unit’s protocol and the attending.

What you do at your stage. The intern never uses a dialysis catheter for blood draws or infusions unless the unit protocol and the nephrology team allow it. The resident examines the exit site and tunnel at every round, and takes blood cultures before the first antibiotic when a catheter patient has fever. The fellow counts catheter days, decides with the attending when to remove a catheter, and treats every catheter as a bridge with an end date.

RememberA catheter is a bridge with an end date. Write the date and the destination in the chart the day it goes in.
Lesson · part 4

Recognizing dysfunction and referring in time

An access fails gradually and then suddenly. A narrowing grows over weeks; blood flow falls, recirculation rises, the Kt/V drifts down, bleeding after needle removal lengthens, and then one morning there is no thrill. The goal is to act in the weeks before that morning, because a thrombosed fistula may not be salvageable and the patient returns to a catheter.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist, per unit protocol, but always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance, when:
  • A fistula or graft has lost its thrill and bruit: thrombosis is time-sensitive.
  • An access bleeds and does not stop with firm pressure, or an aneurysm has thin, shiny or ulcerated skin.
  • A catheter patient has fever, rigors or hypotension at the start of dialysis.
  • The hand on the access side is cold, pale, numb or painful.
  • Redness, pus or tenderness tracks along a catheter tunnel or over a graft.

In the Philippines, intervention depends on what is near. Some provinces have a vascular surgeon but no interventional radiology, or the reverse; many patients pay for angiography themselves. The fellow’s task is to know the local pathway before it is needed: who can see a failing fistula within days, who can declot one, and what it costs. The Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) 2024 hemodialysis guidelines set the local standards that units follow for access care.6

What you do at your stage. The resident brings the flow sheet and the examination together in one sentence: “Venous pressures rising for three weeks, bleeding 25 minutes after needle removal, pulsatile fistula that does not collapse on arm elevation.” The fellow refers with that sentence, agrees on urgency with the attending, and tells the patient what will happen if nothing is done.

RememberAct on the trend, not on the clot. Three weeks of rising pressures and longer bleeding are a referral, not a watching brief.
Lesson · part 5

Preserving veins, starting in the ward

Every future fistula depends on veins that someone else may have used up. Venipuncture, intravenous cannulas and especially PICCs injure the endothelium of arm veins; PICCs and subclavian lines narrow the central veins that every arm access drains through. A patient with CKD stage 4 whose cephalic vein was used for a week of antibiotics may have lost the best fistula site before ever seeing a nephrologist.

KDOQI considers it reasonable to protect all central and peripheral arteries and veins from damage whenever possible, and to avoid peripherally inserted catheters and unnecessary venipunctures in patients with CKD (expert opinion).1 These are expert-opinion statements, but the physiology is not in dispute, and in the Philippines the cost of losing a site is a catheter that the family may pay for again and again.

In childrenSmall vessels make fistulas harder to create and slower to mature in children, and many expect an early living-related transplant, so catheters are used more often. In the International Pediatric Hemodialysis Network registry of 552 children, 628 catheters and 225 fistulas were placed; catheters gave lower blood flows and Kt/V, and infections were reported only with catheters.7 See Attending Pediatric Nephrologist, Module 24.

What you do at your stage. The intern asks before placing a line in a CKD patient’s arm and uses the hand veins for blood draws. The resident questions every PICC order for a patient with an eGFR under 30. The fellow teaches the ward, writes vein preservation into admission orders for CKD patients, and makes sure the dialysis unit knows which arm is reserved.

RememberThe fistula a patient has in three years is decided by the needle a ward team uses today. Ask before you puncture an arm vein in CKD.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The PICC order on the ward

What you see
A resident in Davao is asked to approve a PICC for two weeks of antibiotics in a man with CKD stage 4 and an eGFR of 22.
What is likely going on
A PICC would injure the arm and central veins that a future fistula needs.
What to do
Call the nephrology fellow; consider peripheral hand-vein cannulas or a small-bore internal jugular line per hospital practice; label the arm to be saved.
Why it matters
KDOQI considers avoiding PICCs in CKD reasonable; the cost of a lost site is years of catheters.

The fistula with no thrill

What you see
At 6 a.m. in a Cebu unit, a nurse cannot needle a fistula that worked on Saturday; there is no thrill and no bruit.
What is likely going on
Acute thrombosis.
What to do
Call the fellow and attending now; contact the surgeon or interventional team the same day; arrange temporary access only if dialysis cannot wait.
Why it matters
Salvage depends on time; every hour of delay makes declotting less likely.

The hand that hurts during dialysis

What you see
A woman in Tarlac with a new upper arm fistula says her fingers go numb and cold by the second hour of dialysis.
What is likely going on
Access-related hand ischemia (steal).
What to do
Examine the hand and pulses, record capillary refill and sensation, and refer to the vascular surgeon promptly; report worsening at once.
Why it matters
Untreated ischemia can cause nerve damage and tissue loss.

The arm that swells with every session

What you see
A man in Naga with a left forearm fistula and a history of two left-sided catheters has an arm that swells more each week.
What is likely going on
Central vein stenosis from previous catheters.
What to do
Examine for chest collaterals, document both arms, and refer for venography with the attending.
Why it matters
A central narrowing can make the whole arm unusable for access.
Field notes
  • Write findings, not verdicts: “continuous thrill, collapses on elevation” instead of “AVF good.”
  • Keep a list of who in your province can see a failing fistula within days, and what it costs.
  • Put the catheter insertion date and the planned removal date on the first page of the dialysis chart.
  • Ask every new dialysis patient which arm is reserved, and tell the ward.
  • Examine both arms and the chest, not only the access arm.
Case review

Back to the case: The second catheter this year

Where the Life-Plan failed. Mrs. Dimaculangan never had a Life-Plan. She reached a nephrologist in the emergency room, a catheter became the plan, a fistula was deferred for cost and fear, and peritoneal dialysis was apparently not discussed. Each catheter carried infection risk, and her Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia is the outcome that the observational data warn about. The PICC placed two years ago, before her kidney disease was known, likely injured the left arm veins. The plan must now be written: modality options including peritoneal dialysis, the next access, transplant eligibility and a review date.

The examination. Look at both arms, the neck and the chest; feel for veins suitable for a fistula; check the catheter exit site and tunnel for redness, tenderness or pus; and ask about fever at the start of dialysis. Left arm and face swelling with dilated chest veins on the side of a current catheter and an old PICC suggest left brachiocephalic or central vein stenosis or thrombosis, which also explains the poor flow. Document it in words and arrange imaging with the attending.

What the fellow proposes. Restore adequate dialysis now through catheter assessment or exchange per the unit protocol and the interventional team. Then plan permanent access: vessel mapping with ultrasound, favoring the right arm if the central veins on that side are open, or a graft if the veins are poor, with a referral date set before discharge. Offer peritoneal dialysis honestly as an option that spares her veins. Explain to her and her family what a fistula operation involves, what it costs and what Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) and the hospital can cover, and that each catheter makes the next access harder.

Question 1 of 5An intern is asked to draw blood from a patient with CKD stage 4 whose chart says “save left arm.” What is the best action?
KDOQI considers it reasonable to protect arm veins and avoid peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) and unnecessary venipunctures in CKD (expert opinion). A PICC injures arm and central veins and can ruin future access.
Question 2 of 5A resident examines a fistula that is strongly pulsatile and stays distended when the arm is raised above the heart. Venous pressure alarms have increased. What is most likely?
A narrowing downstream raises pressure upstream, so the vein becomes pulsatile and does not collapse on elevation. Inflow stenosis gives a flat, weak fistula with poor augmentation.
Question 3 of 5A fellow plans access for a 45-year-old man with progressive CKD, eGFR 18 mL/min/1.73 m², good forearm veins and no living donor. Which plan best follows KDOQI 2019?
KDOQI suggests referral for access when eGFR is 15–20 (expert opinion) and an individualized, documented Life-Plan. A forearm fistula first preserves the upper arm; waiting for a catheter start is what the plan is meant to avoid.
Question 4 of 5A patient with a tunneled catheter develops rigors and a temperature of 39°C 20 minutes into dialysis. The exit site looks clean. What should the resident do first?
Fever or rigors soon after connection suggests catheter-related bloodstream infection even with a clean exit site. Cultures come before antibiotics; removal and antibiotics are decided with the attending per IDSA guidance and the unit protocol.
Question 5 of 5A resident presents the Ravani systematic review (62 cohort studies). Which statement is accurate?
The review pooled observational cohorts; sicker patients more often have catheters, so the association does not show the effect of switching a given patient. Grafts were also associated with higher mortality than fistulas (1.18).
