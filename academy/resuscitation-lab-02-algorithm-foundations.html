Team resuscitation practice for dialysis units

This module is study material for dialysis teams. It explains the reasoning behind resuscitation decisions; it is not a unit protocol, an order set, or certification. Doses and energies are quoted from the 2025 American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines and the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) where named, so you can recognize them; give medicines only within your license and your unit’s approved protocol. Escalation always follows your unit protocol, and you always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Every case, name, and identifier is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Describe any resuscitation algorithm with the same ten parts: trigger, assessment, branches, first actions, parallel tasks, reassessment, transitions, common errors, dialysis considerations, and drill.
  • Use five decision questions (responsive, breathing normally, definite pulse, shockable rhythm, stable) to enter the right algorithm.
  • Explain why each branch point changes treatment, using the physiology behind it.
  • Plan the parallel tasks of a seven-person team across one two-minute cycle.
  • Recognize the moments when a patient moves from one algorithm to another, and announce the transition aloud.

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsAn algorithm is like a road map with junctions. You do not memorize every street; you learn to read the signs at each junction. Is the person awake? Breathing? Is there a pulse? Will a shock help? Is the circulation holding? Each answer sends you down one road, and each road has a short list of first moves.

The picture has a limit. Maps assume the road stays still. Patients do not. A man with a slow pulse can lose it while you prepare the pacer; a woman in arrest can regain a pulse during the next two minutes of compressions. So the real skill is not following one road to its end. It is reading the signs again at every pause, and changing roads the moment the answer changes.

This module teaches the shape every algorithm in the course shares. Once you see that shape, the adult arrest, bradycardia, tachycardia and pediatric algorithms stop being separate charts to memorize. They become variations on five questions.

Opening case · fictional

Case: Box by box

At 10:15 a.m., Mrs. Remedios Aquino, 72, is two hours into her session at a hospital-based dialysis unit in Iloilo City. She has been on hemodialysis for six years and takes a beta-blocker. Nurse Mr. Carlo Medina notices she is drowsy. Her heart rate on the monitor is 38 per minute and her blood pressure is 78/40 mm Hg. She opens her eyes to her name and says she feels faint.

Dr. Ana Villaruel, the physician-on-duty, arrives with the defibrillator. While the team attaches pads and prepares atropine, Mrs. Aquino stops answering. Her breathing becomes slow gasps. Mr. Medina cannot feel a carotid pulse.

Before reading on, decide:

  • Which algorithm was the team in at 10:15, and which one are they in now?
  • What should be said aloud at the moment of transition?
  • Which tasks happen at the same time in the next 60 seconds, and who does each?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

One frame for every algorithm

Algorithm charts are compressed. They show boxes and arrows, but not why each box exists or who does it. This course unpacks every algorithm into the same ten parts so you can learn one and recognize the rest.

Lesson · part 2

Five questions that sort the floor

Almost every emergency on the dialysis floor can be placed in the right algorithm by five questions asked in order. Each question exists because its answer changes the physiology you are treating.

QuestionWhy it changes treatmentIf the answer is no
1 Responsive?A responsive patient has a brain that is still perfusedShout for help; check breathing and pulse together
2 Breathing normally?Gasping is a sign of a brain without blood flow, not breathingTreat gasping as not breathing
3 Definite pulse within 10 seconds?No pulse means no circulation: only compressions move bloodStart compressions: cardiac arrest
4 Shockable rhythm?VF and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT) are chaotic electricity that a shock can reset; asystole and pulseless electrical activity (PEA) are notNonshockable path: epinephrine as soon as feasible, find the cause
5 Stable? (when there is a pulse)Low pressure, confusion, shock, chest pain or heart failure mean the rhythm is starving organs nowUnstable: treat the rhythm immediately (atropine or pacing; synchronized cardioversion)

The AHA recommends that pulse checks take no more than 10 seconds, and that compressions restart immediately if no pulse is definitely felt (Class of Recommendation (COR) 1, Level of Evidence (LOE) C-LD).1 The word “definitely” matters: a weak, uncertain pulse in a gasping patient is treated as no pulse. Compressions on a patient who turns out to have a pulse rarely cause serious harm; withholding them from a patient in arrest is fatal.

The fifth question uses signs, not numbers. The 2025 adult advanced life support (ALS) guideline describes instability as hypotension, acutely altered mental status, signs of shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure caused by the rhythm.2 A heart rate of 38 is not itself unstable; a heart rate of 38 with a pressure of 78/40 and drowsiness is.

RememberResponsive, breathing normally, definite pulse, shockable, stable. Ask them in order, out loud, every time.
Lesson · part 3

One chart, seven people, at the same time

Algorithm charts read from top to bottom, which suggests one action after another. A real team works in parallel. While one person compresses, another applies pads, a third manages the airway, a fourth draws up epinephrine, a fifth manages the circuit, a sixth records, and a seventh watches the other patients. The leader’s job is to keep these lanes moving together and to make the decisions at each branch.

At 2 minutesSwaps compressorCharges before the pause if shockableReports circuit safeLeads a pause under 10 seconds

The leader stays hands-off when staffing allows. A leader who is also compressing cannot see the monitor, the clock and the team at once. In a three-person team this is not possible, and Module 13 shows which roles combine safely.

RememberThe chart is sequential; the team is parallel. The leader keeps the lanes moving and owns every branch decision.
Lesson · part 4

When the patient changes roads

The most common recognition error is staying in the wrong algorithm after the patient has changed. A team preparing a pacer for bradycardia keeps working on the pacer after the pulse is gone. A team in arrest keeps compressing after a strong pulse has returned because nobody checked. Every reassessment is a chance to ask the five questions again.

FromChangeToFirst move
Bradycardia or tachycardia with a pulsePulse lost, not breathing normallyCardiac arrestCompressions now
Cardiac arrestOrganized rhythm and a definite pulsePost-ROSC careCheck pressure, oxygen, electrocardiogram (ECG)
Cardiac arrest, nonshockableVF appears at a rhythm checkShockable pathShock
Post-ROSCPulse lost againCardiac arrestCompressions now
Deterioration with a pulse (child)Heart rate under 60 with poor perfusion despite good ventilationPediatric cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)Compressions

Say the transition aloud: “No pulse. We are now in cardiac arrest. Start compressions.” This closes the old plan for everyone at once. The recorder writes the time, and the leader re-names the roles if needed.

RememberAt every reassessment, ask the five questions again. When the answer changes, announce the new algorithm aloud.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

“He has a rhythm”

What you see
During a drill a nurse stops compressions because the monitor shows an organized rhythm.
What is likely going on
The rhythm was taken for a pulse; nobody felt one.
What to do
Feel for a definite pulse within 10 seconds; if none, resume compressions at once.
Why it matters
An organized rhythm without a pulse is PEA, not recovery.

Two leaders

What you see
The physician-on-duty arrives mid-arrest and starts giving orders while the Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) nurse is still leading.
What is likely going on
Leadership changed without a handover.
What to do
The arriving physician asks for a 15-second summary, then says aloud “I am taking the lead.”
Why it matters
Two voices giving orders cause missed and duplicated actions.

The silent numbers

What you see
A tachycardic patient at 160 per minute is alert and talking, and a staff member calls it unstable.
What is likely going on
The number was mistaken for instability.
What to do
Check the five instability signs; if none, monitor, record an ECG and call the physician.
Why it matters
Treatment is driven by signs of compromise, not the rate alone.
Field notes
  • Practice saying the five questions aloud until it is automatic.
  • Make “announce the algorithm” part of every drill debrief.
Case review

Back to the case: Box by box

The two algorithms. At 10:15 Mrs. Aquino was in the bradycardia-with-a-pulse algorithm, and she was unstable: heart rate 38, pressure 78/40, drowsy. The right moves were monitor, oxygen, IV access, atropine as ordered, and preparing to pace, while looking for causes: potassium, the beta-blocker, ischemia. When she stopped responding, began gasping, and had no definite pulse, she moved to cardiac arrest.

The words. Dr. Villaruel says: “No pulse. Cardiac arrest. Carlo, compressions now. Chair flat.” The atropine and pacer plan stops. The recorder notes the time.

The next 60 seconds, in parallel. Mr. Medina compresses; a second nurse takes over the bag-mask; the pads already on the chest go to analysis; the circuit nurse stops fluid removal and the blood pump and announces the blood decision; the runner calls the hospital code team; the watcher stays with the other patients; and the leader asks, “Potassium? When was her last result?” Slow rhythm plus a beta-blocker in a dialysis patient raises bradycardia, renal failure, atrioventricular nodal blockade, shock, and hyperkalemia (BRASH) and hyperkalemia (Modules 6 and 8).

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Five questions and an announced transition

During a facilitated scenario, lead the first two minutes of an evolving case while a supervisor observes. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • CriticalAsks the five decision questions aloud in order: responsive, breathing normally, definite pulse, shockable, stable.
  • CriticalLimits the pulse check to 10 seconds and treats an uncertain pulse with gasping as no pulse.
  • Judges stability by signs (pressure, mental status, shock, chest pain, heart failure), not by heart rate alone.
  • Assigns parallel tasks by name within the first 30 seconds.
  • CriticalRecognizes a change at reassessment and announces the new algorithm aloud.
  • CriticalStops the previous plan (for example, pacing) when the patient enters cardiac arrest.
Placement activity. Shadow the physician-on-duty for one shift and, for each patient event, name the algorithm aloud afterward and the question that placed it there.
Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 5 questions at random from this module’s 10-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 4 of 5 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 10What are the five decision questions, in order?
Best: these five sort almost every floor emergency into the right algorithm. Why not the others: vital signs or the ABCDE survey are useful but do not place the patient in an algorithm; the last option asks rhythm questions before checking for arrest.
Question 2 of 10A patient is gasping and you are unsure whether you feel a pulse after 10 seconds. What do you do?
Best: gasping is not normal breathing, and an uncertain pulse is treated as no pulse; pulse checks last no more than 10 seconds (AHA COR 1). Why not the others: longer checks delay compressions; oxygen and blood pressure do not move blood in arrest.
Question 3 of 10Why does the ‘shockable rhythm?’ question change treatment?
Best: defibrillation stops disorganized electrical activity so a normal rhythm can restart; it has nothing to reset in asystole, and PEA already has organized electricity. Why not the others: shocks are for VF/pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT), not slow rhythms; compressions continue; every arrest needs CPR.
Question 4 of 10A patient with a heart rate of 38 is alert, with a pressure of 128/70 and no symptoms. How do you classify this?
Best: stability is judged by signs, not the number; with no compromise, monitor and find the cause (potassium, drugs). Why not the others: no instability signs means no immediate pacing; there is a pulse; a rate of 38 still needs explanation.
Question 5 of 10What is the leader’s main job during a resuscitation with enough staff?
Best: a hands-off leader can see the monitor, clock and team. Why not the others: compressing or drawing up drugs takes the leader’s eyes off the whole event; recording is a separate role.
Question 6 of 10During pacer setup for bradycardia, the patient stops responding and has no pulse. What should the leader say?
Best: announcing the transition closes the old plan for everyone. Why not the others: pacing and atropine belong to bradycardia with a pulse; waiting withholds compressions.
Question 7 of 10Which finding makes a tachycardia ‘unstable’?
Best: instability means the rhythm is causing organ hypoperfusion: low pressure, altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest pain or acute heart failure. Why not the others: a rate number, palpitations alone or past history do not define instability.
Question 8 of 10In the ten-part frame, what is the ‘trigger’ for the adult cardiac arrest algorithm?
Best: these findings define cardiac arrest. Why not the others: slow rate, low pressure and high potassium are triggers for other algorithms or overlays.
Question 9 of 10What is a common recognition error that this module warns about?
Best: teams keep working on the previous plan, such as pacing after the pulse is gone. Why not the others: the other options are correct habits.
Question 10 of 10Why are the course’s algorithm diagrams original rather than copies of AHA charts?
Best: the content follows 2025 AHA recommendations, but the charts are proprietary course material. Why not the others: the 2025 charts are current; the course does follow AHA; the charts are not physician-only.

Abbreviations

ACLS
Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support.
AHA
American Heart Association.
ALS
Advanced life support — Resuscitation with monitor, defibrillator, drugs and airway beyond basic life support.
BRASH
Bradycardia, renal failure, atrioventricular nodal blockade, shock, and hyperkalemia — A vicious cycle in which potassium and AV-nodal drugs together slow the heart.
COR
Class of Recommendation — The AHA grade of how strongly a guideline recommends an action (1, 2a, 2b, 3).
CPR
Cardiopulmonary resuscitation.
ECG
Electrocardiogram.
IV
Intravenous.
LOE
Level of Evidence — The AHA grade of the quality of evidence behind a recommendation (A, B-R, B-NR, C-LD, C-EO).
PEA
Pulseless electrical activity — An organized rhythm on the monitor with no palpable pulse. Not shockable.
pVT
Pulseless ventricular tachycardia — Ventricular tachycardia with no pulse; treated like VF. Shockable.
ROSC
Return of spontaneous circulation — The heart pumps on its own again: a definite pulse and blood pressure.
VF
Ventricular fibrillation — Chaotic electrical activity of the ventricles; no pumping and no pulse. Shockable.
VT
Ventricular tachycardia — A fast rhythm from the ventricles; may have a pulse or not.

Terms in plain words

Algorithm
A structured sequence of assessments, decisions and actions for one problem.
Branch point
A question whose answer changes the next action.
Reassessment
A planned check of the patient after an action or interval.
Transition
Moving to another algorithm because the patient changed.
Agonal breathing
Gasping breaths of a brain without blood flow; treated as not breathing.
Instability
Signs that the rhythm is starving organs: low pressure, confusion, shock, chest pain, heart failure.
