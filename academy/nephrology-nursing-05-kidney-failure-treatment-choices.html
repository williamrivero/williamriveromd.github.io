Kidney Failure, Treatment Choices, and Conservative Care

The four pathways for a person approaching kidney failure, how nurses support a decision that belongs to the patient, and how cost, distance, and family shape what is realistic in the Philippines.

Self-study for registered nurses

This module is self-study for registered nurses. It explains the reasoning behind safe practice; it does not replace the physician’s orders, your unit’s approved procedures, or the manufacturer’s instructions for the equipment you use. Every case is fictional. A quiz score records self-assessment only; practice competencies are observed and signed off by a clinical preceptor or training provider.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Describe the four treatment pathways for kidney failure and what daily life looks like on each.
  • Explain shared decision-making and the nurse’s role in it.
  • Use teach-back to confirm understanding without testing the patient.
  • Identify practical barriers such as transport, cost, housing, and caregiver availability, and coordinate a support referral.
  • Introduce advance care planning as a routine part of kidney care.
  • Explain when kidney replacement is started and why the decision is not made on a laboratory value alone.
  • Compare the pathways across the domains that matter to patients.
  • Outline the preparation timeline for each pathway.
  • Describe symptom care within conservative kidney management and care at the end of life.
Intuition

Four roads, one traveler

A person whose kidneys are failing stands at a junction with four roads. Each leads somewhere different, each has a toll, and none is right for everyone. You do not choose the road. You are the guide who makes sure the traveler can see all four, knows what a day on each would be like, and has said out loud what matters most before the first step.

The picture has a limit. These roads are not fixed: people move between treatments over a lifetime. And a traveler walks alone, while many Filipino patients decide with their families.

Many patients are shown only one road, usually in-center hemodialysis, and often in an emergency. Your work is to give a fair view of every road while there is still time to choose.

Describe every suitable road in the same way, so that none sounds like the default.

Physiology

What each treatment replaces

Healthy kidneys do two kinds of work. They clean the blood around the clock of water, wastes, potassium, and acid. They also act as glands, making erythropoietin, which drives red cell production, and activating vitamin D. Kidney replacement therapy (KRT) substitutes for part of that work. Dialysis provides only a fraction of the cleaning and none of the gland work, which is why patients on dialysis still need treatment for anemia and bone health. A working transplant restores both.

The fourth pathway is often misunderstood as “doing nothing.” A Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) conference defined comprehensive conservative care as planned, holistic, person-centered care for people with stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD). It includes measures to slow progression, active symptom management, advance care planning, and psychological, social, and spiritual support. It does not include dialysis.1

Each pathway replaces a different share of the kidney’s work and asks a different price. Name both.

The diet row follows from the schedule. Daily peritoneal dialysis removes fluid and potassium a little at a time, every day. In-center hemodialysis removes them in a few sessions a week, so everything the patient takes in between sessions accumulates until the next one.

The pathways are neither exclusive nor permanent. Many people move between them over a lifetime: peritoneal dialysis (PD) first, then hemodialysis (HD); dialysis while waiting for a transplant; back to dialysis if a transplant fails; or a trial of dialysis followed by a decision to stop. Saying so relieves some of the fear of choosing wrongly.

No choice is final. Tell the patient so, early.

Evidence

What is known, and what is not

Transplantation. For suitable candidates, transplantation is associated with longer survival and better quality of life than staying on dialysis. The KDIGO 2020 guideline on transplant candidates advises that people with advanced CKD be considered for it, with referral before dialysis is needed where possible.2 The comparisons are observational, because patients cannot be randomized to a transplant, and listed patients are healthier on average. The benefit is nonetheless large and consistent.

Hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis. Observational studies show broadly similar survival on the two. No trial shows that one is better for all patients. A KDIGO conference therefore concluded that the choice should rest on the patient’s preferences, circumstances, and medical factors.3

Dialysis or conservative care in the frail. For frail older adults with several serious illnesses, observational studies suggest that the survival advantage of dialysis over conservative care narrows, and may be offset by time spent in hospital. This is uncertain. It is a reason for an honest conversation, not a rule.1

Teach-back. In teach-back, the patient restates the information in their own words. A systematic review of 20 studies, mostly of moderate quality and varied in design, found teach-back effective in 19 of them. The outcomes ranged from knowledge recall to hospital readmissions and quality of life.4 Remembering information is a learning outcome. It is a step toward better self-care, not proof of better health. Teach-back costs minutes and no equipment.

Where evidence is thin, say so to the patient. An honest “we do not know” is part of a fair choice.

Evidence

When treatment starts, and how far ahead to prepare

Starting is a clinical decision, not a number. The IDEAL trial randomized 828 adults with advanced chronic kidney disease in Australia and New Zealand. One group was to start dialysis at an estimated filtration rate of 10 to 14 mL/min, the other at 5 to 7. Over a median of 3.6 years, 37.6% of the early group and 36.6% of the late group died (hazard ratio 1.04). Three in four of those assigned to wait started before reaching the lower level, because symptoms appeared.5

The KDIGO 2024 guideline on chronic kidney disease therefore advises starting dialysis on a combined assessment of symptoms, signs, quality of life, and preferences. Its triggers include persistent nausea and weight loss, fluid overload or blood pressure that cannot be controlled, itching, cognitive slowing, and inflammation of the lining of the heart. These often appear when the estimated filtration rate is between 5 and 10 mL/min/1.73 m². These are practice points, which KDIGO does not grade.6,3

Patients are often told a number at which “dialysis will be needed,” and they wait for it in dread. A more accurate message is that the team is watching how they feel and function as well as the tests, and that preparation happens early so the start can be planned.

An unplanned start, through a temporary catheter after an emergency admission, is common. It is associated with more infections, longer hospital stays, and worse early survival than a planned start, although patients who start unplanned are often sicker to begin with. Each line in the table is a way of preventing one.

Prepare early so that the start can wait for the patient, not for an emergency.

Clinical application

Educate, elicit, coordinate

The decision belongs to the patient. Your work has five parts.

Educate without steering. Present every pathway that is medically possible for this patient, in plain language, with what a typical week would look like. Use the same structure for each, so that none sounds like the default.

Elicit what matters. Ask, “What is most important to you in the coming year?” and “What worries you most about treatment?” Invite the family members the patient wants present. In many Filipino families the decision is shared, and the person who will drive, cook, or perform exchanges needs to hear the same information.

Confirm with teach-back. A good prompt puts the burden on you, the teacher: “I have covered a lot. To check that I explained it clearly, can you tell me in your own words how peritoneal dialysis would fit into your day?” If the answer shows a gap, explain it a different way and ask again.

Ask about barriers. A plan lasts only if the patient can live it. Ask about the distance to the nearest center, the fare and travel time for every session, who can come along, work schedules, reliable water and electricity at home, and out-of-pocket cost. The Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) provides benefit packages for dialysis and transplantation, and these change. Refer to the social worker or the unit’s benefits staff for current entitlements instead of quoting figures from memory.

Open the door to planning. Advance care planning is a conversation about goals and about who should speak for the patient if they cannot. It belongs early, while the patient is well enough to think, and it is revisited when health changes. Your part is to treat it as routine, document what is said, and tell the physician.

Record the patient’s own words. They are the evidence that the choice was theirs.

Clinical application

Structuring the conversation

Shared decision-making can be organized in three steps. Each has its own task for you.

  1. Introduce the choice. Make clear that there is a decision to make, that there is more than one reasonable option, and that the patient's own priorities will guide it. Many patients do not realize they have a say.
  2. Describe the options. Use the same headings for each, as in the table above. Check understanding as you go with teach-back. Use pictures, a visit to the unit, or a conversation with a patient already on that treatment where these can be arranged.
  3. Help the patient weigh them. Ask what a good day looks like, what they most want to keep doing, and what they most want to avoid. Reflect back what you hear: “It sounds as though staying at home matters more to you than avoiding daily tasks.” Then give time. A decision does not have to be made in one visit.

In Filipino families, decisions are often made together, and adult children may ask that bad news be kept from a parent out of love. The respectful approach is to ask the patient directly, early and privately, how much they want to know and who they want involved. Some will say, “Tell my daughter and she will decide.” That is a choice the patient is entitled to make, and you document it. A patient who wants to know is told, even when the family is reluctant, with the physician leading the discussion.

Common fears worth naming. “Dialysis is a death sentence.” “I will be a burden.” “The needles.” “We cannot afford it.” “If I choose no dialysis, the doctors will abandon me.” Each has a factual answer and an emotional one. Patients rarely raise them unless asked.

Financial and practical support in the Philippines. Sources of help typically include the national health insurance benefit packages, government medical-assistance programs reached through hospital social service and assistance desks, local government and charity support, and registration as a person with disability, which brings discounts. Programs and amounts change. Your role is a prompt referral to the social worker with the patient's documents ready, not a quotation of figures.

Ask the patient first, privately, how much they want to know and who should decide with them.

Clinical application

Conservative management and care at the end of life

Conservative kidney management (CKM) is active care. Kidney-protective treatment, anemia treatment, and blood pressure and fluid control continue for as long as they help the person feel well. What changes is the aim: comfort and function, not laboratory targets.1 The KDIGO 2024 guideline lists support for comprehensive conservative management, for people who choose not to start kidney replacement, as an ungraded practice point.6

Advance care planning records what the person would and would not want if they became too ill to speak, and who should speak for them. Useful openings are: “What do you understand about where your illness is heading?” “What worries you most?” “If your health got worse, what would matter most to you?” Document the patient's words, tell the physician, and make sure the family knows where the record is.

Stopping dialysis. Some people decide that the burdens of dialysis have come to outweigh its benefits. This is a recognized and lawful choice made with the physician, the patient, and the family. It is different from suicide or abandonment. After dialysis stops, death usually follows within days to a few weeks, depending on remaining kidney function. It is most often peaceful, with increasing drowsiness. Families need to hear this in advance, along with what will be done for breathlessness, restlessness, itch, and secretions, and whom to call at night. Palliative or hospice services are involved wherever they are available.

A time-limited trial is a middle path when the outlook is uncertain. Dialysis is started for an agreed period, with agreed signs of benefit, and reviewed on a set date. It lets a hesitant patient try treatment without feeling the decision is irreversible.1

Choosing no dialysis is not choosing no care. Assess every symptom at every contact.

Nursing case · fictional

Case: Three hours from the nearest center

Lola Corazon Mendoza, 71, has stage 5 CKD. Her nephrologist has told her she will need treatment within months. She lives with her daughter, Rowena, in an upland barangay about three hours by jeepney and tricycle from the nearest dialysis center. Rowena sells vegetables at the market six days a week.

It is Tuesday afternoon at the clinic. Your senior nurse, Ms. Grace Villanueva, asks you to lead the education session. Lola Corazon sits down and speaks before you do: “The doctor said dialysis. My neighbor did that and died after a year. I don’t want to be a burden.”

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, what you would communicate and to whom, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the outline below.

Assess

What she already understands. Her goals and fears. The home: space, water, and electricity. Who is available to help. Travel time and cost.

Communicate

Tell the nephrologist what she said about being a burden and about her neighbor, and that travel is a major barrier. Ask whether PD or conservative care has been discussed.

Teach

Every medically suitable pathway in the same plain structure, with Rowena present. Confirm with teach-back.

Escalate

Symptoms of advanced uremia or fluid overload while she is deciding need prompt medical review. Refer to social work for transport and benefits.
Documentation task. Write the teach-back question you would ask after explaining the pathways, and one open question to elicit what matters most to her.

Competencies

Supervised practice

With your preceptor

With your preceptor, observe or co-lead one modality education session and one family meeting. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the unit’s own competency form; the educator page has a printable version.

  • Presents all medically suitable pathways using the same structure for each.
  • Elicits the patient’s priorities with open questions.
  • Uses a teach-back prompt and re-teaches when a gap appears.
  • Identifies at least two practical barriers and makes an appropriate referral.
  • Documents the conversation, including what the patient said in their own words.
