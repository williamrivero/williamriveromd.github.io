Same Event, Every Role

One emergency on the dialysis floor, seen through four pairs of hands

An emergency in a hemodialysis unit is never one person’s event. Each drill follows a single event, phase by phase, through the Hemodialysis Nurse at the chair, the Physician-on-Duty, the technician, and the Head Nurse. Read your own column first. Then read the others, to learn what your colleagues need from you, and when. On a phone, or after you pick one role, you see that role’s actions with a short “Meanwhile” line for what everyone else is doing.

Intradialytic hypotension

Hour 3, Station 7: Mrs. Santos feels faint

Mrs. Santos is 68, has diabetic kidney disease, and has been on hemodialysis three times a week for four years. She arrived 3.6 kg above her target weight of 60 kg and took her amlodipine this morning. The ultrafiltration (UF) goal is 3.8 L over 4 hours. At 2 hours 50 minutes she yawns, says she is dizzy and nauseated, and her blood pressure (BP) has fallen from 148/80 at the start to 84/50. Her heart rate is 104. The patient and the numbers are illustrative.

Gain 3.6 kg (6% of body weight)UF rate 15.8 mL/kg/hDialysate 36.5 °CLast hemoglobin 9.4 g/dLInsulin-treated diabetes

Phase Hemodialysis Nurse Physician-on-Duty Dialysis Technician Head Nurse
Recognize0 to 2 min
  • Recognize the warning signs: yawning, nausea, dizziness, cramps, or restlessness often come before the number drops.
  • Lay her flat with her legs raised, if her breathing allows. This moves venous blood back toward the heart.
  • Set UF to minimum or pause it. The machine is removing plasma faster than fluid can refill from the tissues.
  • Recheck BP now, then every 5 minutes. Look at her: sweating, pallor, confusion, chest pain?

Handoff Call the physician-on-duty (POD) now if systolic BP stays under 90 mm Hg or she has any red-flag symptom. Do not wait until the saline is finished.

Not yet involved
  • Hear the call or the alarm and move to Station 7.
  • Bring the saline line and the glucose meter to the chair without being asked.

Not yet involved
Respond2 to 10 min
  • If she does not recover with positioning and UF off, give an isotonic saline bolus as your unit protocol sets (usually 100 mL), and reassess BP after each bolus.
  • Check capillary blood glucose. She takes insulin, and hypoglycemia can look exactly like hypotension.
  • Report to the POD in SBAR form (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation): weight gain, UF rate, BP trend, what you have given, glucose result.
  • Come to the chair. Confirm she is responsive and breathing; feel the pulse yourself for rate, rhythm, and strength.
  • Look for the causes that are not simply fluid removed too fast: chest pain or a rhythm change (ischemia, arrhythmia), fever or rigors (bacteremia, especially with a catheter), bleeding, raised neck veins or muffled heart sounds (pericardial effusion), dark or cherry-red blood in the lines (hemolysis), or sudden breathlessness after an air alarm (air embolism).
  • Order a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) for chest pain, a rhythm change, or a poor response to volume.

Handoff Tell the nurse exactly what happens next: continue, resume UF at a lower rate, or end the session early. Write the order.
  • Check the machine: the UF goal and rate entered match the order, dialysate temperature and conductivity are right, and there is no air-detector or blood-leak alarm in the history.
  • Keep the lines secure while the nurse repositions her.
  • Bring the ECG machine if the POD asks for it.

Handoff Report any machine finding (a wrong UF entry, temperature, alarm history) to the nurse and the POD at once.
  • See that one nurse and one technician are tied up at Station 7. Reassign that nurse's other patients for the next 15 to 20 minutes so no one else goes unwatched.
Stabilize or escalate10 to 30 min
  • If she recovers (systolic BP above 100 mm Hg and symptoms gone), resume UF at the lower rate the POD ordered. Accept that she may finish above target weight today.
  • If she does not recover after the saline your protocol allows, or develops chest pain, a new arrhythmia, or drowsiness, prepare to return blood and end the treatment.
  • Decide: resume with a smaller UF goal, extend the time, or end the session. Do not push a patient who has just crashed to the original target weight.
  • Consider cooler dialysate (for example 0.5 °C below her body temperature) for the rest of the session.
  • If she has not recovered, or a cardiac, septic, or bleeding cause is suspected, arrange transfer to the emergency room.

Handoff Inform the Attending Nephrologist: what happened, what was done, and the plan for her next session.

Stop and call No response to volume, chest pain, a new arrhythmia, drowsiness, or suspected sepsis: end the treatment and escalate per unit protocol, but always ask the Attending Nephrologist for guidance.
  • If the session is ending, help with blood return and line care as the nurse directs.
  • Save the alarm history and machine settings for the incident report.
  • If she needs transfer, arrange the bed, the transport, and who goes with her.
  • Check that the Attending Nephrologist has been told, as your unit protocol requires.
Prevent the next oneAfter the shift
  • Document the BP trend, symptoms, saline given, UF achieved against UF prescribed, and the end weight.
  • Teach her, once her doctor agrees: when to take amlodipine on dialysis days, how to keep salt and fluid down between sessions, and why a heavy meal on the chair can drop her pressure.
  • Write the event in the treatment record.
  • Flag the questions for the attending: Is her target weight too low? Is her UF rate regularly above 13 mL/kg/h because of large gains? Should antihypertensives be held on dialysis days? Does she need a longer session, an extra session, or cooler dialysate as her standard?
  • Confirm the machine is disinfected and passes its checks before the next patient.
  • Log any equipment fault for the biomedical technician.
  • Review the event at the next huddle, without blame: Was the 3.6 kg gain flagged before connection? Was the morning amlodipine caught at assessment?
  • Track episodes per patient per month as a quality indicator. Repeated episodes mean the prescription needs review, not more saline.

Handoff Send the list of patients with repeated episodes to the Dialysis Clinical Head for the monthly quality meeting.

Study this in your track: Leading the First Minutes of an Emergency

Where it breaks

Waiting for the POD before actingLying the patient flat and pausing UF are nursing actions. Every minute of continued fluid removal deepens the drop.
Giving saline without stopping UFThe machine removes the saline you just gave. Pause UF first.
Assuming every drop is "just fluid"Ischemia, sepsis, bleeding, tamponade, hemolysis, and air embolism can all present as low blood pressure. Ruling them out is the POD's job.
Chasing the original target weightAfter a crash, finishing the full UF goal often brings a second one. A slightly higher end weight today is the safer choice.
Treating it as a one-offRepeated episodes are linked to heart and brain injury and to faster loss of residual kidney function. They point to a prescription problem.

Team debrief

  1. Who set today's UF goal, and was the 3.6 kg gain flagged before the session started?
  2. When did the nurse call the POD, and was that early enough?
  3. Was the glucose checked, and how long did it take?
  4. What will change in her prescription, medicine timing, or teaching before her next session?

Water system alarm

6:10 a.m., first shift: total chlorine is high after the worker carbon tank

Before the first shift, the water treatment technician tests total chlorine at the sample port after the first (worker) carbon tank. It reads 0.3 mg/L; the limit is 0.1 mg/L. Chlorine and chloramine that reach the dialysate cross the dialyzer and damage red cells, causing hemolysis. Twelve patients are due to start in 20 minutes, and two who arrived early are already connected. The unit and the numbers are illustrative.

Limit: total chlorine 0.1 mg/LTwo carbon tanks in series2 patients connected10 more due at 6:30Test kit reads to 0.1 mg/L
Handoff: information passes to another roleStop and call: per unit protocol, but always ask the Attending Nephrologist for guidance
Water system alarm: what each role does, phase by phase
Phase Hemodialysis Nurse Physician-on-Duty Water Treatment Technician Head Nurse
Recognize0 to 2 min
  • Hear the technician's warning. Start no new patient until the water is released in words.

Not yet involved
  • Confirm the reading: total chlorine kit (not free chlorine), within its expiry date, port flushed, color read at the stated time.
  • Test after the second (polisher) tank at once. That result decides what happens next.

Handoff Tell the charge nurse and the head nurse now: "Chlorine is high after the worker tank. Hold all new starts until I give you the polisher result."

Not yet involved
Respond2 to 10 min
  • Hold all new starts.
  • Watch the two connected patients for hemolysis: chest tightness, back pain, breathlessness, nausea, or blood in the venous line that looks dark, port-wine, or translucent cherry-red.
  • Have blood tubes ready in case the POD orders hemolysis tests.

Handoff Report any symptom or abnormal blood color to the POD at once.
  • Come to the unit and get the numbers from the technician yourself: worker tank result, polisher result, and the time of the last normal test.
  • Examine the two connected patients for signs of hemolysis.
  • Polisher at or below 0.1 mg/L: tell the responsible person the worker tank needs replacement, and test more often, as your procedure states. Treatments continue only as the procedure allows.
  • Polisher above 0.1 mg/L: stop. No treatments on this water. Tell the charge nurse and the medical director immediately.
  • Look for the cause: exhausted carbon, a bypass valve left open, a change in the feed water disinfectant, or a flow rate too high for the carbon's contact time.

Handoff Give the POD and the head nurse both numbers, with times, written on the water log.

Stop and call Polisher above 0.1 mg/L: stop all treatments per unit protocol, but always ask the Attending Nephrologist for guidance.
  • Take charge of the floor and make the decision plain to every nurse: hold starts now, and stop treatments if the polisher fails.
  • Have someone start calling the 6:30 patients to delay their arrival.
Stabilize or escalate10 to 30 min
  • If treatments are stopped and the blood looks hemolyzed, do not return it: clamp the lines and wait for the POD's order. Hemolyzed blood carries free hemoglobin and potassium.
  • Draw the tests the POD orders. Keep the patients under observation; do not send anyone home yet.
  • If hemolysis is suspected, order hemoglobin, potassium, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, and plasma free hemoglobin if available, and look at the ECG for signs of high potassium.
  • Decide who goes to the emergency room: a falling hemoglobin, chest pain, high potassium, or unstable vital signs.
  • Decide which patients must still be dialyzed today, and where (high potassium and fluid overload first).

Stop and call Any patient with signs of hemolysis, high potassium, or instability: escalate per unit protocol, but always ask the Attending Nephrologist for guidance.
  • Replace or rebed the failed carbon. Do not release water until both tanks test at or below 0.1 mg/L after flushing.
  • Record every result, its time, and each corrective action on the water log.
  • Notify the Attending Nephrologist, the Dialysis Clinical Head, and the Unit Operations Manager.
  • Rebuild the day: extended hours, a later shift, or referral of the most urgent patients to a partner unit.

Handoff Assign one nurse to call every waiting patient with a new time. High potassium and fluid overload get the first slots.
Prevent the next oneAfter the shift
  • Document, for each affected patient, the symptoms, tests drawn, and whether blood was returned.
  • Recheck hemoglobin at the next session if the POD ordered it.
  • Write each affected patient's event and plan in the record.
  • Ask for follow-up hemoglobin and an anemia review for any patient who hemolyzed.
  • Find the root cause. Was carbon replacement overdue? Did the feed water disinfectant change? Is the flow too high for the carbon volume?
  • Hand any valve or equipment fault to the biomedical technician and record it in the maintenance log.
  • File the incident report and run a no-blame review with the technician, the POD, and the nurses.
  • Confirm the pre-shift chlorine test is done and signed every time, and that the water-failure plan is posted at the nurses' station.

Handoff Report the event and corrective actions to the Dialysis Clinical Head and the Unit Operations Manager for the quality and compliance file.

Physician-on-Duty: If hemolysis is suspected, order hemoglobin, potassium, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, and plasma free hemoglobin if available, and look at the ECG for signs of high potassium.
Head Nurse: Notify the Attending Nephrologist, the Dialysis Clinical Head, and the Unit Operations Manager.

Prevent the next one After the shift

  • Find the root cause. Was carbon replacement overdue? Did the feed water disinfectant change? Is the flow too high for the carbon volume?
  • Hand any valve or equipment fault to the biomedical technician and record it in the maintenance log.

MeanwhileHemodialysis Nurse: Document, for each affected patient, the symptoms, tests drawn, and whether blood was returned.
Physician-on-Duty: Write each affected patient's event and plan in the record.
Head Nurse: File the incident report and run a no-blame review with the technician, the POD, and the nurses.

Where it breaks

Starting patients because the schedule is fullTwelve patients on chloramine-contaminated water is a mass hemolysis event. A late start is always the smaller harm.
Not testing after the polisherThe second carbon tank exists for exactly this moment. Its result decides whether treatments continue or stop.
Returning hemolyzed bloodFree hemoglobin and potassium go straight back into the patient. Clamp and wait for the POD.
Restarting after one good readingBoth tanks must test clear after the carbon is fixed, and testing stays frequent until the cause is known.
Nobody owns the waiting patientsA missed session can harm a patient with high potassium or fluid overload. Someone must sort them by urgency and rebook them.

Team debrief

  2. Who told whom, and did every nurse hear the hold on new starts?
  3. What was the root cause, and what will stop it from happening again?
  4. How long did it take to rebook the waiting patients, and who decided the order?

Running it as a team drill

Use one event at a monthly huddle or skills day. Give each person a role card that is not their usual job: a technician reads the Physician-on-Duty column, a nurse reads the Head Nurse column. Read the scenario aloud, then walk the phases in order, each person saying what they would do and who they would tell. Stop at every handoff and ask the receiving role what they heard. Finish with the debrief questions and one change your unit will make. It takes about 20 minutes.
