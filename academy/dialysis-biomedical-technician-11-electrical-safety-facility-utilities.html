Study series for dialysis biomedical technicians

This module is study material for biomedical technicians in dialysis. It explains how machines work and why maintenance steps exist; it is not a service manual. Repairs, calibrations, and safety tests are done only by people trained and authorized for that machine model, using the manufacturer’s service manual, approved parts, and calibrated test equipment. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Explain why dialysis patients are especially vulnerable to electrical faults.
  • Carry out a visual inspection and an electrical safety test in the correct order.
  • Describe the utilities each station needs and how each can fail.
  • Work with the facility engineer on outlets, grounding, backup power, water, and drains.

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsThink of a new rice cooker plugged into an old, loose outlet with a tangle of extension cords behind it. The cooker may be in perfect condition, but the household is only as safe as the weakest connection in that chain. A dialysis machine is the same. It is safe only as part of a chain: the building’s wiring, the wall outlet, the cord, and the machine’s own protection. This module covers the periodic electrical test on the machine and the utilities at the station that the machine depends on: power, water, drain, and backup supply.

The picture has a limit. A rice cooker touches dry hands for a moment. A dialysis machine is connected to a patient through blood and saline for hours, and a fault too small to notice at home can matter at the chair.

Figure 1 · Key terms for this module

Protective earthThe grounding path that carries fault current away from the case.
Leakage currentSmall unwanted current flowing to earth or through a person.
InsulationMaterial that keeps electricity in its intended path.
Electrical safety analyzerThe instrument that measures earth resistance and leakage.
Grounded outletA socket with a working earth connection.
Backup powerA generator or battery supply used when mains fails.
Air gapA break between drain hose and drain that prevents backflow.
Opening case · fictional

Case: A tingle from the machine case

It is Thursday, 11:10 in the morning, between shifts. The nurse, Ms. Rowena Pascual, 34, comes to the workshop. While rinsing her hands at the station sink, she rested her other hand on the metal side of machine 8 and felt a slight tingle. The machine works normally and shows no alarms.

You go to the station. Machine 8 is plugged into a multi-socket extension, because the wall outlet at that station “has been loose for months.” The schedule shows that a patient with a central venous catheter is due on machine 8 in an hour.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What does the tingle tell you?
  • What is done with the machine and the station right now, and whom do you tell?
  • Which two separate problems need fixing, and how do you keep them from returning?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Why dialysis patients are vulnerable

Current that enters the body must pass through the skin, and dry skin resists electricity well. A dialysis patient has no such barrier at the access. The patient is connected to the machine by blood and saline, which conduct well, and some patients have a catheter whose tip lies close to the heart. A current too small for a healthy person to feel can then be dangerous. For this reason medical equipment is built with stricter limits than household appliances, and dialysis machines used with central venous catheters carry particular requirements.1,2

Figure 2 · Layers of protection

InsulationKeeps mains voltage inside the wiring.
Protective earthCarries fault current away and blows the fuse.
Low leakage designLimits the tiny currents that always flow.
Building protectionGrounded outlets and protective devices in the distribution board.
Inspection and testingFinds a failed layer before a second one fails.
Lesson · part 2

Why electrical safety is tested

Electric current follows the easiest path to earth. Dry skin is a poor path. A dialysis patient, however, is connected to the machine by blood and saline, which conduct electricity well, and may have a catheter whose tip lies close to the heart. In that situation, currents far too small to feel can be dangerous. Dialysis machines are therefore designed with a grounded metal case, insulated internal circuits, and strict limits on leakage current.1

Figure 5 · Two protections you test

Protective earthIf a live wire touches the case, the earth wire carries the current away and the fuse blows. A broken or corroded earth connection removes that protection without any sign.
Insulation and leakageInsulation keeps mains voltage away from the case and the patient. As it ages or gets wet, more current leaks through it.

Both protections can fail while the machine runs perfectly, so you cannot find these faults by watching a treatment. Periodic testing follows the international standard for recurrent tests, or the national rule that applies to your facility.3 You do the tests with an electrical safety analyzer: at the interval the manufacturer states, after any repair that could affect electrical safety, and after a machine has been dropped, flooded, or opened for major work.

Figure 6 · An electrical safety test, in outline

  1. InspectPlug, cord, strain relief, case, and labels. Many faults are visible.
  2. Protective earth resistanceMeasure from the earth pin to exposed metal.
  3. Leakage currentMeasure by the method the standard and the manual specify.
  4. Functional checkThe machine works normally afterward.
  5. RecordValues, limits, analyzer identity, pass or fail.

For a machine with a detachable power cord, the recurrent-test standard gives a limit of 0.3 ohm for protective earth resistance. Leakage limits depend on the equipment class and the type of patient connection, so take them from the standard and the service manual, not from memory.3

The socket matters as well. The earth wire in the cord is only useful if the outlet connects it to earth, so a perfect machine on an ungrounded outlet is not protected. Dialysis stations need correctly grounded outlets on suitably protected circuits. That is a matter for the facility’s electrical engineer; your part is to notice and report.

Stop and reportA machine that fails an electrical safety test is out of service. So is a machine with a damaged cord, a cracked plug, or signs that fluid has entered the electronics.
Lesson · part 3

Visual inspection comes first

A large share of electrical safety failures can be seen before any meter is connected. A cracked plug or a taped cord is a fault already, and finding it by eye first saves time. Inspect in the same order every time.

ItemLook forIf found
PlugCracks, bent or discolored pins, a missing or loose earth pinReplace it with the approved part, then test
CordCuts, flattening, tape, stiffness, exposed inner wires at the endsReplace; never tape
Strain reliefCord pulled out of its gripRepair it, and check the internal connections
Case and panelsCracks, missing screws, panels that do not closeRepair before testing
Signs of fluidWater marks, corrosion, crystals inside or under the cabinetFind the leak, dry and clean, then test
LabelsRating plate and warnings legibleReplace labels by the manual
AccessoriesNon-standard adapters or extension leadsRemove them, and report

Figure 7 · Order of an electrical safety test

  1. Visual inspectionFix what you find first.
  2. Protective earth resistanceEarth pin to exposed metal, flexing the cord while measuring.
  3. Insulation or leakage testsBy the method the standard and the manual specify for this class of equipment.
  4. Function checkThe machine starts and runs normally.
  5. Label and recordDate, results against limits, analyzer identity, next due date.

The rating plate tells you the machine’s protection class and the type of its patient connection, and the limits for leakage current depend on both. Take the limits from the standard and the service manual, and let the analyzer’s automatic sequence apply them where it can. Flex the cord during the earth test. A broken strand inside the cord may touch its other end in one position and part in another, so a cord lying still can pass a test it would fail when a nurse moves the machine.3

Lesson · part 4

The utilities at each station

UtilityWhat the machine needsHow it failsWho fixes it
Power outletCorrect voltage; a working earth; a circuit that can carry the heater’s loadLoose socket; earth not connected; overloaded circuit tripping during heat disinfectionFacility electrician
Treated water outletPressure and flow within the machine’s stated rangeLow pressure at peak demand; leaking connector; dead leg when the station is unusedWater technician
DrainFree flow; an air gapBlockage; hose submerged; backflowFacility maintenance; you check the air gap
Central concentrate outlets, if fittedCorrect formula; correct connectorWrong line; crystals; leaksWater technician
Backup powerEnough capacity for what the plan says it suppliesGenerator does not start; changeover delay; supplies lights onlyFacility engineer

The machine draws on everything in this table, and a fault in any row reaches the patient through the machine. Outlets are tested, too. A simple outlet tester shows whether live, neutral, and earth are connected correctly. A qualified electrician measures earth quality and checks the protective devices in the distribution board. Keep a list of every station outlet with the date it was last verified.

Stop and reportExtension leads, multi-socket adapters, and two-pin adapters are not used for dialysis machines. They add resistance to the earth path, overload easily, and may have no earth at all.
Lesson · part 5

What happens when the power fails

A power failure gives you no time to read a manual. Know, before it happens, what each machine does on mains failure. It differs between models.

QuestionWhere to find the answer
Does the machine have an internal battery, and what does it keep running?Instructions for use. Commonly the blood pump, the air detector, and alarms for a limited time, so blood can be kept moving or returned; usually not the heater or dialysate preparation
How long does the battery last, and when was it last tested?Manual; your maintenance record
Is there a hand crank for the blood pump?On the machine; check it is present at every visit
What does the generator supply, and how long is the changeover?Facility engineer; the unit’s contingency plan
Does the water system run on backup power?Water technician; contingency plan
Do machines restart correctly when power returns?Test it, by the manual, on an out-of-service machine

Batteries age silently. A battery that runs the machine at start-up may not last the minutes that a real outage demands. Test each machine’s battery at the interval in its manual by running on battery for the stated time, and replace it at the stated age even if it still seems to work. After any power event, ask the nurses how the machines behaved. That is free information about your batteries.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

A cracked plug

What you see
While moving a machine to a new station, you notice a crack in the mains plug and a slightly bent earth pin.
What is likely going on
The earth connection may be unreliable.
What to do
Remove the machine from service, replace the plug or cord with the approved part, and run an electrical safety test.
Why it matters
Many electrical faults are found by looking, before any meter is connected.

An extension cord at the station

What you see
At station 9, a machine is plugged into a household extension cord because the wall outlet is loose.
What is likely going on
The extension may not be grounded, or rated for the load.
What to do
Report it to the charge nurse and the facility engineer. The machine belongs on a proper grounded outlet.
Why it matters
A perfect machine on a bad outlet is not protected.

Breaker trips during heat disinfection

What you see
At about ten at night, when all the machines start heat disinfection together, the circuit breaker for one row trips.
What is likely going on
The combined load of the heaters exceeds what the circuit can carry.
What to do
Report it to the facility engineer. Until the circuit is corrected, stagger the start times of disinfection. Check that every machine actually completed its program.
Why it matters
A tripped breaker means machines that were not disinfected, and wiring that is running at its limit.
Field notes
  • Inspect before you measure.
  • Flex the cord during the earth test.
  • No extension leads, and no adapters.
  • Know what the generator supplies before the power fails.
Case review

Back to the case: A tingle from the machine case

What the tingle tells you. Current is flowing from the machine’s case through Ms. Pascual to the grounded tap. The case is not properly earthed, there is leakage inside the machine, or both. For a patient with a central venous catheter this is a serious hazard.

What happens right now, and whom you tell. Unplug machine 8 and tag it out of service, and do not use the station. Tell the charge nurse, who moves the patient to another station. The move is the nurse’s decision; your part is to make sure no one connects a patient to machine 8 or that outlet.

The two problems, and keeping them from returning. First, the machine: a visual inspection, then a full electrical safety test. Find the source of the leakage, often moisture or a damaged cord, and repair it. Second, the building: the electrician repairs and tests the loose wall outlet, and the extension lead is removed from the unit. Then ask how a loose outlet stayed unreported for months, and check the other stations.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Electrical safety test with an analyzer

Materials: an out-of-service machine, its service manual, an electrical safety analyzer with a valid calibration, an outlet tester, and the test record. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • CriticalCarries out the visual inspection in order and corrects or reports defects before testing.
  • CriticalChecks the analyzer’s calibration date and leads.
  • CriticalMeasures protective earth resistance while flexing the cord and compares with the limit.
  • CriticalPerforms the leakage or insulation tests by the manual’s method and compares with the limits.
  • Confirms the machine functions normally afterward.
  • CriticalTags the machine out of service if any test fails.
  • Records values, limits, analyzer identity, and the next due date, and labels the machine.
  • Checks the station outlet with an outlet tester and reports any fault to the facility electrician.
  • CriticalConfirms no extension leads or adapters are in use at the station.
Placement activity. With the facility engineer, find out what the generator supplies in your unit and how long the changeover takes, and add it to the contingency plan if it is missing.

Question 1 of 8What is the purpose of the protective earth connection?
It prevents the case from becoming live if insulation fails.
Question 2 of 8When is an electrical safety test required apart from the scheduled interval?
Any event that could affect insulation or earthing calls for a retest.
Question 3 of 8Why are dialysis patients especially vulnerable to small electrical currents?
A direct conductive path makes very small currents hazardous.
Question 4 of 8A nurse feels a tingle from a machine’s metal case. What is the correct first action?
A tingle means current is flowing through a person; the hazard is immediate.
Question 5 of 8Why is the power cord flexed during the protective earth test?
Flexing reveals intermittent breaks.
Question 6 of 8Why are extension leads not used for dialysis machines?
The earth path must be direct and reliable.
Question 7 of 8On mains failure, what does a machine’s internal battery commonly keep running?
Batteries typically support the blood side briefly; check the model’s instructions.
Question 8 of 8A machine passes its electrical safety test but its wall outlet has no earth. Is the patient protected?
Protection is a chain from the building to the machine.
