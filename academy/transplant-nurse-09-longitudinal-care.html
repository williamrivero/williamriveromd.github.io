Discharge, Follow-up, and Life After Transplant

How to send a recipient home with a plan they can actually carry out, why the long-term threats shift from rejection to heart disease, diabetes and cancer, how teach-back closes the gap between explaining and understanding, and what changes when a teenager moves to adult care or a graft begins to fail.

Specialty subtrack for registered nurses

This subtrack prepares Philippine registered nurses for supervised kidney transplant ward and clinic work. It teaches assessment, communication and escalation within nursing scope. Candidacy, listing, organ allocation, compatibility decisions, dose changes and benefit approval belong to the authorized transplant team, the histocompatibility laboratory, the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) and the facility. All cases are synthetic.

Learning objectives · about 2 hours · week 5

After this module you should be able to

  • Build a discharge plan covering medicines, sampling, warning signs, contacts and follow-up that the patient can explain back.
  • Use teach-back and demonstration to confirm understanding, and correct gaps.
  • Close the loop on outpatient laboratory results, from receipt to patient communication.
  • Explain the long-term risks of cardiovascular disease, post-transplantation diabetes, cancer and bone disease, and the nursing role in each.
  • Plan adolescent transition by readiness, and support patients when a graft begins to fail.
Intuition

Handing over the controls

In hospital, the team flies the plane: nurses give medicines on time, blood is drawn at the right moment, and abnormal results reach a physician within hours. At discharge the controls pass to the patient and family, who must now do all of that themselves, at home, often far from the center, while recovering from surgery. A discharge plan is a flight manual. It works only if the new pilot can read it, has the instruments, and knows whom to radio when something goes wrong.

The picture has a limit. A plane behaves the same on every flight; a new kidney does not. In the first weeks, rejection is most likely, tacrolimus levels swing as diet, diarrhea and other drugs change its absorption and breakdown, and infection risk climbs as immunosuppression peaks. The aircraft itself is changing while the new pilot learns to fly it. Recipients also get a few days of training, not months. That is why every element of the plan must be tested, not just told, and why follow-up is so frequent at first.

Practice

A discharge plan the family can carry out

Before the patient leaves, confirm access, not just understanding. Reconcile the medicines (Module 6) and verify that the exact formulations are in hand or reliably obtainable, with a refill date and a supply plan. Confirm transport to the first visit and to the laboratory, how results will reach the team, and the after-hours route.

MedicinesEach drug, dose and time on one page; what each is for in one line; which to bring on blood-test mornings; what to do for a missed or vomited dose, per the team's instruction.
Monitoring at homeBlood pressure, weight, temperature and, where ordered, glucose and urine output, with targets and when to call; demonstrated, then done by the patient in front of you.
Warning signsFever or chills, less urine, rapid weight gain or swelling, pain over the graft, vomiting or diarrhea, breathlessness, new rash or blisters.
ContactsA daytime number, an after-hours number, and what to do if neither answers. Written large.
Follow-upDate of the first clinic and laboratory visit, who reviews results, stent removal date, and the prophylaxis stop dates.

Teach-back. Ask the patient to explain or show, in their own words: "Tell me how you will take your tacrolimus on the morning of your blood test." "Show me how you check your blood pressure." "If you have a fever on Sunday night, what will you do?" Correct gaps and ask again. A systematic review in kidney disease found teach-back improved knowledge, self-efficacy and self-management in most studies, although trials were small and heterogeneous.1 It costs minutes and catches errors that a signature on a form never will.

On the transplant ward, discharge day · fictional

Mr. Cabrera, 52, signed his discharge teaching form yesterday. This morning you ask him to show you his medicines for tomorrow. He picks up the tacrolimus box and the mycophenolate box and says, "These are the same, the antirejection ones, I take them together at eight." He is right about the time. He does not know which is which, and his blood test is tomorrow morning.

You lay the boxes on the table with his daughter. You put a colored sticker on the tacrolimus box and write "bring this to the laboratory, take after the blood is drawn." Then you ask him to walk you through tomorrow morning from the moment he wakes up. On the second try he gets it right.

What this shows. A signed form shows that teaching happened. Teach-back shows whether it worked.

Workflow

The first months and closed-loop results

Follow-up is frequent at first and tapers over the first year, because rejection, drug toxicity and infection are most likely early. The 2009 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) recipient guideline recommends measuring serum creatinine (1B) and suggests a schedule (2C for each step): daily for the first week or until discharge, two to three times a week in weeks 2 to 4, weekly in months 2 and 3, every two weeks in months 4 to 6, monthly in months 7 to 12, and every two to three months after that. It recommends measuring calcineurin inhibitor levels (1B), using the 12-hour trough for tacrolimus (2C).2 Each visit typically includes creatinine, electrolytes, blood count, glucose, tacrolimus trough, urine protein, and the scheduled viral tests (Module 8).

Know what each number stands for. Creatinine and the tacrolimus trough are surrogates. Nobody wants a "good trough" for its own sake; the outcomes that matter are a kidney that keeps working and a patient who stays alive and well. A trough in range makes rejection and toxicity less likely, but it does not rule either out. That is why a normal-looking level never cancels a fever, falling urine or a rising creatinine.

A result is complete only when the loop is closed. The steps are: the result is received; an authorized clinician reviews it; an action or disposition is documented (dose change, repeat test, no change); and the patient is told when indicated. Results that arrive after hours, on weekends, or from an outside laboratory are the ones most often lost. Every abnormal result needs a named owner, with a backup when that person is away (Module 11 turns this into a measurable process).

At the results desk, Monday · fictional

Mrs. Javier's potassium was 6.4 mmol/L on a Saturday sample sent from a provincial laboratory. The fax arrived at the clinic on Saturday afternoon. Nobody saw it until Monday.

She was fine, by luck. At the next team meeting, you and Carla map how weekend and outside results arrive. You propose a named weekend reviewer and an after-hours number for laboratories to call with critical values. The team adopts it.

What this shows. Results that arrive after hours or from outside laboratories are the ones most often lost. Close the loop by design, not by luck.

Physiology and evidence

From rejection to heart, sugar, cancer and bone

After the first year, most recipients who lose their kidney either die with a functioning graft or lose it to chronic injury. The leading cause of death with a functioning graft is cardiovascular disease, driven by pre-existing vascular damage from years of kidney disease, plus the hypertension, diabetes and lipid effects of immunosuppression. KDIGO puts the annual rate of fatal or nonfatal cardiovascular events at 3.5 to 5.0 percent in recipients and calls them a highest-risk group.2

Post-transplantation diabetes mellitus (PTDM). Tacrolimus impairs insulin secretion by pancreatic beta cells; corticosteroids increase insulin resistance; weight gain after transplant adds to both. The 2024 international consensus emphasizes that early post-operative hyperglycemia is common and should not be labeled PTDM; the diagnosis is made once the patient is stable on maintenance immunosuppression.3 glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) can be misleading in the first months because of anemia and recent transfusion. KDIGO recommends screening every recipient without diabetes with fasting plasma glucose, an oral glucose tolerance test or HbA1c (1C), at least weekly for four weeks, every three months for a year, then yearly (2D for each interval), and again after starting or raising tacrolimus, an mTOR inhibitor or steroids (2D).2 The 2024 consensus is expert opinion, not a graded guideline. Nurses screen glucose as ordered, teach diet and activity, and recognize hypoglycemia once treatment starts.

In the transplant clinic · fictional

Eight months after transplant, Lorna, 45, has gained 9 kilograms. Her fasting glucose has been 128 to 140 mg/dL on her last three visits. She says, "The doctor said high sugar right after surgery was normal, so I'm not worried."

You explain the difference: high sugar in the first weeks after surgery is common, but she is now stable on her maintenance medicines, and these readings need a doctor's review. You record the trend and her weight and route them to the transplant nephrologist. You also start a conversation about the food she eats at work, and refer her to the dietitian.

What this shows. Early hyperglycemia and diabetes after transplant are not the same thing. The timing of the reading matters.

Cancer. In a linkage of more than 175,000 US solid-organ transplants, overall cancer risk was about twice that of the general population (standardized incidence ratio 2.10). In absolute terms, recipients developed about 1,375 cancers per 100,000 person-years, roughly 720 more than expected. The most common cancer with an elevated risk was non-Hodgkin lymphoma (about 7.5 times expected), and risk was raised for 32 cancer types, some virus-related and some not.4 This is registry data: it shows the size of the risk, not which drug causes it. Skin cancers, not captured in that registry analysis, are the most common malignancy in recipients in many countries. KDIGO recommends telling recipients that their skin cancer risk is very high (1C) and that they should minimize lifelong sun exposure and use ultraviolet-blocking agents (1D). It suggests skin and lip self-examination with reporting of new lesions (2D) and a yearly skin examination by a qualified professional (2D), and screening for other cancers as for the general population (not graded).2 Teach sun protection (shade, clothing, sunscreen) and skin self-examination, and keep age-appropriate screening up to date. Report new lumps, nonhealing skin lesions, night sweats or weight loss.

In the transplant clinic · fictional

Mang Isko, a fisherman from Batangas, shows you a scaly sore on his ear that has not healed in two months. He thought it was sunburn.

You record its size and location, take a photo with consent for the chart, and route it to the transplant team for dermatology referral. Then you talk about a wide-brimmed hat and long sleeves on the boat, and ask him to check his skin each month.

What this shows. Recipients who work outdoors carry a high skin cancer risk. A sore that does not heal is reported, not watched.

Bone and other risks. Steroids and pre-existing mineral and bone disorder raise fracture risk; follow the ordered calcium, vitamin D and bone assessments. KDIGO recommends checking calcium and phosphorus at least weekly early after transplant until stable (1B).2 Hypertension and lipid targets follow the team's plan. Mood, sleep, work, sexuality and reproductive plans belong in follow-up conversations, because they drive adherence and quality of life.

Physiology

The heart and vessels: the long game

After the first year, the most common reason a recipient dies is not rejection or infection. It is heart and blood vessel disease, often while the graft is still working.2 This surprises patients, who have spent years thinking about the kidney. It makes sense physiologically. Years of kidney failure have already stiffened and calcified the arteries. After transplant, several drugs push the same risk factors further: calcineurin inhibitors raise blood pressure and glucose, steroids raise glucose, weight and lipids, and mTOR inhibitors raise lipids. Many recipients also gain weight once the dietary limits of dialysis are lifted.

Home blood pressure, done right. Home readings are only useful if they are taken well. Teach the patient to sit quietly for five minutes with the back supported and feet flat, to use the right cuff size on a bare arm resting at heart level, to avoid coffee, smoking and exercise in the 30 minutes before, and to take two readings a minute apart. Ask him to write down the time and the readings, or keep them on the device, and bring them to clinic. Check his technique once in front of you; many errors only show up then.

What the guideline grades. KDIGO recommends measuring blood pressure at every clinic visit (1C) and suggests a target below 130/80 mm Hg in adults (2C). Lipid screening, tobacco counseling and weight programs are ungraded statements, and it suggests aspirin for recipients with known atherosclerotic disease unless contraindicated (2B).2 Weak grades here mean the targets come mostly from general-population evidence, not from trials in recipients.

Put prevention on the visit checklist. Heart risk builds quietly over years, so it is easy to crowd out by today's creatinine. A short standing list at each visit helps: blood pressure log reviewed, weight recorded, smoking asked, glucose and lipid tests checked as scheduled, activity discussed, and skin looked at (the sun advice above). Each item takes a minute, and together they address the most common cause of death after the first year.

In the transplant clinic · fictional

Mr. Velasco, three years after transplant, brings his home blood pressure log. Every reading is below 125/80. In clinic his pressure is 158/94. You ask him to show you how he measures at home. He uses a wrist cuff, standing in the kitchen, with his arm by his side, right after his morning walk.

You show him the correct position with an upper-arm cuff, and have him take two readings in front of you. They are 154/92 and 150/90. You record both and tell Dr. Santos that the home log has been reading falsely low.

What this shows. A good-looking log is only as reliable as the technique behind it. Watch the patient measure once.

Practice

Follow-up from a distance

Many Philippine recipients live hours from their transplant center. After the first months, much of their follow-up happens at a local laboratory, a provincial hospital or a private clinic, with results sent back to the program. This works only if every result reaches someone who reviews it, and if the patient knows what to do when something goes wrong far from the center.

Make the local arrangement explicit. Before the patient leaves for home, agree in writing which laboratory will draw the samples, how the results will be sent, who at the program receives them, and how quickly. Teach the patient that tacrolimus troughs must be drawn before the morning dose, and give a printed note for the local laboratory that says so. Confirm that the local laboratory actually offers the tests needed, because tacrolimus levels are not available everywhere.

Results sent by phone. Patients often photograph results and send them by messaging app. Accept them through the channel your program has approved, check that the name, date and sample time are visible, record them in the official chart, and close the loop with the patient when the result has been reviewed. Module 10 covers the privacy rules that apply.

Medicine supplyPlan refills early, especially before holidays, long trips and typhoon season. Advise a small reserve as the team allows.
TravelCarry medicines in hand luggage, with a medicine list and the program's number. Keep dose times steady across time zones as the team advises.
Where to goName the nearest hospital the patient would use in an emergency, and make sure it can reach the program.
A written planOne page with medicines, warning signs, test schedule and phone numbers, kept on the refrigerator and in the wallet.
By phone · fictional

Mrs. Dimaculangan lives in Masbate. Her monthly results arrive by photo. This month's tacrolimus level is very high. The photo shows the sample was collected at 10:30 a.m.

You call her. She took her morning dose at 7 a.m., because the laboratory opens at 9 and she did not want to "be late with my tablets." You explain again why the sample must come first, send the laboratory a short note with the time requirement, and record that the level was not a trough. Dr. Santos asks for a repeat next week rather than changing the dose.

What this shows. Distance multiplies small errors. A clear local arrangement and a careful look at each sent result keep the plan safe.

Practice

Teenagers, graft failure and living donors

Adolescent transition. Adolescence and young adulthood are the highest-risk years for graft loss, largely through nonadherence and the disruption of moving from pediatric to adult care. In a US registry analysis of 440 recipients, those transferred to adult care before age 21 had 58 percent higher graft failure rates than those of the same age who transferred later (about 12.9 versus 8.7 graft failures per 100 person-years).5 This is observational, so it cannot prove that early transfer itself causes the losses, but it supports transition based on readiness (self-management skills, understanding of medicines, ability to book appointments) and an agreed clinical handover, not age alone.

In the transplant clinic · fictional

Bea had her transplant at 12. She is now 19, a college student in Manila, and her mother, who managed every dose, has stayed in the province. Her last two tacrolimus levels were very low.

You see Bea alone for part of the visit. She admits that she forgets the evening dose when she studies late. You set up a phone alarm with her, give her the clinic number to save under her own name, and agree that she will book her next laboratory visit herself, with you checking that it happened.

What this shows. Transition is a set of skills to build and check, not a date on the calendar.

When a graft begins to fail. Kidney function may decline slowly over years. Support honest conversations about the options: dialysis access planning, retransplant evaluation (Module 1 applies again), or supportive care. Immunosuppression is usually continued and adjusted by the team during this period; do not let a patient stop medicines because "the kidney is failing anyway."

Living donors have their own follow-up plan (Module 3), separate from the recipient's, with at least annual blood pressure, body mass index, creatinine with estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and albuminuria checks, plus lifestyle and psychosocial review. KDIGO 2017 states these as ungraded recommendations.6

Practice case · synthetic

Case: Medicines named, refills unknown

Day 7, 10:00 a.m., discharge planning. Mr. Ocampo, 63, is ready for discharge after a living-related transplant. His wife, his main caregiver, can name every medicine and its time without looking at the list. You are impressed.

Then you ask how they will get next month's tacrolimus. She looks surprised. "We will look for it when it runs out." They live in Tarlac. The nearest pharmacy they know is in town, and they have never asked whether it stocks his formulation.

They share one mobile phone with their son, who works night shifts in Manila and keeps the phone during the day. The first clinic and laboratory visit is in four days.

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, whom you would contact and what you would say, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the debrief below.

Assess

Days of supply in hand, the exact formulation prescribed, which pharmacies near them stock it, coverage under the post-transplant package, transport to clinic and laboratory, and how the team can reach them.

Communicate

Contact the transplant pharmacist and benefits or social work before discharge to secure a supply route. Record consented contact numbers, including the son's, with times. Confirm the first visit date and who reviews results.

Teach

Teach-back the refill plan: where, when, with which prescription, and what to do if the pharmacy does not have it. Demonstrate home blood pressure and temperature checks.

Escalate

Do not complete discharge with an unresolved immunosuppressant supply gap. Escalate to the transplant team and charge nurse if no supply plan can be confirmed today.
How it played out · fictional

The nurse in the story did not sign the discharge until a supply route was confirmed. She called the transplant pharmacist, who confirmed that the hospital pharmacy could dispense a month's supply, and asked benefits staff to check coverage under the adult post-transplant package.

She recorded the son's number with his consent and agreed a calling window with the family: after 6 p.m., when the son is home. She then asked Mrs. Ocampo to explain the refill plan back: where, when, and what to do if the pharmacy has none. On the first try, she forgot the last part; on the second, she had it.

Mr. Ocampo went home that afternoon with 30 days of tacrolimus, a written plan for the next refill, and a reminder in the clinic tracker for the nurse to check his supply at the four-day visit.

Documentation task. Write a one-page discharge plan and a teach-back record, including the refill route, backup route and the date you will recheck supply.
Second round · what changesThe working caregiver cannot attend clinic visits and the patient depends on a shared phone.
Submit: A follow-up plan with contact consent, transport and refill contingencies, and named backup contacts.

Supervised practice

With your preceptor

On the ward at discharge and in clinic. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own protocols, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record.

  • Write a one-page discharge plan for a synthetic recipient and test it with teach-back on a peer.
  • Observe a patient demonstrating home blood pressure and temperature measurement.
  • Trace one outpatient result from receipt to documented review and patient communication.
  • Teach sun protection and skin self-examination.
  • Review your center's adolescent transition process and readiness checklist.
