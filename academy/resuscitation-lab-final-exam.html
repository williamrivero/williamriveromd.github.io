Before you begin

How this examination works

This examination covers the whole Dialysis Team Resuscitation Lab series. Each sitting draws 39 questions at random from a bank of 130, 3 from each module, in random order, with the answer choices shuffled. Choose the single best answer for each question, then select Check answers.

When to take itAfter you have finished every module and reviewed the questions you missed.
How to take itIn one sitting, without notes. Allow about one minute per question.
Proposed pass mark32 of 39 best answers (80 percent). Your trainer may set a different mark.
If you fall shortEach explanation names its module. Reread those modules, then select New set of questions.
What a pass meansA pass shows that you know the material. It does not show that you can do the work. Competence is demonstrated on your unit’s own equipment and signed off by a preceptor.

How to get your certificate of completion

  1. Pass all 13 module quizzes. Each module quiz is passed at 80%, or at the pass mark the module states. The Certificate checklist above the examination shows which modules are done; each line links to that module's quiz.
  2. Use the same device and browser for the modules and this examination. Progress is saved in your browser, not in an account. On iPhone and iPad, the Home Screen app and Safari keep separate progress.
  3. Pass this timed examination (80%; where these instructions say so, every safety-critical question must also be correct). You have one minute per question from the moment you tap Start the examination; only your first check of a set counts. If you pass before finishing the modules, your pass is saved: finish the module quizzes, then come back here.
  4. Type your name the way you want it printed and tap Make my certificate. You can tick Add a preceptor sign-off line if your unit will sign the printed copy.
  5. Save or print your completion packet: two pages, the certificate and a record of your module results. Use Print or save as PDF to keep both pages together.
  6. Be online when you make it, so the certificate gets its QR code. Anyone can scan the code, or enter it at renalcarematters.com/verify, to confirm it.

Not finished yet? Use Print a progress record in the checklist to show a preceptor how far you have gone; it is clearly marked "not a certificate". The certificate is a self-study certificate of completion, not CPD units: competence is demonstrated on your own unit and signed off by a preceptor. To make the QR code work, your name, track, date and score are saved with the certificate code; to have them removed, use Report a correction on any guide.

Examination

End-of-course examination

Question 1In a large review of dialysis-unit arrests, which session of the week had the most arrests?
Best: arrests were more frequent on Mondays, when potassium, fluid and acid have built up over the two-day gap. Why not the others: midweek and end-of-week sessions follow shorter gaps; the pattern was not even. Module 1.
Question 2Which dialysis prescription feature was associated with cardiac arrest in clinic studies?
Best: a potassium bath below 2 mEq/L was associated with arrest whatever the pre-dialysis potassium; the studies were observational. Why not the others: low (not high) calcium baths were the associated finding; line length and dialyzer size were not reported risk factors. Module 1.
Question 3At 5:40 a.m. the defibrillator shows a battery warning and the adult pads are expired. What is the best action?
Best: a unit without a working defibrillator cannot treat a shockable arrest; fix it and escalate before connection. Why not the others: logging alone leaves the risk; expired pads may fail; waiting leaves every patient exposed all day. Module 1.
Question 4Why does a dialysis unit need a named watcher during an arrest?
Best: every other circuit keeps running, and a needle dislodgement or hypotension elsewhere can go unseen while staff crowd one chair. Why not the others: filming is not a safety role; the watcher never joins the arrest; family contact is the leader’s or charge nurse’s task later. Module 1.
Question 5In one cohort of 729 in-clinic arrests, what was found about simply having a defibrillator on site?
Best: presence alone was not associated with better adjusted survival; a device helps only when it works, reaches the chair fast and is used. Why not the others: no doubling or harm was shown; presence varied between clinics. Module 1.
Question 6Which Philippine requirement for dialysis shifts is verified?
Best: Department of Health (DOH) and Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) rules require current Basic Life Support (BLS) for dialysis nurses, current ACLS for the physician-on-duty, and at least one ACLS-updated nurse per shift. Why not the others: no Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS)-for-all, drill-frequency or physician-only rule was verified. Module 1.
Question 7When should the runner call the ambulance in a freestanding unit?
Best: the minutes until the ambulance arrives belong to your team, so the call goes out at once. Why not the others: each delay adds minutes; waiting for a pulse or the physician wastes time that cannot be recovered. Module 1.
Question 8A patient’s systolic pressure falls by 35 mm Hg in the second hour. How should the team treat this?
Best: a drop of 30 mm Hg or more preceded 16% of arrests in one series; treat it as a warning and look for the cause (Card 3). Why not the others: dismissing it misses a warning; it is not proof of any one cause; more fluid removal worsens it. Module 1.
Question 9Which link in the chain of survival is special to dialysis units?
Best: the circuit decision has no counterpart in general guidelines. Why not the others: calling for help, compressions and defibrillation are links in every chain of survival. Module 1.
Question 10How should code roles be assigned?
Best: named roles prevent the “who does what?” delay. Why not the others: first-come chaos wastes seconds; the physician may arrive late; a monthly list does not match today’s roster. Module 1.
Question 11What are the five decision questions, in order?
Best: these five sort almost every floor emergency into the right algorithm. Why not the others: vital signs or the ABCDE survey are useful but do not place the patient in an algorithm; the last option asks rhythm questions before checking for arrest. Module 2.
Question 12A patient is gasping and you are unsure whether you feel a pulse after 10 seconds. What do you do?
Best: gasping is not normal breathing, and an uncertain pulse is treated as no pulse; pulse checks last no more than 10 seconds (American Heart Association (AHA) Class of Recommendation (COR) 1). Why not the others: longer checks delay compressions; oxygen and blood pressure do not move blood in arrest. Module 2.
Question 13Why does the ‘shockable rhythm?’ question change treatment?
Best: defibrillation stops disorganized electrical activity so a normal rhythm can restart; it has nothing to reset in asystole, and PEA already has organized electricity. Why not the others: shocks are for VF/pVT, not slow rhythms; compressions continue; every arrest needs CPR. Module 2.
Question 14A patient with a heart rate of 38 is alert, with a pressure of 128/70 and no symptoms. How do you classify this?
Best: stability is judged by signs, not the number; with no compromise, monitor and find the cause (potassium, drugs). Why not the others: no instability signs means no immediate pacing; there is a pulse; a rate of 38 still needs explanation. Module 2.
Question 15What is the leader’s main job during a resuscitation with enough staff?
Best: a hands-off leader can see the monitor, clock and team. Why not the others: compressing or drawing up drugs takes the leader’s eyes off the whole event; recording is a separate role. Module 2.
Question 16During pacer setup for bradycardia, the patient stops responding and has no pulse. What should the leader say?
Best: announcing the transition closes the old plan for everyone. Why not the others: pacing and atropine belong to bradycardia with a pulse; waiting withholds compressions. Module 2.
Question 17Which finding makes a tachycardia ‘unstable’?
Best: instability means the rhythm is causing organ hypoperfusion: low pressure, altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest pain or acute heart failure. Why not the others: a rate number, palpitations alone or past history do not define instability. Module 2.
Question 18In the ten-part frame, what is the ‘trigger’ for the adult cardiac arrest algorithm?
Best: these findings define cardiac arrest. Why not the others: slow rate, low pressure and high potassium are triggers for other algorithms or overlays. Module 2.
Question 19What is a common recognition error that this module warns about?
Best: teams keep working on the previous plan, such as pacing after the pulse is gone. Why not the others: the other options are correct habits. Module 2.
Question 20Why are the course’s algorithm diagrams original rather than copies of AHA charts?
Best: the content follows 2025 AHA recommendations, but the charts are proprietary course material. Why not the others: the 2025 charts are current; the course does follow AHA; the charts are not physician-only. Module 2.
Question 21A slumped patient has slow, snoring breaths every 8 seconds and no definite pulse. The monitor shows a regular narrow rhythm at 64. What is this?
Best: gasping with no definite pulse is cardiac arrest, and an organized rhythm without a pulse is PEA. Why not the others: fluid before compressions wastes the critical minute; ventilation alone ignores the absent pulse; the rhythm does not prove circulation. Module 3.
Question 22A patient is not breathing but has a definite strong pulse. What is the first action?
Best: respiratory arrest with a pulse needs ventilation (AHA suggests 10 breaths a minute for adults). Why not the others: compressions and shocks are for absent circulation; epinephrine is not first-line for respiratory arrest. Module 3.
Question 23Why can a rhythm strip not establish whether a patient has a pulse?
Best: electricity and pumping usually travel together but separate in PEA and shock. Why not the others: accuracy is not the issue; pulses are checked centrally; the strip shows all chambers. Module 3.
Question 24The monitor shows a flat line in a patient who is talking. What is most likely?
Best: a talking patient has circulation, so the flat line is a monitoring problem. Why not the others: asystole and VF cause unresponsiveness; arrest cannot coexist with talking. Module 3.
Question 25Which statement about the potassium ECG is correct?
Best: a retrospective review found typical changes often absent at high values. Why not the others: the textbook sequence is not reliable; normal ECGs occur; there is no fixed threshold. Module 3.
Question 26A patient with potassium 7.4 mmol/L is alert, with a pulse, and peaked T waves. Which algorithm applies?
Best: with a pulse, this is pre-arrest hyperkalemia, an emergency treated with membrane protection, shifting and removal. Why not the others: there is a pulse; the ECG change makes it urgent beyond a rhythm problem; delay is dangerous. Module 3.
Question 27Wide, fast, regular rhythm on the monitor. What do you check before choosing treatment?
Best: pulseless VT is treated as arrest; VT with a pulse depends on stability. Why not the others: potassium matters later; weight and fluid temperature do not decide the pathway. Module 3.
Question 28Which finding defines a tachycardia as unstable in the 2025 adult guideline examples?
Best: the guideline gives systolic pressure under 80 mm Hg or altered mentation as signs requiring immediate cardioversion in wide-complex tachycardia. Why not the others: rate alone, history or palpitations do not define instability. Module 3.
Question 29Who should be treated with the pediatric pathway?
Best: pediatric guidance applies before puberty; after puberty adult guidance applies. Why not the others: weight alone, age 18, or infants only do not match the guideline definitions. Module 3.
Question 30A shivering patient’s monitor shows a chaotic baseline, but the patient answers questions. What is it?
Best: a responsive patient cannot be in VF, so the chaotic trace is artifact. Why not the others: shocking a conscious patient is dangerous; the other rhythms do not match a talking patient with a chaotic baseline. Module 3.
Question 31What compression rate and depth does the 2025 AHA adult BLS guideline recommend?
Best: rate 100 to 120 (COR 2a) and depth at least 5 cm, avoiding more than 6 cm (COR 1). Why not the others: slower or shallower moves too little blood; faster or deeper reduces refilling and causes injury. Module 4.
Question 32The recliner jams halfway down during an arrest. What should the rescuer do?
Best: a firm surface is preferred only if it does not delay compressions. Why not the others: waiting means no blood flow; moving a connected patient without clamping risks dislodging the access; breaths alone do not circulate blood. Module 4.
Question 33What should happen immediately after a shock?
Best: resuming compressions immediately after the shock is recommended over a post-shock rhythm check (COR 2a, Level of Evidence (LOE) B-R). Why not the others: pulse checks and re-analysis add pauses; breaths are given within the 30:2 cycle. Module 4.
Question 34Why must the chest recoil fully between compressions?
Best: leaning on the chest stops the heart refilling, so the next compression moves less blood. Why not the others: recoil does not prevent all injury; counting and ventilation are not the reason. Module 4.
Question 35What is the compression-to-ventilation ratio before an advanced airway is placed?
Best: 30:2 is recommended before an advanced airway (COR 2a). Why not the others: 15:2 is the two-rescuer pediatric ratio before puberty; 5:1 is outdated; continuous compressions with asynchronous breaths may be considered but is not the default. Module 4.
Question 36Bag-mask breaths cause the stomach to swell. What is the fix?
Best: large, fast breaths push air into the stomach; aim for visible chest rise over about one second. Why not the others: harder or faster makes it worse; stopping ventilation is unsafe. Module 4.
Question 37An adult patient at the meal table cannot speak or cough after choking. What is the 2025 sequence?
Best: AHA 2025 recommends alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts (COR 1, LOE B-NR). Why not the others: thrusts alone is the older approach; blind finger sweeps can cause harm (COR 3: Harm); water worsens obstruction. Module 4.
Question 38How often should compressors swap?
Best: depth falls with fatigue before the compressor notices, so swap at each two-minute check. Why not the others: longer intervals and waiting to be asked let quality drop. Module 4.
Question 39Where should defibrillator pads be placed on a patient with a right chest-wall dialysis catheter?
Best: either standard position works (COR 2a), adjusted to avoid the catheter and moisture. Why not the others: placing pads over the exit site or wet dressing is unsafe; same-side or limb placement does not send current through the heart. Module 4.
Question 40What does a chest compression fraction of at least 60% mean in the AHA guideline?
Best: the guideline says at least 60% may be reasonable (COR 2b); higher is better because each pause stops flow. Why not the others: it is a floor, not a ceiling; it measures time compressing, not depth or survival. Module 4.
Question 41When should the first dose of epinephrine be given in asystole or PEA?
Best: in nonshockable arrest, epinephrine is reasonable as soon as feasible (COR 2a, LOE B-NR). Why not the others: shocks do not apply; delay is not recommended; an airway is not a prerequisite. Module 5.
Question 42When is epinephrine first given on the VF/pulseless VT path?
Best: give epinephrine after initial shocks fail (COR 2a, LOE B-NR); the algorithm places it after the second shock. Why not the others: the first shock comes first; epinephrine is not withheld for amiodarone; drugs are part of the shockable path. Module 5.
Question 43What is the adult epinephrine dose and interval in cardiac arrest?
Best: 1 mg every 3 to 5 minutes is reasonable (COR 2a, LOE B-R). Why not the others: IM dosing is for anaphylaxis with a pulse; 10 minutes is too long; high-dose epinephrine is not recommended (COR 3: No Benefit). Module 5.
Question 44VF persists after several shocks and epinephrine. What may be considered?
Best: amiodarone or lidocaine may be considered for shock-refractory VF/pVT (COR 2b, LOE B-R). Why not the others: routine calcium and bicarbonate are not recommended (COR 3: No Benefit); routine pacing in arrest is not recommended. Module 5.
Question 45What does the 2025 guideline say about double sequential defibrillation for refractory VF?
Best: usefulness has not been established (COR 2b, LOE B-R). Why not the others: it is not recommended as routine; the guideline does not call it harmful; it does not replace drugs. Module 5.
Question 46Which vascular access does the AHA recommend trying first during cardiac arrest?
Best: attempt IV first (COR 1, LOE A); IO if IV fails (COR 2a, LOE A). Why not the others: IO is second; endotracheal is not the preferred route; central access is considered only if IV and IO fail. Module 5.
Question 47What does an abrupt sustained rise in ETCO2 during CPR suggest?
Best: an abrupt increase may be used to detect ROSC (COR 2b, LOE B-NR). Why not the others: it reflects improved blood flow, not compression rate, rhythm or a need to stop breaths. Module 5.
Question 48A patient arrests in the first 30 minutes of a Monday session after a missed Friday. Which reversible cause is most likely?
Best: early arrest after a long gap points to high potassium. Why not the others: low potassium is more likely late in a session; hypothermia and tension pneumothorax have no clue here. Module 5.
Question 49An arrest happens in the last 20 minutes of a session with large fluid removal. Which causes rise to the top?
Best: late arrests point to volume loss and falling electrolytes. Why not the others: high potassium is more typical early; dialyzer reactions occur in the first minutes; opioids have no clue here. Module 5.
Question 50Why do compressions restart immediately after a successful shock?
Best: post-shock myocardial stunning means a new rhythm may not yet generate a pulse; compressions support flow until the next check. Why not the others: charging is unrelated; shocks often succeed; pads are checked without stopping. Module 5.
Question 51What heart rate does the 2025 adult guideline typically use to define a bradyarrhythmia?
Best: the 2025 algorithm uses a heart rate typically under 50 if bradyarrhythmia (it was under 60 before). Why not the others: 60 was the older threshold; 40 and 70 are not guideline thresholds. Module 6.
Question 52What is the algorithm atropine dose for unstable adult bradycardia?
Best: 1 mg bolus, repeat every 3 to 5 minutes, maximum total 3 mg. Why not the others: 0.5 mg was an older adult dose; 2 mg once and the weight-based dose do not match the adult algorithm. Module 6.
Question 53Atropine has not improved an unstable bradycardia. What are the next options?
Best: if atropine is ineffective, pacing or an adrenergic infusion may be effective (COR 2b, LOE C-LD). Why not the others: atropine has a maximum; adenosine slows the heart further; defibrillation is for pulseless VF/VT. Module 6.
Question 54Which combination defines BRASH syndrome?
Best: bradycardia, renal failure, atrioventricular nodal blockade, shock, and hyperkalemia (BRASH) is a vicious cycle of these five, often with only moderately high potassium and a usual-dose AV-nodal drug. Why not the others: they are invented combinations. Module 6.
Question 55A dialysis patient on a beta-blocker has a heart rate of 34 and potassium of 6.2 mmol/L. Why might atropine alone fail?
Best: atropine counters vagal slowing; it works poorly when potassium and an AV-nodal drug cause the block, so treat both causes. Why not the others: atropine is not contraindicated; it does not raise potassium; 6.2 mmol/L is high. Module 6.
Question 56A stable patient has a regular, narrow tachycardia at 180. What is a reasonable first drug after vagal maneuvers?
Best: adenosine is recommended for regular narrow-complex tachycardia (COR 1, LOE B-R); the algorithm uses 6 mg then 12 mg. Why not the others: the 300 mg amiodarone bolus is a cardiac arrest dose; atropine and epinephrine speed the heart. Module 6.
Question 57A patient with atrial fibrillation at 170 has a pressure of 70/40 and confusion. What does the 2025 guideline suggest?
Best: unstable tachycardia needs synchronized cardioversion; for atrial fibrillation (AF) at least 200 J initial energy is reasonable (COR 2a, LOE B-R). Why not the others: adenosine does not treat AF; observation is unsafe; unsynchronized low energy is wrong. Module 6.
Question 58Why is cardioversion ‘synchronized’?
Best: a shock on the T wave can trigger VF. Why not the others: sedation addresses pain; battery and safety clearing are unrelated. Module 6.
Question 59An unstable patient has polymorphic VT. What shock is used?
Best: the device cannot reliably synchronize to changing QRS shapes, so unstable polymorphic VT is defibrillated. Why not the others: low synchronized energy may fail to fire; adenosine and pacing are wrong here. Module 6.
Question 60A connected, stable patient has a slow rhythm from high potassium. A nurse wants to stop dialysis. What is correct?
Best: dialysis removes potassium; stopping it removes the definitive treatment (Card 10). Why not the others: stopping or delaying worsens potassium; more fluid removal can lower the pressure. Module 6.
Question 61A patient arrests on the machine. What is the circuit nurse’s first action?
Best: fluid removal empties a circulation that has none to spare; stop it at once. Why not the others: another person compresses; the blood decision comes after checking for air, hemolysis or reaction; needles stay in for drugs. Module 7.
Question 62Foam is seen below the venous chamber in a patient who collapses. What is the blood decision?
Best: returning the blood pushes more air into the patient (Card 5). Why not the others: rinse-back and overriding the detector add air; partial disconnection does not stop the air. Module 7.
Question 63The venous line blood looks translucent and port-wine colored during an arrest. What do you do with the circuit blood?
Best: hemolyzed blood carries potassium from broken red cells and is never returned. Why not the others: any return adds potassium; filtering is not a bedside option. Module 7.
Question 64With no air, hemolysis, reaction or open line, what are the two acceptable blood options?
Best: these match the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) principle and Card 1. Why not the others: running the circuit or fluid removal in arrest is unsafe; waiting leaves the circuit unmanaged. Module 7.
Question 65What does ERC 2025 advise about defibrillation with a patient connected to the dialysis machine?
Best: the ERC recommends disconnection unless defibrillation-proof and warns about wet surfaces. Why not the others: connection without a rating is a hazard; dialysis patients are defibrillated; power-off alone does not address the patient connection. Module 7.
Question 66Where do you find out whether your dialysis machine is defibrillation-proof?
Best: the rating belongs to the device and model. Why not the others: guidelines, charts and other units’ lists do not state your device’s rating. Module 7.
Question 67Before giving epinephrine through a dialysis catheter limb, what must be done?
Best: the lock may be concentrated heparin or citrate; aspirating prevents giving it to the patient. Why not the others: pushing the lock is a medication error; air is dangerous; mixing with the lock is wrong. Module 7.
Question 68What does ‘leave the access open’ mean in the ERC guidance?
Best: open means available, never an uncapped needle or unclamped limb, which can draw in air or bleed. Why not the others: each describes an unsafe open access. Module 7.
Question 69Which position does the 2025 AHA guideline recommend for life-threatening gas embolism?
Best: AHA 2025 (COR 2a, LOE C-EO); dialysis teaching of the left side differs, and Card 5 defers to protocol. Why not the others: sitting or head-up can send air to the brain; prone is not recommended. Module 7.
Question 70Why must the circuit nurse let go of the blood lines when the leader calls ‘clear’?
Best: anything touching the patient during the shock can carry current to staff. Why not the others: they are not the reason for clearing. Module 7.
Question 71How does intravenous calcium help in hyperkalemia?
Best: calcium restores the gap between the resting potential and threshold; potassium is unchanged. Why not the others: shifting is insulin or salbutamol; urinary and gut removal are other mechanisms. Module 8.
Question 72A connected, stable patient has potassium 7.2 mmol/L and peaked T waves. What should happen to the dialysis session?
Best: dialysis removes potassium; stopping it removes the only real treatment (Card 10). Why not the others: stopping or pausing loses removal; ultrafiltration alone removes little potassium. Module 8.
Question 73What is the 2025 AHA position on inhaled beta-agonists in hyperkalemic cardiac arrest?
Best: AHA 2025 does not recommend inhaled beta-agonists in arrest from suspected hyperkalemia. Why not the others: they misstate the grade. Module 8.
Question 74What does AHA 2025 say about IV calcium in hyperkalemic cardiac arrest?
Best: AHA rates calcium in suspected hyperkalemic arrest as of uncertain effectiveness. Why not the others: it is neither mandatory nor graded harmful, and it does not replace epinephrine. Module 8.
Question 75What does ERC 2025 recommend for hyperkalemic cardiac arrest?
Best: this is the ERC arrest regimen, with dialysis considered for refractory arrest. Why not the others: salbutamol is an adjunct in patients with circulation; the ERC does give drugs; mixing calcium and bicarbonate causes precipitation. Module 8.
Question 76Why do AHA and ERC differ on hyperkalemic arrest drugs?
Best: an International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) review found insufficient evidence for or against calcium or bicarbonate in arrest. Why not the others: both are 2025; the ERC applies to adults; AHA addresses hyperkalemia but not arrest during hemodialysis. Module 8.
Question 77Why must glucose be checked for several hours after insulin in a dialysis patient?
Best: reduced insulin clearance in kidney failure prolongs its effect. Why not the others: insulin lowers glucose; dialysis does not always cause hypoglycemia; patients without diabetes also become hypoglycemic. Module 8.
Question 78The potassium result is 7.8 mmol/L but the sample is hemolyzed and the ECG shows a wide QRS. What do you do?
Best: an abnormal ECG means treat now even if the sample may be falsely high. Why not the others: waiting or ignoring is dangerous with ECG changes; delay is unsafe. Module 8.
Question 79After ROSC from hyperkalemic arrest in a freestanding unit, who decides whether dialysis restarts?
Best: restarting weighs removal against hemodynamic risk; it is the attending nephrologist’s decision (Card 1). Why not the others: none of them holds that clinical authority. Module 8.
Question 80Which ERC regimen shifts potassium in a patient with circulation?
Best: insulin-glucose and salbutamol shift potassium into cells. Why not the others: calcium protects the membrane; the binder removes potassium slowly; bicarbonate is reserved for the arrest setting in the ERC guideline. Module 8.
Question 81Why can a venous needle dislodgement go unnoticed by the machine?
Best: the venous pressure alarm can stay silent when the needle is out. Why not the others: alarms stay on; the air detector does not detect dislodgement; loss can be rapid. Module 9.
Question 82You find blood under a blanket and the venous needle out. What are the first steps?
Best: these stop the loss and control bleeding (Card 4). Why not the others: reinsertion and returning through an open end risk infection and air; pump speed worsens loss. Module 9.
Question 83What is the danger of returning circuit blood after hemolysis?
Best: hemolyzed blood carries potassium and is never returned (Card 6). Why not the others: those are not the reason. Module 9.
Question 84One patient has port-wine blood in the lines. What should the charge nurse do next?
Best: hemolysis is often a supply problem affecting several patients. Why not the others: restarting before the cause is found is unsafe; the blood is never returned; waiting endangers others. Module 9.
Question 85Within five minutes of starting dialysis, a patient develops hives, wheeze and low pressure. What is first-line treatment?
Best: epinephrine IM comes first; antihistamines and steroids do not replace it; the circuit blood carries the trigger (Card 7). Why not the others: delay epinephrine or return the trigger. Module 9.
Question 86What is the key difference between anaphylaxis epinephrine and cardiac arrest epinephrine?
Best: route and concentration differ; mixing them up causes tenfold errors. Why not the others: they misstate routes or concentrations. Module 9.
Question 87A patient arrests from anaphylaxis. What does AHA 2025 say about fluids?
Best: fluid resuscitation is reasonable in anaphylactic arrest. Why not the others: none matches the guideline. Module 9.
Question 88A dialysis patient on an opioid has slow, shallow breathing, pinpoint pupils and a pulse. What comes first?
Best: respiratory arrest with a pulse needs ventilation; an antagonist should be given for suspected opioid overdose. Why not the others: there is a pulse; waiting risks arrest; atropine does not reverse opioids. Module 9.
Question 89In suspected opioid-associated cardiac arrest, when may naloxone be given?
Best: an antagonist may be reasonable in arrest from presumed overdose if it does not interrupt CPR. Why not the others: compressions come first; it does not replace the arrest algorithm; it is not restricted to after ROSC. Module 9.
Question 90A diabetic patient becomes confused and sweaty in the third hour of dialysis. What is an early check?
Best: low glucose is a common, treatable cause of confusion on dialysis (Card 11). Why not the others: they may matter later but do not catch the most likely treatable cause quickly. Module 9.
Question 91Which infant compression technique did the 2025 guidelines eliminate?
Best: two-finger compressions were eliminated because they often fail to reach depth. Why not the others: two-thumb encircling and heel of one hand are the recommended techniques; a firm surface is still preferred. Module 10.
Question 92What compression-to-ventilation ratio do two rescuers use for a child before puberty?
Best: two rescuers use 15:2 before puberty; one rescuer uses 30:2. Why not the others: 30:2 is single-rescuer or post-puberty; 5:1 is outdated; children need breaths. Module 10.
Question 93What is the usual sequence leading to cardiac arrest in children?
Best: most pediatric arrests are hypoxic. Why not the others: sudden VF is less common in children; the others are not the usual sequence. Module 10.
Question 94A child on dialysis has a heart rate of 50, poor perfusion, and is being ventilated effectively with oxygen. What next?
Best: start CPR for a rate under 60 with compromise despite effective oxygenated ventilation. Why not the others: waiting or stopping ventilation is unsafe; fluid alone does not treat hypoxic bradycardia. Module 10.
Question 95When is atropine recommended for pediatric bradycardia with compromise?
Best: PALS 2025 recommends atropine for vagal or AV-block bradycardia (COR 1, LOE C-LD); hypoxic bradycardia needs ventilation. Why not the others: it is not first for every case; it is not tied to epinephrine failure; it is used in children. Module 10.
Question 96An infant is choking and cannot cry or breathe. What is the 2025 sequence?
Best: infant foreign-body airway obstruction (FBAO) uses back blows and chest thrusts only. Why not the others: abdominal thrusts are for children and adults; blind sweeps can push the object deeper; dangling is not recommended. Module 10.
Question 97A 7-year-old is choking and cannot speak. What is the 2025 sequence?
Best: for children, the guideline alternates back blows and abdominal thrusts. Why not the others: chest thrusts alone are for infants (with back blows); back blows alone or water are not the sequence. Module 10.
Question 98A child arrests and the only AED has adult pads with no attenuator. What do you do?
Best: immediate AED use is recommended, with an attenuator if available; an adult AED is better than none. Why not the others: withholding or waiting leaves VF untreated. Module 10.
Question 99What compression depth is recommended for a child?
Best: one-third of the anteroposterior depth, about 5 cm in a child (about 4 cm in an infant). Why not the others: too shallow or too deep. Module 10.
Question 100With an advanced airway in place during pediatric CPR, what ventilation rate does PALS 2025 suggest?
Best: PALS 2025 suggests targeting 20 to 30 a minute (COR 2b, LOE C-LD). Why not the others: 10 a minute is the adult respiratory-arrest rate; faster is excessive; children need ventilation. Module 10.
Question 101What is the 2025 pediatric arrest epinephrine dose?
Best: this is the dose printed on the 2025 pediatric cardiac arrest algorithm. Why not the others: 0.1 mg/kg of 1 mg/mL IV is a hundredfold-level error (the 0.1 mg/kg dose was the old endotracheal dose); 1 mg is the adult dose and maximum; IM is for anaphylaxis. Module 11.
Question 102A 20 kg child needs arrest epinephrine. What volume of 0.1 mg/mL do you give?
Best: 0.01 mg/kg × 20 kg = 0.2 mg; at 0.1 mg/mL that is 2 mL. Why not the others: tenfold too little, tenfold too much, or the adult dose. Module 11.
Question 103When is epinephrine first given in pediatric asystole or PEA?
Best: PALS 2025 recommends the initial dose as early as possible in nonshockable arrest (COR 2a, LOE C-LD). Why not the others: shocks do not apply; delay and airway prerequisites are not recommended. Module 11.
Question 104What are the pediatric defibrillation energies in the 2025 algorithm?
Best: the algorithm prints 2, then 4, then up to 10 J/kg or the adult dose. Why not the others: reversed, adult-only, or cardioversion energies. Module 11.
Question 105A 25 kg child is in refractory VF. What is the amiodarone bolus?
Best: 5 mg/kg × 25 kg = 125 mg, below the 300 mg maximum. Why not the others: 300 mg is the adult first dose and the maximum; 25 mg is 1 mg/kg; 500 mg exceeds the maximum. Module 11.
Question 106A teammate reads ‘1 mg per mL’ from the epinephrine ampule during a pediatric arrest. What is the risk?
Best: the arrest volume (0.1 mL/kg) assumes 0.1 mg/mL; using 1 mg/mL gives ten times the dose. Why not the others: concentration determines the dose; it can be diluted for IV use per protocol. Module 11.
Question 107For pediatric SVT without compromise, what comes first?
Best: PALS 2025 recommends vagal maneuvers first (COR 1), then adenosine (COR 1). Why not the others: cardioversion is for compromise; amiodarone and defibrillation are not first-line here. Module 11.
Question 108What synchronized cardioversion energy is reasonable for pediatric SVT with compromise?
Best: PALS 2025 (COR 2a, LOE C-LD). Why not the others: those are defibrillation or adult energies. Module 11.
Question 109About what share of a child’s blood volume should total extracorporeal volume stay under, per European pediatric guidance?
Best: ESPN guidance says approximately less than 10% of blood volume (about 80 mL/kg). Why not the others: they do not match the guidance. Module 11.
Question 110A child’s weight today is questioned because of a scale error. Which weight do you use for doses?
Best: a reliable weight or tape estimate prevents dose errors. Why not the others: an unreliable reading, adult defaults or guesses risk large errors. Module 11.
Question 111How is ROSC confirmed?
Best: ROSC is a clinical finding of circulation. Why not the others: an organized rhythm may be PEA; color and elapsed time do not confirm circulation. Module 12.
Question 112After ROSC, what oxygen saturation should be targeted once it can be measured reliably?
Best: AHA 2025 recommends titrating to peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) 90% to 98% (COR 2a, LOE B-R), after 100% until reliable measurement. Why not the others: prolonged 100% and low targets are not recommended. Module 12.
Question 113What blood pressure target does AHA 2025 recommend after ROSC?
Best: prevent hypotension with a MAP of at least 65 mm Hg (COR 1, LOE B-R). Why not the others: they are unsafe or not guideline targets. Module 12.
Question 114Why should the defibrillator pads stay on after ROSC?
Best: the restarted heart is irritable and may fibrillate again. Why not the others: they are not the reason. Module 12.
Question 115A patient has ROSC after a potassium-related arrest. A nurse suggests restarting dialysis while waiting for the ambulance. What is correct?
Best: restarting is the attending nephrologist’s decision (Card 1); an unstable patient may re-arrest when a circuit starts. Why not the others: each restarts without the decision-maker. Module 12.
Question 116When should a 12-lead ECG be recorded after ROSC?
Best: AHA 2025 recommends a 12-lead ECG as soon as feasible for all adults after ROSC (COR 1, LOE B-NR). Why not the others: delay or selective use misses ST elevation that changes the destination. Module 12.
Question 117Which dialysis facts must be in the handover?
Best: these facts shape the receiving team’s volume and potassium decisions. Why not the others: they are irrelevant to immediate care. Module 12.
Question 118What does AHA 2025 say about temperature after ROSC in patients who do not follow commands?
Best: protocolized temperature control at 32 to 37.5 °C (COR 1) for at least 36 hours (COR 2a). Why not the others: they are outside the recommended range. Module 12.
Question 119Saturation reads 92% in a dark-skinned patient after ROSC who looks dusky and breathes irregularly. What is a caution from the 2025 guideline?
Best: AHA 2025 notes this limitation (COR 2b, LOE C-LD), so assess the patient too. Why not the others: they misstate the limitation. Module 12.
Question 120What glucose range may it be reasonable to maintain after ROSC?
Best: AHA 2025 (COR 2b, LOE B-NR). Why not the others: they are unsafe. Module 12.
Question 121What are the three parts of closed-loop communication?
Best: naming, reading back and confirming prevents missed and doubled actions. Why not the others: orders to the room and written-only orders lose ownership. Module 13.
Question 122The attending nephrologist arrives mid-arrest. What should happen?
Best: an explicit leadership handover keeps one voice. Why not the others: two leaders cause conflicting orders; silent takeovers confuse the team; pausing stops compressions. Module 13.
Question 123Why does the watcher never join compressions?
Best: every other circuit keeps running; in the case, a patient’s pressure fell unnoticed. Why not the others: the watcher may be trained; it is a dialysis unit rule, not an AHA rule; rest is not the reason. Module 13.
Question 124What is the main purpose of a debrief?
Best: debriefs turn events into system fixes; structured debriefs are associated with better learning. Why not the others: blame discourages honesty; reports and family communication are separate tasks. Module 13.
Question 125What are the five debrief questions in this course?
Best: these five lead to owned actions. Why not the others: blame-focused, numbers-only or outcome-only reviews miss system fixes. Module 13.
Question 126Why are clinical, team and system performance reported separately?
Best: separate reports point to separate fixes. Why not the others: they are not the reason; public rankings are avoided. Module 13.
Question 127A browser-based drill timer shows a compression fraction of 85%. What must the report state?
Best: every measure states where it came from; browser buttons record timing, not quality. Why not the others: they overstate what the number means. Module 13.
Question 128What does the 2026 systematic review say about spaced simulation training?
Best: 15 studies; similar overall, some retention benefit; no patient outcomes; no optimal interval for dialysis units. Why not the others: they overstate or misstate the findings. Module 13.
Question 129A patient’s daughter arrives during a resuscitation. What is best practice?
Best: families need support and information; presence, when allowed, is supported by a staff member. Why not the others: isolation, decisions under duress, or unsupported presence harm families and the team. Module 13.
Question 130A safety-critical error happens in a drill that otherwise scores 92%. What follows?
Best: safety-critical errors need repeat practice even when the aggregate is high. Why not the others: ignoring, punishing or lowering standards does not fix the step. Module 13.

Abbreviations

ACLS
Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support.
AED
Automated external defibrillator — A portable device that can restart a normal heart rhythm with an electric shock.
AF
Atrial fibrillation — A fast, irregular rhythm with no organized atrial activity.
AHA
American Heart Association.
AV
Atrioventricular — Relating to the conduction between the upper and lower chambers of the heart.
BLS
Basic Life Support.
BRASH
Bradycardia, renal failure, atrioventricular nodal blockade, shock, and hyperkalemia — A vicious cycle in which potassium and AV-nodal drugs together slow the heart.
COR
Class of Recommendation — The AHA grade of how strongly a guideline recommends an action (1, 2a, 2b, 3).
CPR
Cardiopulmonary resuscitation.
DOH
Department of Health — Philippines.
ECG
Electrocardiogram.
ERC
European Resuscitation Council — The body that writes European resuscitation guidelines.
ETCO2
End-tidal carbon dioxide — Carbon dioxide measured at the end of each breath; reflects blood flow during CPR.
FBAO
Foreign-body airway obstruction — Choking on food or an object.
ILCOR
International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation — The international group that reviews resuscitation science.
IM
Intramuscular — Given into a muscle, such as the outer thigh.
IO
Intraosseous — A needle into the bone marrow cavity used to give drugs when no vein is available.
IV
Intravenous.
LOE
Level of Evidence — The AHA grade of the quality of evidence behind a recommendation (A, B-R, B-NR, C-LD, C-EO).
MAP
Mean arterial pressure — The average pressure that drives blood into the organs.
PALS
Pediatric Advanced Life Support — The American Heart Association course name for pediatric advanced resuscitation.
PEA
Pulseless electrical activity — An organized rhythm on the monitor with no palpable pulse. Not shockable.
PSN
Philippine Society of Nephrology.
pVT
Pulseless ventricular tachycardia — Ventricular tachycardia with no pulse; treated like VF. Shockable.
ROSC
Return of spontaneous circulation — The heart pumps on its own again: a definite pulse and blood pressure.
SpO2
Peripheral oxygen saturation — Oxygen saturation measured by a pulse oximeter.
SVT
Supraventricular tachycardia — A fast, usually regular, narrow-complex rhythm from above the ventricles.
VF
Ventricular fibrillation — Chaotic electrical activity of the ventricles; no pumping and no pulse. Shockable.
VT
Ventricular tachycardia — A fast rhythm from the ventricles; may have a pulse or not.
