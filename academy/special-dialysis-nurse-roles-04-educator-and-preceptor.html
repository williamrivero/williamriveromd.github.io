Role subtrack for registered nurses

This subtrack prepares Philippine registered nurses who are assigned a special portfolio in a hemodialysis unit: vascular access, infection prevention, education, quality, safety, coordination, anemia, transplant liaison, records, water and equipment liaison, or adequacy and outcomes. It teaches assessment, coordination, teaching and escalation within nursing scope. Prescriptions, dose changes, access procedures, transplant listing, water-system repair and technical release belong to the physician, the authorized team and the technical staff. All cases are synthetic.

Learning objectives · Special Dialysis Nurse Roles (SDNR)-03 · about 8 hours · week 2

After this module you should be able to

  • Distinguish a training gap from a system gap and turn a real gap into observable learning objectives.
  • Teach a dialysis task in stages, from demonstration to supervised practice to independence.
  • Assess observed performance against explicit critical steps, and calibrate with other assessors.
  • Document feedback, a usable remediation plan, and the limits of what a sign-off covers.

Time plan. 2 h study · 2 h workshop · 4 h supervised practice. The supervised hours are arranged by your unit and recorded in its own competency system; this page records self-study only.

Intuition

A driving instructor, not a lecturer

Nobody learns to drive from a lecture. A good driving instructor explains briefly, shows once, then sits beside the learner on an empty road, then a quiet street, then traffic, and only signs the logbook after watching a full drive without having to grab the wheel. The instructor also notices when the problem is the car (bad brakes) rather than the driver.

The picture has a limit. A driving test happens once. In dialysis, the "road" changes: a new machine model, a new catheter cap, a new disinfectant, a patient with a difficult access. Competence is not a permanent badge. It is a statement that this nurse did this task safely on this date, and it needs rechecking when the task, the equipment or the nurse's practice changes. The educator designs the lessons; the preceptor sits in the passenger seat. This module covers both, and keeps their tasks separate.

In plain wordsTeach in stages, watch the real task, sign only what you saw, and write down what the signature does not cover.
Analysis

Start with the gap, not the slide deck

When something goes wrong, the reflex is to schedule a lecture. Often the lecture is the wrong fix. Before teaching anything, ask whether the problem is that people do not know how (a training gap) or that the system makes the right action hard (a system gap).

QuestionIf yes, it is probablyThe fix
Could the nurse do it correctly if her life depended on it?A system gapChange supplies, layout, staffing, checklists or time; teaching will not help
Has the nurse never been taught or never practiced it?A training gapTeach, rehearse, supervise, assess
Was she taught, but long ago, or on a different device?A skill decay or change gapRefresher and reassessment
Does she know but skip it under pressure?Often a system gap (time, staffing) with a culture elementFix the pressure; then coach

Write objectives you can see. "Understand catheter care" cannot be observed. "Performs catheter disconnection on a training arm with every critical step and no break in asepsis" can. Use action verbs (performs, demonstrates, explains in her own words, identifies) and state the condition and the standard.

The lecture that would not have helped · fictional

After two infiltrations in a week, the manager asks Nurse educator Mylene to give a cannulation lecture. She looks first. Both infiltrations happened on first cannulations of new fistulas, both by nurses in their first three months, both on Saturday when the senior cannulators were off.

The gap is not knowledge; it is assignment. She proposes a rule that new fistulas are cannulated only by signed-off senior nurses until the access map says otherwise, and a supervised practice plan so that two junior nurses can be signed off for complex access over the next two months.

What this shows. A needs analysis found a system gap and a training gap, and neither was fixed by a lecture.

Physiology of learning

How a skill moves from the head to the hands

Motor skills are usually described as passing through three stages. In the cognitive stage the learner thinks through every step and needs full attention; errors are frequent. In the associative stage the steps link together and errors fall, but stress or interruption still breaks the sequence. In the autonomous stage the routine runs with little conscious effort, freeing attention for the patient. A nurse who knows the steps on paper is in the first stage. Only rehearsal moves her to the second, and only repeated supervised practice to the third.

  1. ExplainWhy each critical step matters, linked to the mechanism (for example, why the hub is never touched after scrubbing).
  2. DemonstrateAt normal speed, then slowly with commentary.
  3. RehearseOn a simulator or training arm, with immediate correction, until no critical step is missed.
  4. Supervised practiceOn patients, preceptor beside the learner, stepping in only for safety.
  5. Graded independencePreceptor nearby, then available, as performance holds.
  6. ReassessWhen the device, procedure or performance changes, and at the unit's set interval.

What the evidence shows. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 609 studies with 35,226 health professional trainees found that technology-enhanced simulation, compared with no intervention, was consistently associated with large improvements in knowledge and skills and moderate improvements in patient-related outcomes.1 Most included studies were single-group before-and-after designs and heterogeneity was large, so the size of the benefit is uncertain; the direction is consistent. Simulation is not a luxury: a training arm and an unconnected machine are enough for most dialysis skills.

RememberRehearse on a simulator until the critical steps are automatic, before the learner's first real patient.

Psychological safety. Learners hide uncertainty when mistakes are punished. Say at the start of every session that stopping to ask is expected, agree a word the learner can use to pause ("Time out"), and thank the learner who speaks up. A learner who says "I'm not sure" is the one you can still teach.

Practice

Assess what was actually done

Direct observation against critical steps. Every assessment checklist separates critical steps (missing one means not yet competent, regardless of everything else) from quality steps (feedback, not failure). For catheter disconnection, breaking asepsis at the hub is critical; a slightly slow sequence is not.

95 percent on paper, one touch in practice · fictional

Nurse Kim, three months into orientation, scores 95 percent on the catheter-care quiz. During her observed disconnection, after scrubbing the hub for the full time, she steadies it with her ungloved fingertip. Orientation officially ends tomorrow, and the manager asks preceptor Joanne to sign the form so Kim can take a full patient group.

Joanne does not sign the catheter-care line. She records exactly what she observed, gives Kim immediate private feedback, and sets a remediation plan: two simulator rehearsals this week and three observed disconnections next week. She signs the other lines Kim has met. She tells the manager which tasks Kim may do alone and which still need supervision.

What this shows. A quiz shows knowledge; only observation shows the hands. A missed critical step is "not yet," and the record says why.

Calibrate the assessors. Two preceptors watching the same video of a task should reach the same decision. Calibrate at least once a year: watch a recorded or simulated performance together, score it separately, then discuss differences. Common biases include leniency (passing a friend), halo (a good nurse must be good at everything), and recency (judging by the last session only).

Educator and preceptor are different jobs. The educator designs the program, the objectives and the checklists, and runs simulation. The preceptor teaches and observes at the bedside. One person can hold both roles, but an educator title does not by itself make someone a competent assessor of every procedure. A preceptor may sign only tasks she is competent in herself.

StopNever sign a competency because orientation has ended, the roster is short, or a manager asks. A signature is a statement about observed safety.
Practice

Make remediation usable

A useful remediation plan is specific, short and supportive: the exact step missed, the reason it matters, the practice plan, the supervision level until reassessment, and the reassessment date. It is shared with the learner first and kept in the facility's training record, not in a group chat.

ObservedWhat exactly happened, in neutral words.
Why it mattersThe mechanism: "touching the hub after scrubbing returns skin bacteria to the catheter lumen."
PracticeSimulator sessions, observed patient tasks, reading.
Until thenWhat the learner may do alone and what needs supervision.
ReassessDate, assessor, checklist.
Escalate ifRepeated failure, or a safety concern beyond training (fatigue, illness, impairment): refer to the head nurse.

Competency records. Each signed task carries the date, the assessor, the checklist version, the level reached, and a trigger for reassessment (a new device, a new procedure, an incident, or the unit's interval). These records belong in the facility's system. The Academy records self-study only.

Worked example · Portfolio Toolkit, tool 1Of 14 nurses due for catheter-care reassessment this quarter, 12 were reassessed and 10 performed every critical step. Report 10 of 14 (71 percent) as the measure, not 10 of 12, because the two not reassessed are part of the denominator. The 95 percent range is about 45 to 88 percent. The balancing measure is protected preceptor time: hours available for observation divided by hours planned.
Local practice

In a Philippine unit

The Department of Health (DOH) requires dialysis nurses to hold a renal dialysis training certificate and current basic life support (BLS), and the Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) describes structured renal nursing preparation of three months or more.2,3 Those requirements set the entry floor. They do not tell a unit whether this nurse can safely do this task on this machine. That is the unit's continuing job, and the educator and preceptor carry it.

Practical constraints are real: few senior nurses, tight rosters and high turnover as nurses leave for overseas posts. Units manage by protecting preceptor time on the roster, using simulation for rare or risky tasks (emergency disconnection, venous needle dislodgement, power failure), and building a small library of short checklists shared by all assessors. The National Health Service (NHS) England educator framework describes educator roles at progressive levels, a model that can help a Philippine unit plan who teaches what.4

Practice case · synthetic

Case: Sign the form, orientation is over

Friday, 3:30 p.m. Nurse Kim has finished her three-month orientation. Her quiz scores are excellent. You have observed her catheter disconnections three times: twice correct, and today she touched the scrubbed hub with an ungloved finger. She also has not yet performed an emergency disconnection on a simulator.

The manager needs her on a full patient group from Monday because two nurses resigned. "Please just sign everything. She'll learn on the job."

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, whom you would contact and what you would say, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the debrief below.

Assess

Separate what Kim has shown (most tasks) from what she has not (aseptic hub handling, emergency disconnection). One critical-step miss means 'not yet' for that task.

Communicate

Give Kim private, specific feedback today. Tell the manager which tasks you can sign and which you cannot, and propose how she can start Monday safely: catheter patients assigned to another nurse or her disconnections supervised until reassessed.

Teach

Arrange two simulator rehearsals of hub handling and one emergency disconnection simulation before Wednesday.

Escalate

If pressure to sign continues, bring it to the head nurse with the observation record. Do not sign what you did not see.
How it played out · fictional

The head nurse agreed. Kim started Monday with fistula patients only and supervised catheter tasks. She passed both reassessments the following week, and her record shows the dates, the checklists and the assessor.

Documentation task. Write Kim's remediation plan using the six headings in this module, and the one-paragraph note to the manager listing what is signed and what is not.
Second round · what changesThe unit buys a new machine model next month. Three senior nurses say they 'already know machines' and do not need training.
Submit: A short training-needs plan that separates what is new on the device, who must be reassessed on which critical steps, and how the reassessment will be recorded.

Supervised practice

With your preceptor

Use a training arm, simulator or unconnected machine for rehearsal, and real patients only under supervision. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own protocols, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record.

  • Write three observable objectives for one dialysis task and have the educator review them.
  • Deliver a 10-minute microteaching session using explain, demonstrate and rehearse.
  • Observe and score two colleagues on the same checklist with another assessor; compare and discuss differences.
  • Write one remediation plan for a fictional learner.
  • Audit the unit's competency records for reassessment triggers after a device or procedure change.

Terms

Critical step
A step whose omission makes the performance unsafe, so the learner is 'not yet competent' for that task.
Calibration
Assessors scoring the same performance separately and reconciling differences, to make decisions consistent.
Training gap
A problem caused by not knowing or not having practiced how to do something.
System gap
A problem caused by supplies, layout, time or workload that makes the right action hard.
