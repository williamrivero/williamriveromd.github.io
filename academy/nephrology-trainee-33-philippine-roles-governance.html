Training track for nephrology fellows, residents and medical interns

Study material for medical interns, residents and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and gives no drug doses. Doses, rates and order sets come from current guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved protocols and your supervising physician. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Intern: Separate licensure, appointment and clinical privileges, and name the registered physician who supervises each task you do as a trainee under Republic Act (RA) 2382.
  • Resident: Check your own eligibility before you accept a physician-on-duty (POD) shift, against the Department of Health (DOH) order, the Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) 2024 guidelines and the unit’s written appointment.
  • Fellow: Navigate DOH facility licensing, PSN standards, the Data Privacy Act and adverse-event reporting, and lead a learning response when care goes wrong.

Start here

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · knowing which key opens which doorThe intern owns one question: who is supervising me for this task, and do they know I am doing it? The resident owns a second: am I allowed to be here, in this post, tonight, on the documents and not just on the roster? The fellow owns a third: when something goes wrong in a unit I help run, does the system learn from it, and are the records true? None of these questions is clinical in the narrow sense. All of them decide whether patients are safe.
In plain wordsThink of a hospital as a building with many locked doors and a ring of separate keys. Your professional license is the key to the profession. Your appointment is the key to this particular building. Your clinical privileges are keys to particular rooms inside it. The dialysis unit itself needs a building permit, and the payer has its own contract with the building. Holding one key does not open the other doors.

The picture has a limit. Keys are all or nothing; real authority is graded. An intern is allowed to examine a patient but only under the direct supervision of a registered physician. A resident is licensed but works inside a training program that sets what she may sign. A fellow may run the dialysis rounds yet still answers to a consultant. Keys can also be copied on paper without the competence behind them: a name in a logbook is not the same as a physician in the room. Governance exists to keep the paper and the reality the same.

Most governance questions you will meet are ordinary: whose name goes on the order, whether a photograph may go into a group chat, who is told when a patient falls. A few are sharp: a request to sign for a shift you did not cover, an offer of a POD post you are not eligible for, a death that someone wants left out of the logbook. Your work is to know the systems well enough to answer the ordinary questions without fuss, and to recognize the sharp ones early enough to say no, call the attending, and write things down as they happened.

Opening case · fictional

Case: The logbook on Saturday morning

Saturday, 7:40 a.m., a freestanding hemodialysis unit with 16 stations in Malolos, Bulacan. Dr. Paolo Santiago is a second-year internal medicine resident in a private hospital in Quezon City. He has worked weekend POD shifts at this unit for four months; before that he had no dialysis experience. He has a current Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) card. He has not taken a PSN post-graduate POD course.

The unit manager, Ms. Lorna Cruz, hands him the POD logbook. “Doc, Thursday afternoon is blank. Dr. Reyes had to leave at two for a family emergency and the second shift ran with the nurses. Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) and the regional DOH office may come for a visit next week. Can you sign Thursday for us? You know the patients anyway.” The nurses’ notes for Thursday show that one patient, Mr. Isidro Valdez, 71, had a fall in pressure to 78/44 mm Hg at hour three, was laid back and given saline by the nurse, and recovered. No physician saw him. The complications logbook has no entry for it.

Ms. Cruz adds that the unit is short of PODs and asks whether Paolo can also take the full Sunday schedule alone, all 16 stations.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What does Paolo do about the request to sign Thursday’s logbook, and what does he do instead?
  • Is Paolo eligible to work as a POD in this freestanding unit at all, on the DOH order and the PSN guidelines as written?
  • What should happen to Thursday’s gap in physician coverage and Mr. Valdez’s episode, so that the unit learns from it?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Licensure, appointment, privileges: the separate systems

Philippine kidney care sits inside several systems that do different jobs and rarely talk to each other. Confusing them is the root of most governance mistakes a trainee makes. The causal chain is simple. Each system checks one thing. If you assume that passing one check means passing the others, you end up doing work that no one has actually authorized.

The license. The Medical Act of 1959, RA 2382, governs medical education, the licensure examination and the practice of medicine. Its Section 11(a) exempts a medical student under training who serves without a professional fee in a government or private hospital, provided the service is rendered under the direct supervision and control of a registered physician.1 That clause is the legal frame for unlicensed trainee work. Ask your school and your hospital how it applies to your internship year, and assume that it covers supervised training work, not independent practice. The Professional Regulation Commission (PRC) issues and renews the license and the professional identification card.2

The facility. A hemodialysis center operates under a license to operate issued under DOH Administrative Order (AO) 2012-0001. The order sets the personnel requirements, including those for the POD, the nurses and the medical director, the logbooks the unit must keep, and the penalties: the licensing office may suspend, cancel or revoke the license of a facility found violating the order.3

The profession. The PSN writes professional standards, such as its 2024 hemodialysis guidelines, accredits nephrology training institutions and runs the specialty board.4,5 Its bylaws separate member-in-training, board-eligible and diplomate status from the membership title of Fellow.6 A nephrology fellow-in-training is not a Fellow of the PSN; never let a letterhead or a clinic sign say so.

The payer. The Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) accredits facilities and pays claims under its own circulars, including quality standards for maintenance hemodialysis.7 Module 34 covers the benefits.

The hospital. Your appointment letter and the credentialing committee’s grant of privileges decide what you may do in that institution. A license allows you to practice medicine in the country; it does not admit you to a particular hospital or let you do a particular procedure there.

There is little trial evidence on governance itself, and this module does not pretend otherwise. The case for these systems rests on regulation, law and the logic of accountability, plus observational and quality-improvement evidence that clear roles and structured communication reduce error. A multicenter study of a structured resident handoff program, in 10,740 admissions across nine hospitals, found medical errors fell from 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions and preventable adverse events from 4.7 to 3.3 per 100 admissions after implementation.8 It was a before-and-after design without randomization, so it shows association, not proof that the program caused the fall; it does show that who tells whom what, and how, is measurable and improvable.

At your stageIntern: before any task, name the registered physician who supervises it, and make sure that person knows. Resident: keep copies of your license, appointment and privileges where you can produce them on a shift. Fellow: know which of your unit’s rules come from DOH, which from PSN, which from PhilHealth and which are local, because the consequences of breaking each are different.
RememberOne key does not open another door. Before you act, ask which system authorizes this task: your license, your training program, your hospital, the unit’s DOH license, or none of them.
Lesson · part 2

Checking your eligibility before you take a POD shift

The moonlighting POD shift is where the separate systems collide. A unit needs a physician in the room for every session. A resident needs income. The roster gets filled. The question that is easy to skip is whether the person on the roster is actually eligible under the rules that license that unit.

What the DOH order says. Under AO 2012-0001, the POD attends to patients in the center under the supervision of the medical director, must be licensed by the PRC, must have experience in the care of patients with kidney failure, must hold updated ACLS training, and must be present during the hours of operation. The order lists the routes to eligibility: board certified or board eligible in nephrology; board certified or board eligible in internal medicine or pediatrics with at least 3 months of work in a DOH-licensed hemodialysis center; undergoing an accredited residency in internal medicine or pediatrics, a route the order limits to training hospitals; or a general practitioner with at least 6 months of work in a DOH-licensed center. The ratio of POD to stations shall not be more than 1:15.3

What the PSN 2024 guidelines add. The PSN sets a priority order: board-certified or board-eligible nephrologists first, then nephrology fellows-in-training in hospital-based centers, then qualified internists or pediatricians with center experience. Internal medicine residents may serve as POD in hospitals with a Philippine College of Physicians (PCP)-accredited program as their training manual stipulates, with at least 3 months of center experience; residents of private hospitals need at least 6 months of work in a DOH-licensed center. The POD needs updated ACLS from a DOH-accredited provider and an updated certificate from a PSN-conducted or PSN-accredited post-graduate POD course, must be physically present, and makes no referral to another specialty without the knowledge of the attending nephrologist or the Dialysis Clinical Head. The ratio is ideally 1:15, with up to 1:20 allowed where PODs are scarce and patients’ needs can still be met.4

Notice where the two documents differ. The DOH order is a regulation that licenses the facility; the PSN guideline is a professional standard. The DOH order caps the ratio at 1:15; the PSN guideline allows up to 1:20 in scarcity. The PSN guideline asks for a POD course that the DOH order does not name. Regional DOH offices also use their own assessment tools, and amendments may exist that this module has not seen. When the documents disagree, the unit’s license is judged against the DOH order and its current issuances, and a doubt about your eligibility is resolved by the licensing office through the unit’s management, not by you on the roster.

  1. Read the appointmentAsk the unit for a written appointment that names you as POD and the medical director or Dialysis Clinical Head who supervises you.
  2. Match your routeFind your route in the DOH order and the PSN guideline. Write down which one you meet and how.
  3. Count the stationsAsk how many stations run per shift and how many PODs cover them.
  4. Find the attendingGet the attending nephrologist’s number and the escalation list. Confirm that someone answers at night and on weekends.
  5. Read the standing ordersRead the unit’s protocols for hypotension, chest pain, hyperkalemia and cardiac arrest before your first session.
  6. Check your own programConfirm that your residency or fellowship allows outside work and how many hours.

Signing is the sharpest part of the POD role. A logbook, a treatment sheet or a claim form is a record that a particular physician was present and responsible at a particular time. The DOH order makes presence during operating hours a POD requirement, and the PSN guideline repeats it.3,4 Signing for a shift you did not cover makes a record false. It hides the coverage gap that the next licensing visit should have found, and it hides the patient who was treated without a physician. A false entry in a medical or claim record can also carry legal consequences for the person who signs it. No amount of goodwill toward a short-staffed unit changes that.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist, per unit protocol, but always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance, when:
  • you are asked to sign for a shift, a session or a patient you did not cover;
  • you are asked to cover more stations alone than the DOH ratio allows;
  • you arrive and there are no written standing orders, no working emergency cart, or no reachable attending;
  • you realize partway through a shift that you do not meet the eligibility route the unit assumed.
At your stageIntern: you are never the POD and never “watch the unit”; if asked, say so and call your supervising resident. Resident: do the checklist above once for every unit and keep it with your papers. Fellow: when you help run a unit, check the eligibility of every POD on the roster, not only your own.
RememberA POD is a physician in the room who meets a written route to eligibility. If either half is missing, the shift is not covered, whatever the logbook says.
Lesson · part 3

Records that tell the truth, and privacy that holds

A medical record does three jobs at once. It is the clinical memory that the next physician relies on. It is the legal account of what was done and by whom. It is the evidence behind a PhilHealth claim. A record that fails one job usually fails all three. The causal chain runs like this: a missing or false entry leads the next clinician to a wrong assumption, the claim to a payment that should not have been made, and the inquiry, if one comes, to a conclusion that protects no one.

The DOH order requires each patient record to be kept confidential and states that a patient’s right to obtain records of treatment and other relevant medical information shall be observed. It also requires a complications and adverse events logbook that includes delayed discharges, admissions after dialysis, deaths during or right after dialysis, dialysis and vascular access complications, disease complications and outcomes.3 The PSN guideline makes the medical records officer the custodian of records and asks that informed consent be witnessed by two people who also sign.4

The Data Privacy Act. RA 10173, the Data Privacy Act of 2012, treats information about an individual’s health as sensitive personal information and requires that personal data be processed with transparency, for a legitimate purpose and in proportion to that purpose.9 In practice, this law reaches the places where trainees handle data most carelessly: the group chat, the phone camera, the shared spreadsheet and the case presentation.

The rules are not there to slow you down. A record you would be comfortable having read aloud, in front of the patient and the licensing officer, is usually a record that is both safe and useful. Viber orders are a particular trap: an order sent by message is still an order, and the unit’s protocol should say how it is confirmed, written and countersigned. If the unit has no such rule, ask the attending to write one.

At your stageIntern: document what you saw and did, at the time, and sign with your name and status. Resident: never sign for another physician’s work, and never let another sign for yours. Fellow: teach the team how to share images and results safely, and model it.
RememberWrite what happened, when it happened, as it happened. Label anything late, never erase, and share health data only with people who need it to care for the patient.
Lesson · part 4

When care goes wrong: reporting, learning and quality

Errors in a dialysis unit are rarely the work of one careless person. They grow from conditions: a short-staffed shift, a protocol nobody updated, a machine alarm everyone has learned to silence, a POD who left early and was never replaced. If the response to an adverse event is to find the nearest person and blame them, staff learn to stop reporting, the conditions stay, and the event repeats. If the response is to report, look at the system and fix it, the same event becomes less likely. That is the causal logic of a reporting culture.

The DOH national patient safety policy for health facilities, AO 2020-0007, revises and enhances the earlier national policy and sets patient safety as a program in health facilities.10 For dialysis, the DOH order gives the concrete minimum: the complications and adverse events logbook described in the previous lesson.3 The PSN guideline adds a monthly continuous quality improvement (CQI) meeting led by the Dialysis Clinical Head, a medical audit that covers ACLS activations, ambulance transfers, successful resuscitations and deaths, and risk metrics that include falls, needle injuries, medication errors and blood spills.4 PhilHealth’s maintenance hemodialysis quality standards add a payer’s view of the same work.7

What the evidence shows. Quality improvement can change outcomes that patients feel, but most of the evidence comes from designs weaker than a trial. In a collaborative of 17 outpatient hemodialysis facilities supported by the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, a package of chlorhexidine for catheter exit sites, staff training and competency checks, hand-hygiene and access-care audits, and feedback of rates to staff was followed by a fall in pooled bloodstream infection rates from 1.09 to 0.89 per 100 patient-months, and access-related bloodstream infections from 0.73 to 0.42 per 100 patient-months; modeled rates fell 32% and 54% at the start of the intervention.11 The facilities volunteered, most were hospital based, and there was no control group, so the result is an association with a plausible mechanism, not proof that every unit would see the same. Bloodstream infection is a real clinical outcome, not a surrogate.

A coverage gap, like Thursday’s in the case, is itself an incident. A session ran without a physician; a patient had a fall in pressure that no physician assessed. The useful questions are about conditions. Why was there no backup when Dr. Reyes had to leave? Is there a call list of replacement PODs? Did the nurses know whom to call? Was the attending told? Hiding the gap with a signature destroys the one piece of information that would have prompted the fix.

At your stageIntern: report what you see, including near misses, to your resident and in the hospital’s system. Resident: write incident entries as facts and times, and attend the review when invited. Fellow: run or join the CQI meeting, ask what condition allowed the event, and choose one measurable change.
RememberReport the event, fix the condition. A unit that punishes the reporter will stop hearing about its own errors.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The intern asked to watch the unit

What you see
At 6 a.m. in a hospital-based dialysis unit in Tarlac City, the POD has not arrived and a nurse asks the medical intern to “just watch the patients until Doc comes.”
What is likely going on
An unlicensed trainee is being asked to stand in for a POD.
What to do
Decline politely, stay to help as a trainee if useful, and call the supervising resident and the attending nephrologist at once so a physician comes.
Why it matters
RA 2382 frames trainee work as directly supervised; an intern in the POD role leaves patients without a physician.

The photograph in the group chat

What you see
A resident in Davao City sees a classmate post a dialysis patient’s fistula photograph, with the patient’s face, in a large batch group chat asking whether it is infected.
What is likely going on
Sensitive health information shared far beyond the care team.
What to do
Message the classmate privately to delete it, suggest the hospital’s approved consult channel, and raise the practice at the next department meeting without naming anyone.
Why it matters
The Data Privacy Act treats health data as sensitive personal information; images in large groups cannot be recalled.

The order by Viber

What you see
During a Sunday shift in Cainta, Rizal, the attending sends a Viber message changing a patient’s fluid removal goal. The unit has no rule on message orders.
What is likely going on
A valid clinical instruction with no defined way to be recorded and countersigned.
What to do
Read the order back by call, write it in the record as a telephone or message order with the time and the attending’s name, have it countersigned per policy, and ask the attending to write a unit rule.
Why it matters
An order that exists only on a phone is invisible to the next shift and to any review.

The needle-stick at the end of the shift

What you see
A dialysis nurse in Lipa City sustains a needle-stick while disconnecting a patient with hepatitis B and wants to “not make a fuss.”
What is likely going on
An occupational exposure that needs immediate assessment and an incident record.
What to do
Follow the unit’s exposure protocol at once, inform the head nurse and attending, record it as an incident, and bring the circumstances to the CQI review.
Why it matters
Needle injuries are a PSN risk metric; unreported exposures deny the nurse care and the unit its lesson.
Field notes
  • Keep photographs of your license, appointment, ACLS card and POD course certificate in one folder you can show on any shift.
  • Before your first shift at a new unit, read its standing orders and find the attending’s number; save it under the unit’s name.
  • If someone asks you to sign something you did not do, say “I can write what I saw, but I cannot sign for what I did not do,” and call the attending.
  • Write incident entries as times and facts; leave conclusions to the review.
  • Learn which of your unit’s rules come from DOH, PSN, PhilHealth or local policy; it changes whom you call when a rule cannot be met.
Case review

Back to the case: The logbook on Saturday morning

The logbook. Paolo does not sign. He did not cover Thursday afternoon, so a signature would make the record false and would hide both the coverage gap and an unassessed hypotensive episode. He explains this to Ms. Cruz without accusing anyone, and he calls the attending nephrologist and the Dialysis Clinical Head. Instead of a signature, the unit needs a true account: the nurses’ notes as written, a late entry in the complications logbook for Mr. Valdez’s episode, labeled as late, and a written note of the coverage gap. If a licensing or PhilHealth visit comes, the unit shows the gap and what it did about it.

Eligibility. On the documents as written, Paolo does not meet a route. The DOH order allows residents in an accredited internal medicine program to serve as POD only in training hospitals, and this is a freestanding unit. Its general-practitioner route needs 6 months of center work; he has 4. The PSN guideline asks private-hospital residents for 6 months of center experience and a PSN POD course certificate, which he lacks. He should stop taking shifts here until the unit has his eligibility verified with the DOH licensing office, and he should check whether his own residency allows outside work. Covering all 16 stations alone on Sunday would also breach the 1:15 ratio in the order.

Learning from Thursday. Thursday’s gap and Mr. Valdez’s episode go to the attending now and to the next CQI meeting, as an incident to learn from, not a fault to bury. The review asks why there was no replacement when a POD left, whether the nurses had a call list, and whether the attending was told. One change follows, such as a written backup POD roster with a named on-call physician, and the unit tracks uncovered hours each month. Mr. Valdez and his family are told what happened, and his next sessions are reviewed by the attending.

Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 5 questions at random from this module’s 5-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 4 of 5 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 5A medical intern on a medicine rotation is asked by a nurse to adjust an intravenous fluid order because “the resident is busy.” What is the intern’s correct position?
RA 2382 Section 11(a) frames trainee service as service under the direct supervision and control of a registered physician. Enrollment in a program (the tempting distractor) does not make the intern an independent prescriber.
Question 2 of 5A second-year internal medicine resident from a private hospital, with 4 months of dialysis experience and no PSN POD course, is offered POD shifts at a freestanding unit. Which statement fits the DOH order and the PSN 2024 guideline as written?
The DOH residency route is limited to training hospitals; the general-practitioner route and the PSN private-resident route need 6 months of center work, and PSN adds a POD course. ACLS alone (the best distractor) is necessary but not sufficient.
Question 3 of 5A fellow on the quality committee is told that a patient’s fall in the dialysis unit should be left out of the logbook “to avoid problems with the inspection.” What is the best response?
The DOH order requires a complications and adverse events logbook and PSN asks for monthly CQI with falls among the risk metrics. Keeping it in nurses’ notes only (the best distractor) hides it from the review that could prevent the next one.
Question 4 of 5At 10 p.m. the POD is asked by the unit owner to cover 19 stations alone because a colleague is sick. What should the POD do?
The DOH order caps POD coverage at 1:15. The PSN 1:20 allowance (the tempting distractor) is a professional standard for scarcity, not a waiver of the licensing order, and the decision belongs with the attending and management, not the POD alone.
Question 5 of 5A resident wants advice on a patient’s potassium result and is about to post the patient’s full laboratory sheet, with name and photograph, to a 300-member alumni Viber group. What is the safest choice?
Health information is sensitive personal information under the Data Privacy Act and must be processed for a legitimate purpose and in proportion to it. Removing only the photograph (the best distractor) still discloses the name and results to hundreds of people outside the care team.
