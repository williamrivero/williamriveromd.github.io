Study series for dialysis water treatment technicians

This module is study material for dialysis water treatment technicians. It explains the reasons behind safe practice; it does not replace your unit’s approved procedures, the manufacturer’s instructions for your equipment, or the limits your facility has adopted. Limits quoted here come from the international standard and are given so the numbers make sense; your facility’s written limits and action levels are the ones you work to. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Explain how biofilm forms and why disinfection is scheduled before counts rise.
  • Compare heat and chemical disinfection and prepare a correct dilution.
  • Carry out a chemical disinfection with proof of reach, dwell, and rinse.
  • Handle disinfectants safely and respond to a spill or exposure.

Start here

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsThink of the inner pot of a rice cooker. Washed after every meal, it comes clean in a minute with soap and water. Left for a week, the rice at the bottom dries into a crust that soaking and scrubbing barely move, and sometimes the pot is ruined.

The picture has a limit. You can see the crust in a pot. You cannot see biofilm in a loop; you learn about it only from culture results, weeks after it started. Disinfection is therefore not a cure for a contaminated system. It is routine housekeeping that keeps a clean system clean. Done on schedule, it is quick and uneventful. Done late, after biofilm has matured, it may not work at all, and parts of the system may have to be replaced.

Figure 1 · Key terms for this module

BiofilmBacteria attached to a surface in a protective slime.
DisinfectantA chemical or heat treatment that kills microbes.
DilutionMixing a strong stock chemical with water to the working strength.
Dwell timeHow long the disinfectant stays in the system.
ResidualDisinfectant remaining after the rinse.
Safety data sheetThe document listing a chemical’s hazards and first aid.
Opening case · fictional

Case: Chills on the Wednesday shift

It is Wednesday, a little after 08:00. On the morning shift, three patients develop chills and a mild fever about an hour into treatment: Mr. Arnel Ramos, 58, at station 2, Mrs. Teresita Lim, 66, at station 6, and Mr. Jojo Pascual, 49, at station 9. The nurse, Ms. Liza Fernandez, finds no sign of an access infection in any of them, and she calls you to the floor.

You check the file. The last monthly culture, two weeks ago, was 40 colony-forming units per milliliter at the end of the loop, up from 10 the month before. The loop disinfection due last week was postponed because the unit was busy.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What do these three patients share, and what does that point to?
  • Was the last culture result acceptable, and did it already call for action?
  • What do you do in the next hour, and what is not your decision?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Where bacteria live in a water system

Figure 2 · How biofilm forms

  1. AttachmentA few bacteria stick to a pipe wall or a joint.
  2. GrowthThey multiply and build a protective slime around themselves.
  3. MaturityThe layer now resists disinfectant and sheds cells and endotoxin.
  4. SpreadThe shed bacteria settle farther along the loop and start again.

Biofilm forms fastest where the water is warm, where it stands still, or where the surface is rough: storage tanks, dead legs, old flexible hoses, the lines that connect machines to the loop, and bicarbonate concentrate containers. Once the layer is mature it is very hard to remove, because the slime shields the bacteria underneath. The strategy is therefore prevention: continuous flow, sound design, and regular disinfection before counts rise.1

Lesson · part 2

Choosing and scheduling the method

HeatChemical
How it worksHot water is circulated through the loop for a set timeA disinfectant is circulated and then left to dwell
ResidueNoneIt must be rinsed out and proven absent
How often it can be doneFrequently, even daily or weekly, often automatically overnightLess often; each cycle takes the system out of use for hours
RequirementsPipework, pumps, and fittings rated for the temperatureCompatible materials; protective equipment; test strips
Typical agentsHot waterPeracetic acid products; bleach (sodium hypochlorite); others as the manufacturer approves
Watch out forScalds; sections that do not reach temperatureMembrane and metal damage from the wrong agent; residual; fumes

The manufacturer’s instructions state which method and which agents each part of the system tolerates. Bleach destroys many reverse osmosis membranes, so the membrane and the loop often have different procedures.2

Set the frequency from your unit’s own culture results: often enough that counts never approach the action level. Many units disinfect the loop monthly and adjust from there. Your culture results are the test of the schedule. If counts are rising between disinfections, either the interval is too long or something is seeding the loop.

Lesson · part 3

Working out a dilution

Chemical disinfectants arrive as a concentrated stock. You dilute the stock to the working strength stated in the procedure. Too weak, and the disinfection fails without any sign. Too strong, and you risk damage to the system and a longer rinse. The arithmetic below is the same every time.

Stock volume needed = (working strength × total volume) ÷ stock strength
Illustration: a procedure calls for 200 liters at 1 percent, and the stock is 5 percent. Stock needed = (1 × 200) ÷ 5 = 40 liters, made up to 200 liters with dialysis water. Use the strengths in your own procedure and on your own product label.
CheckWhy
The strength printed on the stock containerProducts with the same name come in different strengths
The expiry date and storageDisinfectants lose strength with age, heat, and light
The system volumeThe loop, tank, and hoses together; this figure should be written in the procedure
A test strip for concentrationIt confirms the working strength was reached at the farthest point
A separate test strip for residualA concentration strip is not sensitive enough to prove the rinse
RememberUse two different strips: a high-range strip to prove the disinfectant arrived at full strength, and a low-range strip to prove it has gone.
Lesson · part 4

Disinfecting the system

Figure 5 · Two ways to disinfect

HeatHot water is circulated through the loop for a set time. It leaves no chemical behind, so it can be done often. The system must be built to take the heat.
ChemicalA disinfectant such as peracetic acid or bleach is circulated and left to dwell. It must then be rinsed out completely, and a residual test must be negative.

Figure 6 · A chemical disinfection, in outline

  1. PlanNo patients on dialysis. Post warning signs. Wear the protective equipment listed on the safety data sheet.
  2. DoseAdd the amount the procedure states and circulate it.
  3. Prove it arrivedTest for the disinfectant at the farthest point.
  4. DwellLeave it for the stated time.
  5. RinseFlush until the residual test is negative at every outlet.
  6. Record and releaseWrite down concentrations, times, test results, and names.

Follow the manufacturer’s instructions for your system. The wrong chemical, strength, or temperature can destroy membranes and pipework. Never mix bleach with acid products, because the mixture releases chlorine gas. The lines that connect each machine to the loop, and the machines themselves, are disinfected as part of the same program. A clean loop that feeds a dirty connector hose still delivers contaminated fluid.

Stop and reportA positive residual test means disinfectant is still in the water. No patient is connected until every outlet tests negative and the result is recorded.
Lesson · part 5

Troubleshooting a disinfection

ProblemLikely causeAction
No disinfectant at the farthest pointA closed valve, an air lock, too small a dose, or a pump not runningDo not start the dwell clock; find and correct the cause; confirm at every point
Concentration low everywhereA dilution error, expired stock, or a system volume larger than assumedRecalculate; check the stock; add more by procedure and retest
Residual will not clear at one outletA dead leg or a long hose holding disinfectantFlush that outlet longer; report the dead leg
Residual clears, then reappearsDisinfectant trapped in a tank, a filter housing, or a branch is washing out slowlyKeep rinsing; drain and rinse the tank or housing; retest every outlet
Counts back up within two weeksMature biofilm, or a source such as a dead leg, a tank, old hoses, or the bicarbonate systemReport; investigate the source; a different agent, repeated cycles, or replacement of parts may be needed
Strong smell, coughing, sore eyesPoor ventilation or a spillLeave the area, ventilate, follow the safety data sheet, and report

Notice that the first row stops the clock. Dwell time counts only from the moment the disinfectant is proven at full strength at the farthest point. Time spent before that does not count, however long it was.

Lesson · part 6

Working safely with chemicals

Figure 9 · Rules for the chemical store

Read the safety data sheetDo it before you first handle any product.
Wear what it saysUsually gloves, eye protection, and an apron; sometimes a respirator.
Never mixBleach with acid, or any two products, unless the procedure says so.
Label everythingIncluding spray bottles and transfer containers.
VentilateKeep the room aired during disinfection.
Know the eyewashFind it before you need it.

Salt, citric acid, peracetic acid, and bleach are common in a water room. Each is safe when handled as directed and harmful when it is not. If you spill one, report it and clean it up by the written method. Do not hose it into the drain.

On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

Disinfectant did not reach the end

What you see
During a chemical disinfection on Sunday, the test strip at the farthest outlet, beside station 14, shows no disinfectant.
What is likely going on
A valve is closed, the dose was too small, or the disinfectant was not circulated long enough.
What to do
Do not start the dwell time. Find the cause, correct it, and confirm disinfectant at every point before the timing begins.
Why it matters
A disinfection that misses part of the loop leaves a seed for bacteria to grow back.

A chemical splash

What you see
During the Sunday disinfection, peracetic acid splashes onto a colleague’s forearm as he disconnects a hose.
What is likely going on
The chemical burns skin, and its vapor irritates the lungs.
What to do
Rinse with running water for the time stated on the safety data sheet, remove any contaminated clothing, and report the injury for medical assessment.
Why it matters
First aid in the first minute decides how bad the injury will be.

Residual at one outlet

What you see
After rinsing, every outlet tests negative except the one in the isolation room, which stays faintly positive.
What is likely going on
That branch is longer or has less flow, so disinfectant is still washing out of it.
What to do
Keep flushing that outlet and retest until it is negative. Do not release the system while any outlet is positive. Report the branch as a possible dead leg.
Why it matters
One missed outlet means one patient exposed to disinfectant.

Disinfection postponed again

What you see
The monthly disinfection has been put off twice, because the unit ran extra shifts.
What is likely going on
Counts are probably climbing, and biofilm is maturing.
What to do
Report the overdue status in writing and ask for a fixed slot. Bring the culture sampling forward.
Why it matters
A disinfection that waits for a quiet day never happens.
Field notes
  • Disinfect on schedule, before the counts rise.
  • Check the strength printed on the stock container every time.
  • Use the high-range strip to prove the disinfectant arrived, and the residual strip to prove it has gone.
  • Never mix bleach and acid products.
Case review

Back to the case: Chills on the Wednesday shift

What the three patients share. They share a shift, and they share the same water and dialysate supply. Reactions in several patients at the same time point to something common to all of them, not to three separate infections. Tell the charge nurse immediately that the water system may be involved and that a disinfection was missed.

Was the last culture acceptable? The number was, but the trend was not. The result of 40 colony-forming unit (CFU)/mL was under the limit of 100 and under a typical action level of 50. It had also quadrupled in a month. That rise, together with a postponed disinfection, called for action two weeks ago.

What to do now, and what is not yours. Follow the direction of the nurse and the medical director. Collect water and dialysate samples for culture and endotoxin before any disinfection, so that the evidence of what patients were exposed to is not destroyed. Then disinfect and document the rinse. Whether treatments continue, stop, or move is a clinical decision, not yours. Finally, report the postponed disinfection as a system problem to be reviewed, because a unit that is busy will always find a reason to postpone it again.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: A documented loop disinfection

Materials: the disinfection procedure, the approved disinfectant and its safety data sheet, protective equipment, concentration and residual test strips, warning signs, and the disinfection record. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. This checklist supports supervised learning; your unit’s own procedure and competency form are the authority. A printable version of every checklist is on the trainer page.

  • CriticalConfirms no patient is on dialysis, tells the charge nurse, and posts warning signs.
  • CriticalReads the safety data sheet and wears the stated protective equipment.
  • CriticalChecks stock strength and expiry and calculates the dilution correctly.
  • Doses and circulates by procedure.
  • CriticalProves working concentration at the farthest point before starting the dwell time.
  • Observes the full dwell time.
  • CriticalRinses and obtains a negative residual test at every outlet, including unused ones.
  • Records concentrations, times, results, and names, and releases the system in writing.
  • CriticalStates the first aid for skin and eye contact with the disinfectant in use.
Placement activity. Take part in one complete disinfection, then check the next month’s culture results and discuss with your preceptor whether the interval is right.
Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 5 questions at random from this module’s 8-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 4 of 5 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 8What is biofilm?
Biofilm sheds bacteria and endotoxin and resists disinfectant.
Question 2 of 8After a chemical disinfection, what must be shown before any patient is connected?
Residual disinfectant reaching a patient causes harm.
Question 3 of 8Why must bleach never be mixed with acid products?
Chlorine gas is toxic. Use and store these products separately.
Question 4 of 8Several patients on one shift develop chills at about the same time. What is the technician’s first step?
Report first. The clinical team decides about patients, and samples should be taken before disinfection.
Question 5 of 8A procedure calls for 100 liters at 2 percent. The stock is 10 percent. How much stock is needed?
(2 × 100) ÷ 10 = 20 liters, made up to 100 liters.
Question 6 of 8Why are two different test strips used during chemical disinfection?
High-range and residual strips answer different questions.
Question 7 of 8Counts return to their old level two weeks after disinfection. What does this suggest?
Rapid regrowth means something survived or keeps reseeding.
Question 8 of 8Why might the reverse osmosis membrane and the loop have different disinfection procedures?
Agent compatibility differs between components.
