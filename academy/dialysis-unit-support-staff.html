Nonclinical support pathway · orderlies and environmental service aides

This subtrack prepares orderlies, environmental service (EVS) aides, utility workers and contracted cleaners for nonclinical support work in an adult hemodialysis unit. It does not authorize nursing care, machine operation, needle or catheter work, medicines, dialyzer reprocessing, water-treatment work or technical release of a station. Your employer’s written task list, your supervisor and the nurse on duty decide what you may do. All people and cases are fictional.

Start here

A nonclinical track that works alongside nursing

This track is for the people who keep a hemodialysis unit clean, safe and moving: orderlies, environmental service (EVS) aides, utility workers, porters and contracted cleaners. It sits in the Ancillary Staff Track and links closely to the Nephrology Nursing course, because nurses direct the patient care that your work supports. That link does not make you a nurse or a nursing assistant, and nothing here authorizes nursing, machine or water-system work.

The 15 modules explain the reason behind every task before the task itself. Why does dried blood matter if you cannot see it? Why does a disinfectant need to stay wet? Why does a patient faint after dialysis, and why should you never lift a person alone? Each module opens with a plain picture, then explains what is really happening, then says how strong the evidence is, then shows exactly what you do and what you leave to others. Every module has a fictional case set in a Philippine unit, a debrief, a harder second round, a 15-question knowledge check with a reason for every answer, and a checklist to practice with your preceptor.

⚠️

What this track is, and is not

It is education and practice preparation. Finishing it does not prove competence, grant permission for any task, or carry Department of Health (DOH), Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth), Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) or Professional Regulation Commission continuing professional development (CPD) recognition, or basic life support certification. Your employer authorizes tasks after observed practice. Hours and pass marks are Academy proposals, not national minimums. Draft for clinical, infection prevention and regulatory review.

What the work looks like

A day in the San Isidro Dialysis Unit

Before the modules, here is what the job looks like. The day below is fictional, and so is everyone in it, but every moment happens in Philippine dialysis units every week. Each moment points to the module that prepares you for it. You will meet the same small team throughout: Ate Lorna, the EVS supervisor who has cleaned dialysis units for twenty years; Kuya Ramon, the senior orderly; and Nurse Jessa, the charge nurse of the San Isidro Dialysis Unit, an 18-station unit in a provincial hospital that runs three shifts a day.

5:40 a.m. · before the first shift

What happens
A new aide fills a spray bottle from a big jug of concentrate “by feel,” the way he did at his last job in a mall.
What you do
Read the label’s dilution, measure with the marked jug, label the bottle with the product and date, and check its wet contact time. Module 6.

10:05 a.m. · shift change

What happens
The next patient is waiting. The patient at Station 7 is still connected, with ten minutes left. Someone asks you to “start wiping the chair so we save time.”
What you do
Wait for clinical clearance and for the patient to leave. Then clean, keep the surfaces wet for the full contact time, and hand over the station as “environmental work complete.” Module 7.

11:30 a.m. · waste room

What happens
A yellow bag is heavy and lumpy at the bottom. Something pokes against the plastic.
What you do
Do not press, squeeze or reach in. Hold the bag by the neck away from your body, set it in a rigid bin, and report a sharp in the wrong container. Modules 9 and 10.

2:50 p.m. · after the session

What happens
Mang Celso, 74, has just finished dialysis. Three liters of fluid were removed. He stands up quickly to go home and sways.
What you do
Help him sit, keep him safe, call the nurse, and stay. Move him only by wheelchair and only when the nurse says he is ready. Modules 11 and 12.

4:15 p.m. · isolation station

What happens
You are cleaning the hepatitis B isolation station. The clean-supply cart from the main floor is parked just inside the door.
What you do
Use only the isolation station’s dedicated tools, and tell the nurse the cart must leave without anything being taken from it. Module 5.

7:45 p.m. · end of day

What happens
You missed the wall-mounted handrub dispenser at Station 3 during terminal cleaning. Nobody would ever know.
What you do
Go back, clean it, and record the correction truthfully. Honest records are what make the next audit useful. Module 15.

None of these moments asks you to make a clinical decision. Every one depends on you noticing, doing your own task exactly, and telling the right person in time. That is what this subtrack trains.

Two pathways

Environmental service aide or orderly

Environmental service aideAll 15 modules. Practice emphasis: hand hygiene and protective equipment, aseptic habits, product literacy, zone and station cleaning, spills, waste, linen, hazards and records. Patient movement is recognition, route preparation and calling for help. Station and machine-surface cleaning are conditional assignments.
OrderlyAll 15 modules and the same environmental foundations, plus observed escort and transfer practice for the duties in your job description, always under the nurse’s plan. Environmental tasks only if they are in your job and signed off.

Neither pathway includes needles, catheters, dressings, medicines, blood or bloodlines, vital signs or weighing for treatment decisions, machine controls or alarms, dialyzer reprocessing, concentrate preparation, or water-room work. Prior experience allows a diagnostic check, not exemption from critical observed skills. Contracted staff receive the same unit induction and sign-offs as directly hired staff. Module 1 has the full duty and scope matrix.

Before you begin

Entry and readiness

Before practical work, complete your employer’s induction, an occupational health review that fits your actual duties (including your hepatitis B vaccination record), and chemical, protective-equipment and manual-handling orientation. Covered workers also need the occupational safety and health seminar required by Philippine law; this track does not replace it. Recommended readiness: you can understand safety instructions and labels in English or Filipino with help, tell someone about an urgent problem, and take part in hands-on practice. Ask for reasonable accommodations if you need them.

Delivery. About 30 hours of guided learning and 28 hours of practice or simulation, 58 hours in all. At eight scheduled hours a day (excluding breaks) this fits about eight days, followed by observed practice at work until each assigned skill is demonstrated. Never shorten a wet contact time, a patient’s recovery or your own rest breaks to meet a timetable.

The modules

15 modules

Day 1Module 13 hS1 Your Role in the Dialysis Unit What a dialysis unit does, why its patients are unusually vulnerable to infection and injury, who decides what, and exactly where an orderly’s or environmental service aide’s work begins and ends. Day 1Module 23 hS2 Dignity, Privacy and Clear Communication Why dialysis patients are tired, slow and sometimes short-tempered, how to protect their privacy and dignity at a shared station, what you may and may not say, and how to report what you see with Stop, Tell, Stay. Day 2Module 33 hS3 How Germs and Blood Travel in a Dialysis Unit The chain of infection in plain words, why hepatitis B and C spread so easily in dialysis units, how long germs live on chairs and buttons, and the five steps by which hands carry them from one patient to the next. Day 2Module 44 hS3 Hand Hygiene and Protective Equipment Why hand hygiene is the single habit that protects patients most, the moments that apply to support work, how long to rub, why gloves are not clean hands, and how to choose, put on and take off protective equipment without contaminating yourself. Day 3Module 53 hS3, S4 Aseptic Habits: Clean to Dirty, and Never Back What aseptic habits mean for people who never do sterile procedures: clean to dirty and top to bottom, one cloth per area, never back into a clean bucket, keeping clean supplies clean, the clean-versus-dirty layout of the unit, and the extra rules of the hepatitis B isolation station. Day 3Module 64 hS4 Cleaning Products, Tools and Zones The difference between cleaning and disinfecting, how disinfectants actually kill germs, why wet contact time is not optional, how to read a label and a safety data sheet, how to dilute by measurement, which chemicals must never be mixed, and how color-coded tools keep zones apart. Day 4Module 75 hS1, S4 Safe Turnover of a Dialysis Station Turnover has a clinical phase and an environmental phase, and yours begins only after clinical clearance and the patient has left. The four station statuses, the surfaces and sequence, keeping surfaces wet, what a “clean” tag does and does not mean, and what the trials of better cleaning actually show. Day 4Module 84 hS4, S6 Routine and Terminal Cleaning, Restricted Areas and Linen The rhythm of cleaning beyond the station: between patients, daily, weekly and terminal; toilets and waiting areas where patients fall; the restricted technical rooms you enter only with permission; pests; and how to handle soiled and clean linen so it does not spread what it carries. Day 5Module 94 hS5 Blood Spills, Sharps and Exposure Response How to make a blood spill safe and clean it within your authorization, why you never pick up a loose sharp, how needlesticks happen to support staff, and the exact first minutes after an exposure, when washing, reporting and fast medical assessment matter most. Day 5Module 105 hS6 Health Care Waste: From Segregation to Storage Why waste is sorted where it is made and never re-sorted by hand, the Philippine waste categories and how to read your facility’s color chart, the waste a dialysis unit produces, filling, closing and labeling, moving waste on a safe route, central storage, and who may take it away. Day 6Module 114 hS2, S7 Patient Escort and Transport Why patients faint and fall after dialysis, explained from the blood vessels up; checking the wheelchair and the route; protecting the fistula arm and catheter during transport; escorting within the nurse’s plan; handing over to a named person; and moving isolation patients without spreading germs. Day 6Module 124 hS7, S8 Transfers, Falls and Protecting Your Back Why the back is injured by load, distance and twisting rather than by weakness; why lifting a whole adult by hand is never safe; slide sheets, transfer boards, gait belts and lifts; what to do when a patient is falling or has fallen; and a worked lifting calculation for the heavy boxes and bins of the unit. Day 7Module 133 hS1, S8 Supplies, Equipment and Technical Boundaries Receiving, storing and moving supplies so they stay sealed, dry, in date and clean; first in, first out; why you never open, sort or tidy clinical supplies; and the technical boundaries around machines, power, water and concentrates, with exactly what to report when something looks wrong. Day 7Module 144 hS5, S8 Emergencies and Worker Safety Your part in a patient emergency, a fire, a power failure, an earthquake or a flood, when every patient is attached to a machine and cannot simply walk out; and how to protect yourself from heat, fatigue, violence and chemical harm on an ordinary day. Day 8Module 155 hS1–S8 Honest Records, Improvement and the Capstone Why truthful records protect patients and you, how fluorescent-marker and hand hygiene audits work and what a percentage really means, how small improvement cycles fix problems without blame, and the six capstone stations that end the track.

When you have finished: take the end-of-course knowledge check, 15 questions, one from each module, drawn at random. The pass mark is 12 of 15.

Assessment

Two separate records

RecordRequirementRecorded by
Academy self-study completionAll modules read and the end-of-course knowledge check passed (12 of 15 or better).The learner, here (optionally saved in this browser only)
Employer-verified task competenceThe six capstone stations passed with no critical failure, and each authorized high-risk task observed as satisfactory on at least two separate occasions (more if performance varies).The employer, on signed printable forms in its own records

Critical failures are never averaged away. A failed action leads to explanation, demonstration, practice and reassessment with an equivalent scenario; until then the task stays under direct supervision or outside authorization. Repeated difficulty leads to a tailored plan, not automatic dismissal. This website cannot check identity or observe your hands, so nothing you do online records workplace competence.

For educators and preceptors

Localize before teaching

The lessons use generic workflows on purpose. Before teaching, gather your unit’s map, the approved task cards and safety data sheets, the product list with dilution and wet contact time, the waste color chart and route, the linen procedure, the exposure hotline and after-hours route, the emergency codes and exits, the transport aids actually available, and the written task list for each worker. Use them in every simulation. Use training props: a harmless spill substitute, mock sharps, empty sample containers and fluorescent marker gel. Never use real blood or real sharps for teaching.

Six capstone stationsHand hygiene and doffing · product card and station turnover · spill and exposure · waste and linen · escort and transfer (orderly) · emergency activation with handoff.
Stop, Tell, StayStop the task or keep the person safe · tell the nurse or supervisor what you saw, where, and when · stay until someone takes over.

RefresherAnnual focused review and observation, and immediate retraining after an incident, a product or process change, a long absence or a poor audit.
Tools

The Support Staff Toolkit

Built for this track. It has four tools: a label dilution and contact-time planner that uses only the numbers printed on your product’s label; a station turnover time budget that shows whether the gap between shifts leaves time for full wet contact; a safe lift check using the revised NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) lifting equation for boxes, jugs and bins; and an audit tally for fluorescent-marker cleaning checks and hand hygiene observations.
