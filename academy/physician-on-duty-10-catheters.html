Study series for physicians on duty

This module is study material for physicians on duty in hemodialysis units. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol. At the chairside, follow your unit’s protocol and ask the attending nephrologist for guidance. Doses and cut-off values are set by your unit. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Tell a tunneled catheter from a non-tunneled catheter, and explain why the difference matters.
  • Inspect the exit site, the tunnel, and the external limbs before a catheter is used.
  • Apply the steps of safe connection and disconnection, and explain what each prevents.
  • Work through poor catheter flow step by step.
  • Recognize catheter infection, air entry, bleeding, dislodgement, and an exposed cuff, and carry out the first actions.
  • Explain why every catheter needs a written plan for its removal.

Start here

The big picture, in plain words

In plain wordsThink of a dialysis catheter as a door cut through the outside wall of a house, opening straight into the main hallway. Deliveries can start the same day, but whatever comes through it reaches every room within seconds. Safety depends on how the door is opened, how it is closed, and how soon the wall can be repaired.

The picture has limits. A catheter is also a plastic tube lying inside a large vein, with its tip close to the heart. Blood clots on it, bacteria grow on it, and the vein around it scars. Those three processes explain most catheter problems.

Figure 2 · Key terms for this module

Central venous catheter (CVC)A tube placed in a large vein, with its tip in or near the heart. A dialysis CVC has two channels.
LumenOne channel inside the catheter. The red (arterial) lumen draws blood out. The blue (venous) lumen returns it.
Tunneled catheterA catheter that runs under the skin before it enters the vein. It has a cuff.
CuffA fabric band around a tunneled catheter. Tissue grows into it.
Exit siteThe place where the catheter comes out through the skin.
HubThe threaded end of each catheter limb, where the cap or the blood line attaches.
LockFluid left inside each lumen between sessions to keep it from clotting.
Catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI)Bacteria or fungi in the blood that came from the catheter.
Opening case · fictional

Case: The catheter that would only push

Mrs. Lourdes Villanueva, 54, dialyzes on Tuesdays and Fridays through a tunneled catheter in her right internal jugular vein. It was placed five months ago “until the fistula is ready.” No fistula surgery has been scheduled.

On Friday morning the nurse, Jun, calls you to her chair. Saline flushes easily into the red lumen, but he cannot draw blood back from it. The blue lumen works both ways. The chart shows that the lines were reversed for the last two sessions, with a lower pump speed and repeated pressure alarms.

Jun offers two ideas: push harder with a small syringe, or reverse the lines again. Mrs. Villanueva mentions that she felt cold and shaky near the end of Tuesday’s session and feverish that night. Her temperature now is 37.9°C. There is a small crust at the exit site.

Before reading on, decide:

  • What explains a lumen that accepts a flush but will not give blood back?
  • Which bedside steps are safe to try now, and which are not?
  • Which two other findings in this history must reach the attending nephrologist today?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Two kinds of catheter, and where the tip sits

A dialysis machine needs a large, steady blood flow. A catheter supplies it from a central vein close to the heart. A dialysis catheter has two lumens. By convention the red limb draws blood out to the machine and the blue limb returns it.1

The two kinds differ in what lies between the skin and the vein. A non-tunneled catheter passes straight through the skin into the vein. Skin bacteria have a short path along its outer surface, and only a stitch holds it. A tunneled catheter travels under the skin first, and a fabric cuff sits inside that tunnel. Over the weeks after insertion, tissue grows into the cuff. The cuff then anchors the catheter and acts as a barrier between the skin and the vein.1

FeatureNon-tunneled catheterTunneled, cuffed catheter
What you seeEnters at the neck or groin, held by a stitchExits on the upper chest; a small scar on the neck marks where it enters the vein
CuffNonePresent, hidden inside the tunnel
Intended useDays, for urgent startsWeeks to months, as a bridge or when no other access is possible

The Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guideline prefers tunneled catheters because the infection risk is lower. It considers it reasonable to limit a non-tunneled catheter to under two weeks, or to the period set by facility policy. Both statements are expert opinion, not trial results.2

The tip matters as much as the entry point. The tip of a tunneled catheter is meant to lie in or near the right atrium, where blood flow is high.1 Bacteria or air that enter the catheter therefore arrive at the heart at once. A catheter that has slipped outward may have its tip in a narrower vein, where it flows poorly.

RememberLearn three things about each catheter: which kind it is, when it was placed, and what the plan is for replacing it.
Lesson · part 2

Inspecting the exit site, the tunnel, and the limbs

A catheter is examined before every connection, not only when there is a complaint. KDOQI considers a physical check of the access before each use reasonable practice.2 An infection found before connection is a local problem. The same infection found after the pump has started may already have been flushed into the bloodstream.

The nurse removes the old dressing with clean technique. Then ask, look, and feel, in the same order each time.

Figure 5 · Catheter check before connection

  1. AskFever, chills, pain at the catheter, or a wet or loose dressing since the last session.
  2. Look at the exit siteRedness, swelling, crust, pus, or bleeding. On a non-tunneled catheter, check that the stitch is intact.
  3. Look for the cuffIt should not be visible. Compare the length of catheter outside the skin with the last recorded length.
  4. Feel the tunnelPress gently from the exit site toward the neck. Tenderness, warmth, or a firm cord is abnormal.
  5. Check the limbsBoth clamps closed, both caps on, no cracks, no kinks.
  6. Look at the patientSwelling of the face, neck, or arm on the catheter side suggests a narrowed central vein.

The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) separates two local infections by distance. An exit-site infection is redness, hardening, or tenderness within 2 centimeters of the exit site, with or without pus. A tunnel infection extends more than 2 centimeters from the exit site along the tunnel.3 A tunnel infection lies deeper than a topical treatment can reach, and closer to the vein.

A healed exit site is dry and painless. Pus is never normal.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist before the catheter is used when the cuff is visible, when the catheter looks longer than it was, or when the stitch of a non-tunneled catheter has come loose. Secure the catheter with tape and a sterile dressing, with both clamps closed and both caps on. Do not push it back in, and do not use it until the nephrologist has decided.

An exposed cuff means that the catheter has moved outward. The anchor and the barrier are lost, and the tip may have moved. The part that has been outside is no longer sterile, which is why it is never pushed back. Whether the catheter can be used is the nephrologist’s decision.

Lesson · part 3

Safe connection and disconnection, and what each step is for

During connection and disconnection the hub is open for a few seconds. An open hub is a direct path to the right atrium for bacteria and for air.

Bacteria. Organisms reach the blood by two routes. They can track along the outside of the catheter from the skin, which the tunnel and cuff are designed to slow. They can also enter the inside of the catheter through the hub, carried by hands, breath, or a contaminated cap. Once on the plastic, they can form a layer called a biofilm. A biofilm helps organisms withstand the body’s defenses and makes them less susceptible to antibiotics.4

Air. Pressure in the central veins is low. During a deep breath in it can fall below the pressure of room air, especially in a patient who is sitting up or who is volume depleted.5 If a hub and its clamp are both open, air can be drawn into the vein. A review of hemodialysis emergencies lists uncapped catheters and failure to clamp among the causes of air embolism.6

The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) publishes a hub protocol for dialysis catheters. The steps below follow its order.7 Your unit’s procedure sets the antiseptic and the details.

Figure 8 · Opening a catheter hub safely

  1. PrepareMasks, hand hygiene, and new clean gloves.
  2. Clamp firstConfirm that the clamp is closed before the cap comes off. This step keeps air out.
  3. ScrubScrub the threads and the end of the hub with antiseptic, using a new pad for each hub. This removes bacteria before they are pushed inside.
  4. Let it dryAntiseptic needs contact time. A scrubbed hub must not touch clothes, the chair, or the bed.
  5. Attach at onceConnect the sterile syringe or blood line. An uncapped catheter is never left unattended.
  6. Finish cleanRemove gloves and perform hand hygiene.

Disconnection follows the same logic: clamp, disconnect, scrub the hub, attach a new sterile cap, and confirm that the clamp is still closed.7 The lock placed in each lumen is part of the prescription.

What the evidence shows

In a cluster-randomized quality improvement study of 211 matched pairs of facilities, one group adopted chlorhexidine with alcohol for exit-site care and alcohol pads to scrub the hubs. That group had 0.81 positive blood cultures per 1,000 catheter-days, compared with 1.04 under usual care.8

In a CDC collaborative of 17 volunteer facilities, a package of catheter care, staff training, audits, and feedback was followed by a 54% fall in modeled access-related bloodstream infection rates.9 The project had no control group, so the fall cannot be credited to the package with certainty. Some elements rest on weaker ground. The CDC protocol notes that data supporting masks are lacking, though KDOQI considers them reasonable.7,2

RememberA dialysis catheter is not a line for routine blood draws, intravenous fluids, or medicines outside dialysis. Each extra opening is one more chance for bacteria and air to enter.
Lesson · part 4

Poor flow, step by step

KDOQI defines catheter dysfunction as failure to maintain the prescribed blood flow without lengthening the treatment.2 At the chair you see arterial pressure alarms, a reduced pump speed, or a lumen that will not give blood back.

Why a catheter pushes but will not pull

The two directions of flow are not equal. The venous lumen pushes blood out, and pushing tends to clear its opening. The arterial lumen draws blood in by suction, and suction pulls nearby material over the opening. That material may be the vein wall, a small clot, or a fibrin sheath. A fibrin sheath is a sleeve of protein and cells that grows along the outside of a catheter over weeks.1

Timing suggests the cause. Poor flow in the first days after insertion is usually mechanical: a kink, a tight stitch, or a tip in the wrong place. Poor flow after weeks of good function more often comes from clot or a fibrin sheath.1

Figure 11 · Poor catheter flow, in order

  1. Look at the patientCheck blood pressure, breathing, and temperature. Ask about chills. A failing catheter may also be infected.
  2. Look at the outsideClamps fully open, no kink under the dressing, no tight stitch, blood lines not twisted.
  3. Check the lengthIf the cuff shows or the catheter looks longer, stop and call.
  4. Change positionAsk the patient to cough, turn the head, or lie flatter. Flow that returns points to a tip resting against the vein wall.
  5. Flush per protocolA trained dialysis nurse may flush the lumen with saline, following the unit’s nursing procedure. Nobody forces a flush against resistance. Tell the attending nephrologist that it was needed.
  6. Lower the pump speed per protocolA slower speed needs less suction, but it also delivers less dialysis. Blood flow is part of the prescription, so the attending nephrologist is informed.
  7. Reverse the lines only if protocol allowsReversal is a nursing procedure and a temporary measure to finish today’s session. It increases recirculation, so the attending nephrologist is informed each time.
  8. Call and recordReport to the attending nephrologist the flow achieved, each step tried, and whether the lines were reversed. Write the same in the treatment record.

Forcing a flush is unsafe. For the same push of the thumb, a narrow syringe produces a higher pressure than a wide one. A review of catheter flushing, written about venous catheters other than dialysis catheters, states that a small syringe or a hard push against resistance increases the risk of damage to the catheter.10 Whatever is forced out of a blocked lumen also enters the bloodstream. Syringe size and flushing technique are set by your unit’s nursing procedure.

Reversing the lines often restores flow, because the blocked lumen now pushes. It has a cost. The intake now lies downstream of the return, so some cleaned blood is drawn straight back into the machine. This is called recirculation. In a prospective study of 102 patients with tunneled catheters, recirculation above 10% was found in 3% with the lines in the usual position and in 86% with the lines reversed. Among patients usually run reversed, about half could be run in the usual position. In them, measured clearance was no better with the lines reversed, despite a higher pump speed.11 This was one center, and clearance was measured in a subgroup. KDOQI describes reversal as a temporary respite that allows a session to be completed.2 Reversal does not treat the cause, and repeated reversal is a reason to call.

KDOQI considers a conservative bedside approach reasonable as the first step. For clot, it conditionally recommends a clot-dissolving (thrombolytic) medicine placed inside each lumen.2 In a randomized trial of 225 patients with new catheters, a weekly thrombolytic lock reduced catheter malfunction and bacteremia compared with heparin locks alone.12 Thrombolytic locks, imaging, and catheter exchange are ordered by the nephrologist per protocol. KDOQI regards replacement at a new site as the last resort.2

Stop and callCall the attending nephrologist when a catheter cannot deliver the prescribed blood flow, when the lines had to be reversed, or when it has flowed poorly at more than one session. A slowed or shortened session delivers less dialysis, and the response belongs to the prescription.
Lesson · part 5

Recognizing catheter infection and taking the first steps

A catheter gives bacteria a surface to live on and a route to the blood. In a systematic review of 62 cohort studies with 586,337 participants, patients using catheters had a higher risk of fatal infection than patients using fistulas (risk ratio 2.12).13 The studies were observational, and the authors judged the risk of selection bias to be high, since sicker patients are more likely to have catheters.

TypeWhat you findWhy it matters
Exit-site infectionRedness, tenderness, crust, or pus close to the exit site. The patient may feel wellLocal, but it can spread along the catheter
Tunnel infectionTenderness, redness, or hardening along the tunnelDeep to the skin and next to the vein
Bloodstream infectionFever or chills, often during dialysis. Low blood pressure or confusion. The exit site may look normalCan seed the heart valves, spine, and joints

A normal exit site does not rule out a bloodstream infection, because the bacteria may live inside the lumen. Chills that begin after dialysis starts are typical, because the pump washes organisms from the catheter into the circulation. Elderly and diabetic patients may have no fever, only low blood pressure or confusion.

Stop and callFever or chills in a patient with a catheter is a catheter-related bloodstream infection until proven otherwise. Examine the patient, start your unit’s protocol, and call the attending nephrologist at once. Do not send the patient home to “observe.”

Figure 13 · First steps for suspected catheter infection

  1. AssessTemperature, blood pressure, heart rate, mental state, exit site, and tunnel. Look for another source of infection.
  2. Call earlyUse the structured report from Module 1. Say plainly that the patient has a catheter.
  3. Culture firstDraw blood cultures per protocol before the first antibiotic. Swab any pus.
  4. Protect the veinsFor a peripheral sample, use a hand vein, not a forearm vein that a future fistula will need.
  5. Give antibiotics as orderedThe choice comes from the nephrologist and the unit protocol.
  6. Decide where the patient goesAn unstable patient needs the hospital.

The order comes from guidelines. IDSA gives its strongest grade to obtaining blood cultures before antibiotics. It advises taking peripheral samples from vessels that a fistula will not need, such as hand veins.3 KDOQI also considers cultures before antibiotics reasonable.2

What happens to the catheter is the nephrologist’s decision. Options include removal, exchange over a wire, and an antibiotic lock. For certain organisms the catheter is always removed, as the nephrologist and infectious disease guidance direct.3

Lesson · part 6

Why every catheter needs an exit plan

In the systematic review above, patients using catheters also had a higher risk of death from any cause than patients using fistulas (risk ratio 1.53).13 These are associations, and part of the difference reflects who receives a catheter.

Three harms accumulate with time. Each week adds exposure to infection. Clot and fibrin reduce flow, which reduces the dialysis delivered. And the catheter lies against the wall of the central vein, which can scar and narrow over time. Catheters in the neck and chest veins are a leading cause of this narrowing, called central vein stenosis, particularly after long use or several catheters. It can cause swelling of the arm, face, or neck, and it threatens a fistula or graft on that side.14,2

KDOQI therefore suggests a fistula or graft over a catheter for most patients, and suggests that most patients who start with a catheter convert when possible. It grades the supporting evidence as very low to moderate.2 It accepts a tunneled catheter while a fistula matures, and as long-term access for selected patients with no better option. It asks that each patient have a documented, regularly reviewed access plan.2 The CDC lists catheter reduction among its core practices for preventing bloodstream infection.15

Figure 15 · From catheter to fistula or graft

  1. Urgent startA catheter is placed because dialysis cannot wait.
  2. ReferThe nephrologist arranges vein mapping and a surgical opinion.
  3. CreateThe surgeon creates a fistula or places a graft.
  4. MatureThe fistula develops over weeks. The catheter stays in use and is cared for at every session.
  5. CannulateThe team begins using the new access.
  6. RemoveThe catheter comes out once the new access works reliably.

You do not write this plan, but you can notice when it has stalled. Write the insertion date and the plan in your note, and tell the attending nephrologist about any catheter with no plan.

RememberKeep needles and blood pressure cuffs away from the arm chosen for a fistula. Module 9 explained why those veins must be saved.
Lesson · part 7

Catheter emergencies and the first actions

Catheter emergencies are uncommon. Each has a first action that does not wait for an order. Start it per your unit’s protocol while a colleague calls the attending nephrologist.

EmergencyWhat you seeFirst actions
Air entryAn open, unclamped hub or a disconnected line. Sudden cough, breathlessness, chest pain, low blood pressure, confusion, or collapseClamp the catheter. Stop the blood pump. Do not return the blood. Give oxygen. Position the patient per protocol
Bleeding from a limb or hubBlood running from a missing cap, a loose connection, or a cracked limbClamp between the leak and the patient. Replace the cap with sterile technique
Bleeding around the catheterBlood soaking the dressing at the exit siteApply firm, direct pressure with sterile gauze. Check blood pressure
Catheter pulled partly outCuff visible, or a longer length outside the skinDo not push it back. Do not use it. Secure it with tape and a sterile dressing
Catheter pulled completely outCatheter in the patient’s hand or on the floorLay the patient flat. Press firmly over the exit site and over the point where the catheter entered the vein. Cover with an airtight dressing. Call for help

Air

Air that enters a central vein travels to the right side of the heart and the lung arteries. There it can block blood flow and cause cardiac arrest. In a patient who is sitting up, air can also rise through the jugular vein toward the brain. A published review cites 100 to 300 milliliters as a volume that may be fatal in an adult.6

The same review advises stopping dialysis, not returning the blood in the circuit, giving oxygen, and supporting the circulation. For suspected venous air embolism it recommends lying the patient on the left side. It warns that a head-down position may worsen brain swelling when air has reached the brain.6 Positioning advice is not uniform across sources. It rests on physiological reasoning and case experience, not on trials. Follow your unit’s protocol.

Bleeding and dislodgement

The review lists uncapped ports, disconnection from the circuit, and accidental removal as catheter-related causes of serious bleeding, and it advises continuous direct pressure.6 After a tunneled catheter comes out, the hole in the vein lies at the base of the neck, several centimeters from the exit site. Press on both places. The patient lies flat and the dressing is airtight because air can enter through the empty track.5

Bleeding around the exit site is most common soon after insertion and after heparin. Heparin is part of the prescription, so report the bleeding before the next dose.

Stop and callStop and call for bleeding around a catheter that continues despite firm pressure, for any bleeding with a fall in blood pressure, and for any suspected air entry. Start the first actions while a colleague makes the call.

Figure 16 · Call card: catheter emergency

  1. RecognizeAn open or disconnected hub, blood on the dressing or the floor, a visible cuff, a catheter out of place, or fever and chills.
  2. First 2 minutesClamp the catheter and stop the blood pump. Press on any bleeding point. Give oxygen if the patient is breathless or collapsed. Check airway, breathing, pulse, and blood pressure.
  3. Stop dialysis?Yes for air entry, dislodgement, uncontrolled bleeding, or an unstable patient. For poor flow or suspected infection in a stable patient, follow protocol and the nephrologist’s advice.
  4. Return the blood?Do NOT return blood if air may be in the circuit or may have entered the patient, if the circuit is clotted, or if the catheter is damaged, pulled out, or showing its cuff. Otherwise return it per protocol and as the attending nephrologist advises.
  5. CallThe attending nephrologist. Activate the emergency response for collapse or suspected air embolism.
  6. WriteTime, findings, catheter length outside the skin, estimated blood loss, actions, the patient’s response, and who was informed.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The wet dressing

What you see
A patient with a tunneled chest catheter arrives with a damp, half-peeled dressing. He says he bathed that morning and the plastic cover slipped. He feels well.
What is likely going on
The exit site has been wet and uncovered for hours. Moist skin under a loose dressing lets bacteria multiply next to the catheter.
What to do
Have the nurse remove the dressing with clean technique, both wearing masks. Inspect the exit site and feel the tunnel before connection. If both are normal, the nurse cleans and redresses the site per protocol. Report any redness, pus, or tenderness to the attending nephrologist before the catheter is used. Review with the patient how he keeps the dressing dry.
Why it matters
KDOQI considers it reasonable to protect catheter dressings from wet and dirty conditions. A problem found before connection is still local.

The borrowed line

What you see
An admitted dialysis patient is wheeled in with intravenous fluid running through the blue limb of her dialysis catheter. The ward could not find a peripheral vein.
What is likely going on
The catheter has been opened by staff who are not trained in hub technique. The lock has been flushed into the patient, and the hub may be contaminated.
What to do
Have the dialysis nurse stop the infusion, clamp the limb, and handle the hub per unit protocol. Check the patient for fever and for signs of fluid overload. Tell the attending nephrologist, and ask the ward team to arrange other venous access through the nephrologist.
Why it matters
Every opening of a hub is a chance for bacteria and air to enter. Module 2 explained why unplanned intravenous fluid is a hazard in a patient who makes no urine.

Alarms after turning to sleep

What you see
Two hours into a session, the arterial pressure alarm sounds repeatedly on a patient with a neck catheter. She has turned on her side and fallen asleep. The catheter worked well until now.
What is likely going on
The tip is probably resting against the vein wall, or a limb is kinked under her shoulder. Flow that changes with position is usually mechanical.
What to do
Look at the patient first, then at the limbs and blood lines for a kink. Wake her gently and help her change position. If flow returns, record it. If the alarms continue in every position, follow your unit’s poor-flow protocol and call the attending nephrologist.
Why it matters
Most positional problems resolve without any intervention on the catheter. Silencing the alarm without finding the cause delivers less dialysis.

The catheter in the patient’s hand

What you see
A confused elderly man with a non-tunneled femoral catheter stands up from his chair before connection. The catheter catches on the armrest and comes out completely. Blood runs down his thigh.
What is likely going on
The stitch has torn through, and the femoral vein is open to the skin.
What to do
Lay him flat. Press firmly and continuously over the groin where the catheter entered the vein. Call for help, check his blood pressure, and have a colleague call the attending nephrologist. Keep the catheter so that its tip can be checked for completeness. Do not attempt to reinsert anything.
Why it matters
Direct pressure controls most venous bleeding. A patient without access also needs an urgent plan for the next dialysis session.

The cap on the floor

What you see
During disconnection a new nurse drops a sterile cap. To pick it up she lets go of the catheter limb. The hub is open, and you see that the clamp is not closed. The patient is sitting upright and talking.
What is likely going on
For those seconds the central vein was open to room air and to the nurse’s gloves.
What to do
Close the clamp at once. Check the patient for cough, breathlessness, chest pain, or a change in alertness, and measure blood pressure and oxygen saturation. If there is any symptom, treat it as air entry: do not return blood, give oxygen, follow the unit protocol, and call for help. If she is well, the nurse scrubs the hub and applies a new sterile cap. Report the event to the attending nephrologist and the charge nurse as a near miss.
Why it matters
The clamp protects against air, and the scrub protects against bacteria. Reporting a near miss, without blame, is how a unit corrects technique before a patient is harmed.
Field notes
  • Read the insertion date before you look at the catheter. A non-tunneled catheter that has stayed for weeks is a finding even when it looks clean.
  • Count clamps and caps with your eyes every time you pass a catheter: two clamps closed, two caps on.
  • When a nurse says the catheter is “positional,” ask for how many sessions. A catheter that needs a special position every time is failing and should be reported.
  • Ask every patient with a catheter how they bathe and how they keep the dressing dry. The answer tells you how much they have been taught.
  • Write the access plan in every note. If you cannot find one, that sentence is the most useful line you will write that day.
Case review

Back to the case: The catheter that would only push

What explains a lumen that accepts a flush but will not give blood back? Something covers the opening of the red lumen only when suction is applied. Pushing saline lifts it away, and pulling draws it back. After five months of use, a fibrin sheath or a small clot at the tip is most likely. A tip resting against the vein wall is the other possibility.

Which bedside steps are safe, and which are not? The safe steps are the ordered ones. Examine Mrs. Villanueva first. Check the clamps, the limbs, and the length of catheter outside the skin. Ask her to cough and change position. A saline flush by the nurse is allowed if your unit’s nursing procedure describes it. Pushing hard with a small syringe is not safe, because the pressure can damage the catheter and force material into her circulation. Reversing the lines a third time without telling the attending nephrologist is also wrong. Reversal increases recirculation, and her pump speed was lowered, so she has probably received less dialysis than prescribed for a week, on a twice-weekly schedule with little margin.

Which two other findings must reach the nephrologist today? The first is the chills on Tuesday, the fever that night, and today’s temperature. Chills on dialysis in a patient with a catheter suggest a bloodstream infection until proven otherwise. Blood cultures come before any antibiotic, per protocol, and the crusted exit site needs a careful look. The second is that this catheter has no exit plan. It was placed as a bridge, and five months later no surgery is booked.

One call covers all three. Whether she dialyzes today, and how, is the nephrologist’s decision.

Skills lab · with your preceptor

Skills checklist: Exit-site inspection and hub handling

Your preceptor observes you examine three catheters before connection, and observes you supervise one connection and one disconnection performed by a dialysis nurse. Your preceptor rates each step as satisfactory, needs practice, or not observed. Steps marked Critical must all be satisfactory before sign-off. Question 3 of 15Why is a catheter examined before every connection?
Once the pump starts, organisms at the exit site or in the lumen can be flushed into the blood.
Question 4 of 15Before connection, a ring of white fabric is visible at the exit site of a tunneled catheter. What comes next?
A visible cuff means the catheter has moved outward. The exposed part is not sterile and the tip may be out of position.
Question 5 of 15Which step, done before a cap is removed, protects most directly against air entry?
Central venous pressure can fall below room pressure during a breath in. Scrubbing and masks protect against bacteria.
Question 6 of 15A ward nurse asks to run an overnight antibiotic through a patient’s dialysis catheter because the veins are difficult. What is the best answer?
Each extra opening of the hub adds risk of infection and air entry.
Question 7 of 15What did the cluster-randomized study of catheter care compare?
Intervention facilities had fewer positive blood cultures per 1,000 catheter-days.
Question 8 of 15Why can a lumen accept a saline flush and still fail to give blood back?
Pushing lifts the obstruction away and pulling draws it back, like a flap.
Question 9 of 15A tunneled catheter placed three days ago flows poorly from the first session. Which cause is most likely?
Early poor flow is usually mechanical. Clot and fibrin sheath are typical after weeks of good function.
Question 10 of 15A lumen resists a saline flush. Why should nobody push harder with a small syringe?
For the same push, a small syringe generates a higher pressure. Stop and call the attending nephrologist.
Question 11 of 15In the study of 102 patients with tunneled catheters, what happened when the blood lines were reversed?
Recirculation above 10% was found in 86% with lines reversed and 3% in the usual position.
Question 12 of 15Forty minutes into dialysis, a 67-year-old man with a tunneled catheter has shaking chills. His exit site is clean. How do you interpret this?
Bacteria inside the lumen are washed into the blood when the pump runs. The exit site may look normal.
Question 13 of 15For the same patient, what should happen before the first antibiotic?
Guidelines give priority to cultures before antibiotics. Forearm veins are saved for a future fistula.
Question 14 of 15At disconnection a hub is found open and unclamped. The seated patient coughs, becomes breathless, and her blood pressure falls. What do you do first?
This is suspected air entry. Returning blood or flushing could push more air into the patient.
Question 15 of 15A patient eight months into catheter dialysis says a fistula operation is unnecessary because the catheter works. What is the most useful response?
Catheter use is associated with more infection and death than fistula use. The access plan belongs to the nephrologist.

Terms in plain words

Central venous catheter
A tube placed in a large vein with its tip in or near the heart.
Tunneled catheter
A catheter that runs under the skin before entering the vein and carries a cuff.
Cuff
A fabric band on a tunneled catheter that tissue grows into, anchoring the catheter.
Exit site
The place where the catheter comes out through the skin.
Hub
The threaded end of a catheter limb where the cap or blood line attaches.
Lock
Fluid left in each catheter lumen between sessions to prevent clotting.
Fibrin sheath
A sleeve of protein and cells that grows along the outside of a catheter and can block its openings.
Recirculation
Cleaned blood drawn straight back into the machine instead of mixing with the patient’s circulation.
Catheter-related bloodstream infection
Bacteria or fungi in the blood that came from the catheter.
Air embolism
Air in the bloodstream that blocks blood flow in the heart, lungs, or brain.
