Quality, Equity, and Special Populations

How to turn a recurring failure, such as abnormal results that sit unread over a weekend, into a measurable improvement project, and how to adapt the standard transplant workflow for adolescents, frail older adults, women planning pregnancy, and patients for whom distance, cost or language is the real barrier.

Specialty subtrack for registered nurses

This subtrack prepares Philippine registered nurses for supervised kidney transplant ward and clinic work. It teaches assessment, communication and escalation within nursing scope. Candidacy, listing, organ allocation, compatibility decisions, dose changes and benefit approval belong to the authorized transplant team, the histocompatibility laboratory, the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) and the facility. All cases are synthetic.

Learning objectives · about 2 hours · week 6

After this module you should be able to

  • Define a closed-loop process measure with a clear numerator, denominator, exclusions, data source and owner.
  • Plan a single Plan-Do-Study-Act test of change with a balancing measure.
  • Identify inequities in transplant access and adapt workflows for distance, cost, language and literacy.
  • Adapt care for adolescents, frail older adults, and women planning pregnancy after transplant or donation.
  • Protect privacy in quality reporting.
Intuition

Every system is perfectly designed for the results it gets

If abnormal results regularly sit unread from Friday evening to Monday morning, that is not a series of individual lapses. It is what the system was built to produce: a single reviewer, no backup, notifications that go to an empty inbox. Blaming the nurse who was off duty will not change Monday's pile. Changing the system will.

Quality improvement is the discipline of making that change visible and testable. You define what "good" looks like as a number, measure where you are, try one change on a small scale, measure again, and check that you have not broken something else.

The picture has a limit. No one designs failure on purpose, and people still make choices inside a system. But in transplant care the failures that hurt patients are rarely one person's slip. A creatinine that rises on Friday and is first read on Monday has given a rejection or a drug toxicity three days to injure the graft, whoever was on duty. Treating failure as a property of the system, rather than of a person, is what makes it fixable.

Method

A measure you can trust

Take the closed loop on abnormal results (Modules 7 to 9). A useful measure is:

Percentage of abnormal transplant results reviewed and dispositioned within the local interval
Numerator: results meeting the prespecified abnormal threshold that have a documented clinician review and disposition within the interval (for example, 24 hours).
Denominator: all results meeting the same prespecified threshold, in the defined population and period.
Exclusions: defined in advance (for example, results drawn during an inpatient stay, where a different process applies).

Three details make the measure trustworthy. The threshold must be prespecified (which creatinine rise, which tacrolimus range, which viral load), or the denominator will drift. Notification is not acknowledgment: a message sent to an inbox does not count as review. And the data source and the person who extracts it must be named, so the measure can be repeated the same way every month.

Know what the measure stands for. Review time is a process measure. It stands in for the outcome you actually care about, fewer grafts injured because a result waited, which is too rare to count month by month in one clinic. Like a tacrolimus trough, it is a surrogate: useful because it plausibly predicts the outcome, but not proof that patients did better.

At a partner clinic's quality meeting · fictional

The first audit reports that 98 percent of abnormal results were "handled within 24 hours." Carla asks how "handled" was counted. It turns out that any result automatically sent to a physician's inbox was counted as handled.

You recount October using the agreed definition: a documented review and a disposition. The figure falls to 71 percent. Nobody likes the new number, but now the team knows where it actually stands.

What this shows. A measure is only as honest as its definitions. Notification is not review.

The quality assessment and performance improvement (QAPI) and continuous quality improvement (CQI) toolkit helps with run charts and aim statements.

Method

One small test at a time

  1. PlanState the aim ("95 percent of abnormal results dispositioned within 24 hours by March"), the change (a named weekend backup reviewer with a callback script), the prediction, and what you will measure.
  2. DoTest on a small scale: one weekend, one clinic, synthetic cases first if the process is new.
  3. StudyCompare the result with the prediction, using the same measure; look at failures one by one.
  4. ActAdopt, adapt or abandon the change; plan the next cycle.

Balancing measures. Every change has a cost somewhere. A weekend backup reviewer may improve review times while increasing weekend workload or callback volume, or delaying other tasks. Track at least one balancing measure so that a gain in one place is not quietly paid for in another.

At the same partner clinic, six weeks later · fictional

For one month, a named weekend backup reviewer gets a callback script and the laboratory's after-hours number. Abnormal results reviewed and dispositioned within 24 hours rise from 71 to 93 percent.

Your balancing measure tells the rest of the story. Weekend calls to the on-call physician have doubled, and two of them were about results that were not urgent. For the next cycle, the team tightens the list of values that trigger a weekend call.

What this shows. Small tests show both the gain and its cost. The next cycle fixes the cost instead of abandoning the gain.

Privacy in quality work. Audit lists and run charts used in meetings, posters or presentations must not carry identifiable information. Use counts, codes or synthetic examples.1

Patient safety

Learning from errors without blaming people

Transplant care runs on many handoffs, high-risk drugs and time-critical tests. Errors will happen. A unit that hides them repeats them. A unit that reports and studies them can make the same error harder to repeat. The quality methods in this module work only in a culture where people feel safe to say what went wrong.

Report near misses as well as harm. A mislabeled crossmatch sample caught at the laboratory, a tacrolimus dose almost given at the wrong strength, or an offer call almost made to the wrong patient all caused no harm. Each one shows a weak point in the system before it hurts someone. Report them through your facility's incident system, with facts rather than blame: what happened, when, what was caught, and how.

Just culture. A just culture separates three kinds of behavior. An honest slip in a poorly designed process calls for fixing the process and supporting the person. Risky behavior, such as a shortcut that has become normal on the ward, calls for coaching and for understanding why the shortcut felt necessary. Reckless disregard of a clear safety rule is rare and calls for accountability. Most transplant errors are of the first two kinds.

Question in a reviewWeak answerUseful answer
What happened?"The nurse gave the wrong tacrolimus.""The extended-release capsules were dispensed in place of the immediate-release ones and given twice daily for two days."
Why did it happen?"She was careless.""The two products are stored side by side, look alike, and the order did not name the formulation."
What will prevent it?"Remind staff to be careful.""Separate storage, formulation written on every order, and a second check at dispensing, tested with a Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle."

Notice that "be more careful" never fixes a system. Good fixes change the design: they make the right action easier and the wrong action harder, such as forcing functions, standard order sets, checklists at handoffs and read-back. These are the changes worth testing with the PDSA method above.

At the unit huddle · fictional

A new nurse admits that she nearly sent a crossmatch sample labeled with the patient's ward bed number instead of his hospital number. She caught it herself at the laboratory window. She is embarrassed and expects to be scolded.

Nurse Carla thanks her in front of the team, files a near-miss report with her, and asks the group why it nearly happened. Two other nurses admit they have done the same during night admissions, because the pre-printed labels are not available after 10 p.m. The unit arranges for labels to be printed at the bedside at any hour.

What this shows. One near miss, reported safely, revealed a problem three nurses shared. Thanking the reporter is what makes the next report possible.

Ethics

Who gets referred, and who gets lost

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) candidate guideline states that access to transplant evaluation should not depend on socioeconomic status, sex or ethnicity.2 In practice, patients are lost at every station for reasons unrelated to their medical suitability: they live far from a center, cannot afford repeated travel, cannot leave work, are not literate in the language of the forms, have no reliable phone, or are women whose own needs are placed after the family's.

DistanceBundle visits; arrange local tests that the program accepts; plan lodging; use teleconsultation where allowed.
CostRefer early to benefits and social work; map the post-transplant package; plan medicine supply before discharge.
Language and literacyQualified interpreters, plain-language materials, pictures, teach-back, materials in Filipino and regional languages.
ConnectivityAgree contact windows, text-first approaches and backup contacts, documented with consent.
Disability and sensory lossLarge-print schedules, pill organizers, caregiver involvement with the patient's consent.

Measure equity, too. Referral rates by sex, distance from the center or payer can reveal where the pathway is leaking.

At the quality meeting · fictional

Your clinic's referral log for the past year shows that men with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 20 were referred for transplant evaluation at about twice the rate of women with the same kidney function. No one had noticed, because each decision looked reasonable on its own.

When you review ten of the women's charts, the same notes recur: "will discuss with husband," "takes care of grandchildren," "cannot leave the house." The team adds a standard question to every visit with an eGFR below 20, asked to the patient directly, about whether she wants a referral.

What this shows. Inequity hides inside individually sensible decisions. Counting by group makes it visible.

Measurement

Measuring what patients feel

Most transplant measures are things the program counts: creatinine, levels, rejection rates, days to evaluation. They matter. They are not the whole story. A recipient with a perfect creatinine may be too tired to work, frightened of every fever, or spending a third of his income on medicines. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) ask the patient directly about symptoms, function and quality of life, using structured, validated questionnaires.

Surrogate or patient-important? The medical teaching standard used throughout this course separates the two. A tacrolimus level in range is a surrogate: it is useful because it tends to predict fewer rejections and less toxicity. Being able to return to work, sleeping through the night, or feeling confident to travel are outcomes the patient feels directly. A good quality program tracks some of each and does not assume that improving the first automatically improves the second.

Starting small. You do not need a large program to begin. A clinic can add one short, validated questionnaire at a fixed visit, such as the six-month review, and look at the results together each quarter. Ask the patient's permission, protect the data as health information,1 and make sure someone acts on worrying answers, such as severe fatigue, low mood or financial distress, the same day. A questionnaire nobody reads is worse than none, because it tells the patient his answers do not matter.

In the transplant clinic · fictional

Your clinic starts a short quality-of-life questionnaire at the six-month visit. In the first quarter, most recipients score well. But six of the twenty report that worry about the cost of medicines is affecting their sleep and their families.

The team invites the social worker and the benefits officer to the six-month clinic, so that cost concerns are addressed at the same visit. The next quarter, the number reporting serious financial worry falls to three.

What this shows. Asking patients directly found a problem no laboratory value would show. Measure what patients feel, and make sure someone acts on it.

Practice

Adolescents, frail older adults and pregnancy

Adolescents and young adults. Nonadherence peaks in these years, and early transfer to adult care is associated with higher graft failure.3 Assess self-management skills, the caregiver's role, confidentiality and consent rules for minors, school and work schedules, and readiness for adult services. Hand over with a joint visit or a named contact, not just a letter.

Frail older adults. Frailty measured at transplant is associated with death independent of age. In one prospective cohort of 537 recipients, five-year survival was 91.5 percent in nonfrail, 86.0 percent in intermediately frail and 77.5 percent in frail recipients, and frailty carried about 2.2 times the risk of death after adjustment.4 This is observational and single-center; it identifies risk, not a proven fix. Assess mobility and falls, vision and hearing, cognition, medication complexity and caregiver strain. Simplify schedules with the team, use pill organizers, and plan early mobilization and rehabilitation. The Frailty Assessment calculator supports a structured screen.

Pregnancy after transplant. Fertility often returns within months of a successful transplant, sometimes before a patient expects it. A meta-analysis of 6,712 pregnancies in 4,174 kidney recipients, drawn from 87 mostly observational studies, found a live-birth rate of 72.9 percent, with high rates of preeclampsia (21.5 percent), cesarean delivery (62.6 percent) and preterm delivery (43.1 percent).5 The 2009 KDIGO recipient guideline suggests waiting at least a year after transplant and attempting pregnancy only when kidney function is stable with proteinuria below 1 g/day (2C). It recommends stopping mycophenolate or replacing it with azathioprine before pregnancy is attempted (1A), one of its strongest grades, because mycophenolate causes birth defects.6 That switch happens before conception, under the team's direction (Module 6). Ask about pregnancy plans and contraception at every review, and route a possible pregnancy to the team the same day.

By phone · fictional

Jessa, 27, had her transplant ten months ago. She calls to say her period is three weeks late and a home test is positive. She is still taking mycophenolate.

You do not tell her to stop the tablet on her own, and you do not leave it for her next visit. You tell her the transplant team needs to speak with her today, call the transplant nephrologist at once, and stay on the line until a same-day appointment is booked. You document the call, the plan and her read-back.

What this shows. A possible pregnancy on mycophenolate is a same-day call to the team. The regimen change is theirs to direct.

Pregnancy after donation. Women who have donated a kidney have a higher risk of gestational hypertension or preeclampsia than matched nondonors. In a retrospective Ontario cohort, it occurred in 15 of 131 donor pregnancies (11 percent) versus 38 of 788 nondonor pregnancies (5 percent), with no difference in preterm birth or low birth weight. They need antenatal care that knows their donor history.7

Practice case · synthetic

Case: Abnormal results waiting for Monday

The audit. Your transplant clinic asked you to review three months of results. Over that period, 46 tacrolimus levels and creatinine results met the clinic's abnormal thresholds after 5 p.m. on Fridays. Only 19 had a documented review and disposition before Monday noon.

In two cases, a high creatinine was not acted on until Tuesday. One of those patients was admitted with acute rejection the following week. Nobody was negligent: the single reviewing nephrologist is off on weekends, and notifications go to a shared inbox that nobody checks on Saturdays.

At the staff meeting, someone suggests a memo reminding everyone to "be more careful with results."

Before reading on, decide what you would assess, whom you would contact and what you would say, what you would teach, and what would make you escalate. Then compare with the debrief below.

Assess

Baseline: 19 of 46 (41 percent) reviewed within the interval. Map the process: who receives results, how notification happens, where it fails (no backup, inbox not monitored).

Communicate

Present the data to the clinic team without naming individuals. Agree an aim and propose one change: a named weekend backup reviewer with a callback script and documented acknowledgment.

Teach

Explain the difference between notification and acknowledgment, and how the measure will be calculated each month.

Escalate

Report the two delayed high-creatinine cases through the facility's safety reporting system, as system events, for review.
How it played out · fictional

The nurse in the story argued, politely, that a memo would not change the inbox. She presented the 19 of 46 without naming anyone, mapped where results went on a Friday night, and proposed one small test: a named weekend backup reviewer with a callback script, for one month.

She reported the two delayed cases through the safety reporting system as system events, and set up the measure so that only a documented review and disposition counted, not a notification.

After one month, the share of abnormal results reviewed within the interval rose from 41 percent to 88 percent. Weekend calls rose by a third, which became the balancing measure for the second round.

Documentation task. Write a one-page improvement charter: population, failure description, numerator and denominator, baseline and target, change to test, balancing measure, responsible role and review date.
Second round · what changesThe callback intervention improves review times to 88 percent but increases weekend callback workload by a third.
Submit: A revised charter stating the measure, the balancing measure result and the next test of change.

Supervised practice

With your preceptor

With your unit's quality lead. These are prompts for supervised learning, not a procedure. Use your center’s own protocols, contacts and forms; your preceptor observes and signs off each item on the facility’s competency record.

  • Define a closed-loop measure with numerator, denominator, exclusions, data source and owner.
  • Draw a run chart from synthetic monthly data and identify a trend.
  • Plan a PDSA cycle with a balancing measure and present it to your preceptor.
  • Adapt a discharge plan for a frail older adult or an adolescent recipient.
  • Review your center's pregnancy-planning and contraception counseling process.

Terms

Process measure
A measure of whether a step in care was done, as opposed to the clinical outcome.
Balancing measure
A measure that checks whether an improvement in one area has caused a problem elsewhere.
Run chart
A graph of a measure over time used to see trends and the effect of changes.
PDSA cycle
Plan-Do-Study-Act, a small, rapid test of a change.
