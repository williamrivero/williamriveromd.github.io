Training track for nephrology fellows, residents and medical interns

Study material for medical interns, residents and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and gives no drug doses. Doses, rates and order sets come from current guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved protocols and your supervising physician. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Intern: Recognize glomerular blood and protein in the urine (cola-colored urine, dysmorphic red cells, foamy urine, edema), report them the same day, and collect the history that points to a cause.
  • Resident: Separate the nephritic from the nephrotic syndrome, order the first-line serologies and quantified proteinuria, and recognize the rapidly progressive pattern that needs the attending tonight.
  • Fellow: Decide whether a kidney biopsy will change management, read the pathology report (light microscopy, immunofluorescence, electron microscopy), and treat by the current Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) chapters with their stated grades.

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · the urine that tells you the filter is inflamedThe intern is usually first to see it: a urine bag the color of cola, a dipstick that reads blood and protein, puffy eyelids in the morning. Your job is to report it the same day and to ask about sore throats, skin infections, joint pains, rashes and new medicines. The resident sorts the picture into a syndrome, orders the right tests in the right order, and spots the patient whose creatinine is climbing by the day. The fellow decides whether the patient needs a biopsy, reads it with the pathologist, and writes a treatment plan that matches the KDIGO chapter for that disease.
In plain wordsThink of the glomerulus as a coffee filter. A healthy filter lets water and small molecules through and holds back the grounds. Tear the paper and grounds, here red cells, leak into the cup. Clog the paper with grease and the flow slows. Stretch the pores and fine powder, here albumin, slips through while the water still runs.

The picture has a limit. A coffee filter is passive paper; the glomerular wall is living tissue in three layers, each damaged in a different way. The endothelium faces the blood and is hit by circulating immune complexes, complement and antibodies. The glomerular basement membrane (GBM) in the middle can be attacked directly (anti-GBM disease) or thickened by deposits. The podocytes on the urine side hold the final slit diaphragm; when they are injured or lose their foot processes, albumin pours through without inflammation. Where the injury sits decides what you see. Inflammation inside the capillary tuft (endocapillary or mesangial) breaks capillaries and lets red cells out: the nephritic picture. Injury to the podocyte without inflammation lets protein out: the nephrotic picture. Many diseases sit between the two.

Most patients with blood or protein in the urine have a slowly evolving disease that leaves days or weeks to complete the workup. A few have a rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis that can destroy the kidneys in days and needs the attending tonight. Your work is to name the syndrome, test in an order that answers the urgent question first, decide with the fellow and attending whether a biopsy will change what you do, and treat by the chapter and grade, not by habit.

Opening case · fictional

Case: The night-shift agent with cola-colored urine

Thursday, 7 a.m., the outpatient clinic of a tertiary hospital in Quezon City. Kristine Dela Cruz, 24, works the night shift at a call center in Ortigas. Two days ago she had a sore throat and fever. Yesterday her urine turned the color of cola. She had no rash, no joint pain and no cough with blood. She takes no regular medicines except an over-the-counter painkiller for headaches “a few times a week.” Her pre-employment urinalysis two years ago showed “red blood cell (RBC) 5–10/hpf, trace protein,” and nobody followed it up.

Her blood pressure is 146/92 mm Hg, repeated after rest. She has mild ankle edema. Her creatinine is 1.3 mg/dL; it was 0.9 mg/dL at her pre-employment test. Her urine shows 3+ blood and 2+ protein. The intern spins the sample and finds dysmorphic red cells and two red cell casts. The spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) is 1.8 g/g. Serum albumin is 3.6 g/dL. Her mother asks whether this means dialysis, and whether the company health maintenance organization (HMO) will cover a biopsy.

Before reading on, decide:

  • Is this nephritic or nephrotic, and which tests do you need today rather than this week?
  • Does she need a kidney biopsy, and what would the result change?
  • If the biopsy shows IgA nephropathy, what will the treatment plan look like over the next six months?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

Nephritic, nephrotic, and the patterns between

Start with physiology. When immune complexes or antibodies settle in the capillary wall or mesangium, they fix complement and attract neutrophils and macrophages. The inflamed capillary loop breaks. Red cells squeeze through the gap and are deformed on their way down the tubule, which is why they look dysmorphic. Some are caught in a cast of Tamm–Horsfall protein in the distal tubule: a red cell cast, which proves the bleeding is glomerular. The inflamed tuft filters less, so the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) falls, sodium is retained, and blood pressure rises. That is the nephritic syndrome: hematuria with dysmorphic cells or casts, a rising creatinine, hypertension and edema, with protein usually below the nephrotic range.

When the podocyte is injured without inflammation, the slit diaphragm leaks albumin while the capillaries stay intact. Albumin is lost faster than the liver can replace it. The liver responds by making more of everything, including lipoproteins and clotting factors, so cholesterol rises and the blood clots more easily. Edema forms from sodium retention by the kidney and, in some patients, from the fall in oncotic pressure. That is the nephrotic syndrome: heavy proteinuria (conventionally more than 3.5 g/day in adults), low serum albumin, edema and hyperlipidemia, usually with a bland sediment.

Two other presentations matter. Asymptomatic urinary abnormalities are microscopic hematuria with or without low-grade proteinuria, found on a screening urinalysis like Kristine’s pre-employment test. They can be the quiet start of IgA nephropathy, thin basement membrane disease or Alport syndrome, and they deserve a repeat test and a quantified protein, not a shrug. Visible hematuria during an upper respiratory infection (synpharyngitic hematuria, within a day or two of the sore throat) suggests IgA nephropathy. Hematuria that appears one to three weeks after a throat or skin infection suggests post-infectious glomerulonephritis.

The intern’s most useful act is to look at the urine. An automated report of “RBC 25–50/hpf” cannot tell you whether those cells came from a glomerulus or a bladder. Module 4 teaches sediment microscopy; a resident who has seen the casts with their own eyes will rarely miss a glomerular disease.

RememberName the syndrome from the urine you looked at yourself. Dysmorphic cells and casts mean the filter is inflamed; heavy protein with a bland sediment means the podocyte is leaking.
Lesson · part 2

Ordering tests in the order that answers the urgent question

Serology is not a panel to send all at once and read next week. It is a sequence. The first question is always: is this rapidly progressive? A creatinine that has risen over days, oliguria, hemoptysis, or a nephritic sediment with a creatinine already above baseline means you need antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) and anti-GBM antibodies today, because the treatment for those diseases is time-critical (Module 18). The second question is: what is the likely cause? That depends on the syndrome and the history.

Complement tells you about mechanism. Immune-complex diseases that activate the classical pathway, such as lupus nephritis, consume C3 and C4. Post-infectious glomerulonephritis and C3 glomerulopathy mainly lower C3. IgA nephropathy, ANCA vasculitis, anti-GBM disease and the podocytopathies usually leave complement normal. A normal C3 in Kristine’s case points away from post-infectious disease and lupus and toward IgA nephropathy.

Anti-PLA2R changed membranous nephropathy. Beck and colleagues identified the M-type phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) on podocytes as the target antigen in 2009. Serum from 26 of 37 patients (70%) with primary membranous nephropathy recognized it; serum from patients with secondary membranous disease did not.1 The antibodies were mainly IgG4, the same subclass found in the glomerular deposits. A positive antibody in a typical nephrotic adult strongly supports primary membranous nephropathy, and the level falls before proteinuria does when treatment works. A negative result does not exclude it.

In the Philippines, ANCA, anti-GBM and anti-PLA2R tests are often sent to a reference laboratory in Metro Manila, with results in days and the cost paid out of pocket. Plan around that. Draw and send the urgent tests first, document the date sent, and do not delay a decision the attending must make tonight because a send-out result is pending.

Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist tonight, per unit protocol, but always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance, when you see:
  • A nephritic sediment with a creatinine that has risen over days, or is already more than about 1.5 times the known baseline
  • Oliguria or a creatinine that rises on two successive daily checks
  • Hemoptysis, a falling hemoglobin with lung infiltrates, or hypoxemia in a patient with hematuria
  • A nephrotic patient with a swollen leg, chest pain or breathlessness (thrombosis)
  • Blood pressure at emergency levels with headache, visual change or confusion
RememberAsk first whether the disease is rapidly progressive. If it might be, send ANCA, anti-GBM and complement today and call; everything else can follow in a planned sequence.
Lesson · part 3

Will the biopsy change what you do?

A kidney biopsy is the only test that shows the lesion. Serology suggests; the biopsy names the disease, grades how active it is, and shows how much is already scarred. That last point is the one trainees forget. Two patients with the same serology can have very different futures: one with cellular crescents and active inflammation that will respond to treatment, the other with sclerosed glomeruli and fibrosis that will not.

The KDIGO 2021 glomerular guideline frames the decision around whether the biopsy result will change management.2 The KDIGO 2025 IgA nephropathy guideline moved toward a more liberal biopsy policy, suggesting that a biopsy be considered in adults with proteinuria of 0.5 g/day or more when IgA nephropathy is possible, and noting that only a biopsy can diagnose it.3,4

Risks, with absolute numbers. Poggio and colleagues pooled 87 studies of native kidney biopsies done with automated needles under imaging, a total of 118,064 biopsies. Hematoma was estimated in 11%, visible hematuria in 3.5%, bleeding needing transfusion in 1.6%, an intervention to stop bleeding in 0.3%, and death in 0.06% (0.03% for outpatient biopsies). Complications were more frequent in hospitalized patients and in those with acute kidney disease.5 These are pooled observational estimates; your own unit’s numbers may differ. Uncontrolled blood pressure, a low platelet count, anticoagulants and antiplatelets, uremia and a small or single kidney raise the risk. Correct what you can before the procedure, by your unit’s protocol.

Reading the report. A complete native kidney biopsy has three parts. Light microscopy counts glomeruli (an adequate sample has enough glomeruli for the question being asked), describes the pattern (mesangial, endocapillary, crescentic, membranous, sclerosing), and grades chronicity: globally sclerosed glomeruli, interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. Immunofluorescence shows what is deposited: IgA dominant in IgA nephropathy, “full house” IgG, IgA, IgM, C3 and C1q in lupus, linear IgG along the GBM in anti-GBM disease, little or none (“pauci-immune”) in ANCA vasculitis, granular IgG along capillary walls in membranous nephropathy. Electron microscopy shows where the deposits sit and whether the podocyte foot processes are effaced, the only finding in minimal change disease.

Philippine practice. Many provincial hospitals can do the biopsy but cannot do immunofluorescence or electron microscopy, so the tissue must be split, placed in the right media, and sent to Manila. A biopsy with light microscopy alone may not answer the question. Confirm before the procedure where each core is going, who pays, and how long the report will take. HMO and Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) coverage for the procedure and the special stains varies; ask the billing office before, not after. When biopsy is unavailable or unsafe, document why and treat on the best clinical and serologic evidence with the attending.

Lesson · part 4

The diseases you will meet most

IgA nephropathy (IgAN). The working model has four hits: poorly galactosylated IgA1 circulates, autoantibodies form against it, immune complexes deposit in the mesangium, and the mesangium proliferates and recruits complement. Bleeding follows mucosal infections because mucosal immunity drives IgA production. Proteinuria and nephron loss then add hyperfiltration injury. The KDIGO 2025 guideline names proteinuria and estimated GFR (eGFR) as the only validated prognostic markers, recommends scoring the biopsy by the Oxford MEST-C system and using the International IgAN Prediction Tool for shared decisions, and says neither tool should be used to choose a specific therapy.3 It defines rapidly progressive IgAN as a fall in eGFR of 50% or more over 3 months or less, after other causes are excluded.3

Membranous nephropathy. Antibodies, most often against PLA2R, bind antigens on the podocyte; the complexes form beneath it and complement injures the cell. Some patients remit without immunosuppression, so treatment is matched to the risk of progression. In MENTOR, 130 adults with proteinuria of at least 5 g/day after three months of angiotensin blockade were randomized to rituximab or cyclosporine. At 24 months, complete or partial remission was maintained in 60% with rituximab versus 20% with cyclosporine; serious adverse events occurred in 17% versus 31%.6 KDIGO 2021 recommends, for membranous nephropathy with at least one risk factor for progression, immunosuppressive therapy with rituximab, or cyclophosphamide with glucocorticoids, or a calcineurin inhibitor-based regimen (1B).2

Minimal change disease (MCD) and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS). In MCD the podocyte foot processes flatten, seen only on electron microscopy, and the lesion responds to glucocorticoids in most adults; KDIGO 2021 recommends high-dose oral glucocorticoids for initial treatment (1C).2 FSGS is a lesion, not a disease. The fellow’s job is to ask which kind: primary (a circulating factor, with diffuse foot process effacement and full nephrotic syndrome), genetic, or secondary to reduced nephron mass, obesity, viruses or drugs (with segmental effacement and often no hypoalbuminemia). Only primary FSGS is treated with immunosuppression.2

Diabetic kidney disease is the most common glomerular disease you will see, and Module 13 covers it. Its typical course is years of diabetes, retinopathy, rising albuminuria and gradual eGFR decline. Any break from that course is a reason to biopsy.

Lesson · part 5

Two tracks of treatment: protect the nephrons, and stop the immune attack

Every glomerular disease has two problems. The immune problem is the disease itself. The nephron problem is what the disease has already done: fewer working glomeruli, each hyperfiltering, plus proteinuria that injures the tubules downstream. KDIGO 2025 makes this explicit for IgAN: manage the consequences of existing nephron loss in everyone, and add disease-specific treatment for patients at risk of progression.3

TrackWhat it doesWhat it includes
Supportive (every patient)Lowers glomerular pressure and proteinuria; slows scarringMaximally tolerated angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), blood pressure control, sodium below 2 g/day, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor where indicated, stop smoking, weight control, statin when indicated
Disease-specific (selected patients)Stops the immune injuryGlucocorticoids, rituximab, cyclophosphamide, calcineurin inhibitors, mycophenolate, targeted-release budesonide; chosen by disease, risk and chapter
Safety (anyone immunosuppressed)Prevents the harm of treatmentHepatitis B and tuberculosis screening, infection prophylaxis per protocol, vaccination, bone protection, contraception counseling

IgAN in KDIGO 2025. All patients should receive an optimized, maximally tolerated angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB); the blood pressure target is 120/70 mm Hg or lower; lifestyle advice includes sodium below 2 g/day. Where approved, sparsentan may be used in patients at risk and should replace, not be added to, the ACE inhibitor or ARB; a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor may be added. For the immune track, a 9-month course of targeted-release budesonide is suggested for patients at risk; where it is unavailable, a reduced-dose systemic glucocorticoid regimen with antimicrobial prophylaxis. The goal is proteinuria below 0.5 g/day, ideally below 0.3 g/day, and a rate of eGFR loss near the physiologic, below about 1 mL/min per year.3,4 Tonsillectomy, used in Japan, should not be performed as a treatment of IgAN in non-Japanese patients.3

The evidence behind the glucocorticoid option. TESTING randomized 503 patients with IgAN, proteinuria of at least 1 g/day and an eGFR of 20 to 120 mL/min per 1.73 m² after at least three months of optimized supportive care, at 67 centers in Australia, Canada, China, India and Malaysia, to oral methylprednisolone or placebo. Over a mean of 4.2 years, the primary outcome (40% eGFR decline, kidney failure or death from kidney disease) occurred in 28.8% versus 43.1% (hazard ratio 0.53), an absolute difference of 4.8 percentage points per year. Kidney failure alone occurred in 19.5% versus 27.2%.8 The trial was interrupted after an excess of serious infections, and the dose was reduced with pneumocystis prophylaxis added; the benefit was seen with both regimens.8 The population was mostly Asian, which is relevant to Filipino patients. The harm is real: the safety track is not optional.

A surrogate, labeled. In PROTECT, 404 adults with IgAN and proteinuria of at least 1 g/day despite maximized angiotensin blockade received sparsentan or irbesartan. The chronic eGFR slope was −2.7 versus −3.8 mL/min per 1.73 m² per year (difference 1.1), and proteinuria remained lower with sparsentan at 110 weeks.9 eGFR slope and proteinuria are surrogate outcomes; the trial was not sized to show fewer patients reaching kidney failure.

What you do at your stage. The intern records weight, blood pressure and urine output daily, and flags edema, breathlessness or a swollen leg. The resident makes sure every patient is on the supportive track before anyone talks about immunosuppression, completes the safety screen, and quantifies proteinuria the same way each time (the same lab, a spot UPCR or a 24-hour collection, not a mixture). The fellow chooses the disease-specific regimen with the attending, writes the target and the date it should be reached, and explains the cost. In the Philippines, rituximab, budesonide, sparsentan and SGLT2 inhibitors are paid out of pocket by most patients. A plan the family cannot sustain is not a plan; discuss the evidence-based choices that fit their means, and document the conversation.

Pregnancy changes the plan. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors, sparsentan and some immunosuppressants must be stopped or changed before conception; KDIGO 2025 advises preconception counseling.3 Ask every woman of childbearing age about plans and contraception before you start.

RememberPut every patient on the supportive track first, screen for infection before any immunosuppression, and write the proteinuria target with the date you expect to reach it.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The pre-employment hematuria

What you see
A 27-year-old seaman bound for his first contract has microscopic hematuria on the agency medical exam; protein is trace and blood pressure is normal.
What is likely going on
Asymptomatic urinary abnormality: possibly early IgA nephropathy, thin basement membrane disease, or a non-glomerular cause.
What to do
Repeat the urinalysis and look at the sediment yourself, quantify protein with a UPCR, check creatinine and blood pressure, and ask about family hematuria, hearing loss and kidney failure.
Why it matters
Most of these patients need monitoring, not a biopsy, but the ones whose protein rises are the ones who lose kidneys silently.

The edematous grandmother in Pampanga

What you see
A 68-year-old woman has new nephrotic syndrome, albumin 2.1 g/dL, and weight loss of 5 kg over three months.
What is likely going on
Membranous nephropathy, possibly secondary to malignancy; also amyloidosis or a paraprotein disease.
What to do
Send anti-PLA2R, serum and urine protein electrophoresis with free light chains, hepatitis serologies, and age-appropriate cancer screening; ask about clot symptoms; discuss biopsy with the fellow.
Why it matters
A nephrotic older adult with weight loss needs a search for a secondary cause before immunosuppression.

The student with lupus and a creatinine that will not wait

What you see
A 20-year-old with known lupus has new hematuria with casts and a creatinine that rose from 0.8 to 1.6 mg/dL in a week.
What is likely going on
Active lupus nephritis, possibly class III or IV, or a superimposed rapidly progressive process.
What to do
Call the fellow and attending today, send complement and anti-double-stranded DNA, plan an urgent biopsy, and do not wait for send-out results before the attending decides on treatment.
Why it matters
A doubling creatinine in days is a rapidly progressive pattern; Module 18 covers treatment.

The biopsy with eight glomeruli and no immunofluorescence

What you see
A provincial hospital biopsy shows mesangial proliferation on light microscopy only; no tissue was sent for immunofluorescence.
What is likely going on
The report cannot distinguish IgA nephropathy from lupus or other mesangial diseases.
What to do
Discuss with the pathologist whether stored tissue can be stained; correlate with complement, ANA and the clinical picture; plan with the attending whether a repeat biopsy is justified.
Why it matters
A biopsy without the right stains may not answer the question it was done for; plan the split before the needle goes in.
Field notes
  • Spin the urine yourself on every new glomerular patient; a photo of the casts on your phone helps your presentation.
  • Write the date each send-out test left the hospital in the progress note.
  • Use one method for proteinuria (spot UPCR or 24-hour collection) and keep to it so that trends mean something.
  • Before any immunosuppression, check hepatitis B surface antigen, core antibody and a tuberculosis screen, and document them.
  • Ask every woman of childbearing age about pregnancy plans before you start an ACE inhibitor, ARB, SGLT2 inhibitor or immunosuppressant.
Case review

Back to the case: The night-shift agent with cola-colored urine

Nephritic, and what today. Kristine has a nephritic picture: dysmorphic red cells and red cell casts, hypertension, edema and a creatinine that has risen from 0.9 to 1.3 mg/dL, with sub-nephrotic proteinuria and near-normal albumin. Visible hematuria two days after a sore throat (synpharyngitic) and old microscopic hematuria point to IgA nephropathy. Because her creatinine has risen, send ANCA, anti-GBM antibody, C3, C4 and ANA today, and recheck creatinine within 24 to 48 hours to see whether it is still climbing. Hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV can follow. Ask her to stop the over-the-counter painkiller, which may be an NSAID.

The biopsy. Yes. KDIGO 2025 suggests considering a biopsy in adults with proteinuria of 0.5 g/day or more when IgAN is possible, and only a biopsy can diagnose it. It will also show whether there are crescents or active inflammation that would change urgency, and how much scarring exists. Before the procedure, control her blood pressure, confirm normal platelets and clotting, and arrange for immunofluorescence and electron microscopy. Explain to her mother the risks with numbers: bleeding needing transfusion in about 1.6% and death in about 0.06% in pooled data. Ask the billing office about HMO coverage for the procedure and the special stains.

The plan if IgAN. Start the supportive track now: an ACE inhibitor or ARB titrated to the maximum she tolerates, blood pressure at or below 120/70 mm Hg, sodium below 2 g/day, and an SGLT2 inhibitor if affordable. Recheck UPCR at intervals with the same method. If proteinuria stays at or above 0.5 g/day, discuss disease-specific therapy: targeted-release budesonide where available, or a reduced-dose glucocorticoid regimen with prophylaxis, after hepatitis B and tuberculosis screening. Counsel her on contraception, because several of these drugs cannot be taken in pregnancy. Agree on a target of proteinuria below 0.5 g/day, ideally below 0.3 g/day, and review her night-shift schedule so that she can attend follow-up.

Question 1 of 5An intern sees cola-colored urine in a 24-year-old two days after a sore throat. Which finding best confirms that the blood is coming from the glomerulus?
Casts form in the tubule and dysmorphic cells are deformed passing through it, so both prove a glomerular source. A dipstick reads blood from anywhere in the urinary tract, and also reads myoglobin.
Question 2 of 5A resident sees a patient with a nephritic sediment whose creatinine has risen from 1.0 to 2.4 mg/dL in five days. What is the best next step?
A creatinine more than doubling in days with a nephritic sediment is rapidly progressive until proven otherwise; ANCA and anti-GBM diseases need treatment within days. Anti-PLA2R is a nephrotic-syndrome test and does not answer the urgent question.
Question 3 of 5According to KDIGO 2025, what is the proteinuria treatment goal in IgA nephropathy?
KDIGO 2025 lowered the target from the older 1 g/day threshold, because registries show progression even at 0.5 to 1 g/day. A 25% reduction is a lupus-style response marker, not the IgAN goal.
Question 4 of 5A 52-year-old with nephrotic syndrome and normal eGFR has a strongly positive anti-PLA2R antibody. Which statement is most accurate?
KDIGO 2021 allows a diagnosis of membranous nephropathy on a positive anti-PLA2R in a typical case without biopsy. Secondary causes (hepatitis B, malignancy, drugs) must still be sought, because a positive test supports but does not exclude them in every case.
Question 5 of 5In the TESTING trial of oral methylprednisolone for high-risk IgA nephropathy, what happened?
TESTING showed a hard kidney composite benefit (heart rate (HR) 0.53) over 4.2 years, but early serious infections forced a lower dose and pneumocystis prophylaxis. The population was largely Asian. Proteinuria-only benefit describes surrogate trials such as the PROTECT slope analysis.
