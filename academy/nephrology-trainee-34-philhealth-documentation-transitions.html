Training track for nephrology fellows, residents and medical interns

Study material for medical interns, residents and fellows. It explains the reasoning behind common decisions; it is not a protocol and gives no drug doses. Doses, rates and order sets come from current guidelines, product labels, your institution’s approved protocols and your supervising physician. Benefit rules change: confirm them on the date of service. Every case is fictional.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to

  • Intern: Document a dialysis patient’s diagnosis, sessions, medicines and handoffs accurately, so that the record can support both the next clinician and a Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) claim.
  • Resident: Complete PhilHealth Dialysis Database (PDD) registration and hemodialysis (HD) benefit records correctly, and explain the 156-session benefit to a patient and family.
  • Fellow: Own benefit-aware transitions between hospital, units, peritoneal dialysis (PD) and transplant without letting benefit rules drive the prescription.

Start here

The big picture, in plain words

Your role · the paper that keeps the patient dialyzingThe intern writes the notes and the medicine list that everyone downstream will copy. The resident fills in the registration and benefit papers and sits with the family to explain them. The fellow decides how the patient moves between settings, and checks that the prescription was written for the kidneys, not for the payer. When the paper is wrong, a patient arrives at a new unit with no record, no registration and no sessions recognized, and misses dialysis while the papers are fixed.
In plain wordsThink of the hemodialysis benefit as a book of prepaid bus tickets. Each calendar year the registered passenger gets 156 tickets, one per trip, enough for three trips a week all year. The tickets work only on accredited buses, and only if the passenger was registered before the first trip.

The picture has a limit. A bus ticket does not care where you go. A dialysis prescription does: the number of sessions, their length and what is given in them come from the patient’s kidneys, heart, volume and laboratory results, not from the size of the ticket book. PhilHealth says so itself: the services in the package depend on the provider’s clinical assessment and are not intended to be provided on the patient’s demand.1 The benefit removes a barrier; it does not write the order.

Most transitions go smoothly when three things travel with the patient: a true diagnosis of chronic kidney disease stage 5 with its evidence, an active PDD registration, and a complete transfer packet. A few go wrong because one of these is missing, or because someone chose a prescription to fit the benefit. Your work is to make the paper accurate, to make the handoff complete, and to keep the clinical decision clinical.

Opening case · fictional

Case: Going home to Sorsogon

Thursday, 3 p.m., the medicine ward of a tertiary hospital in Quezon City. Mr. Romeo Dizon, 58, a tricycle driver with type 2 diabetes for 15 years, was admitted three weeks ago breathless and vomiting, with a creatinine of 11.8 mg/dL and a potassium of 6.6 mmol/L. A creatinine of 4.1 mg/dL from a clinic visit 14 months ago is on his phone. His kidneys are 8.6 and 8.9 cm and echogenic. He started HD through a right internal jugular tunneled catheter and has had nine sessions. The attending nephrologist has documented chronic kidney disease stage 5 from diabetic kidney disease. He still passes about 500 mL of urine a day.

He will be discharged on Saturday to his family in Sorsogon City, where there is an accredited HD unit about 20 minutes from his home. Dr. Andrea Lim, the second-year resident, is asked to “fix the PhilHealth papers.” His son, Jun, who works in Dubai and is on the phone, asks: “Can Papa do dialysis only twice a week so the 156 sessions last, and so we spend less on the fare?” The unit in Sorsogon has asked for “the usual referral.”

Before reading on, decide:

  • What must be documented and registered before Saturday so that Mr. Dizon’s benefit works in Sorsogon?
  • How should the team answer Jun’s request for twice-weekly dialysis?
  • What goes in the transfer packet, and how should the handoff to the Sorsogon unit be done?

Keep your answers in mind. The lesson gives you what you need, and the case review near the end shows one sound response.

Lesson · part 1

The hemodialysis benefit: 156 sessions and what they require

PhilHealth Circular 2023-0009 institutionalized the expansion of the HD benefit from 90 to 156 sessions a year, explaining the number as three sessions a week for 52 weeks, the frequency it describes as the current standard for adequate HD. It defines the PDD as the system that collects data on members and dependents with chronic kidney disease stage 5.2 PhilHealth’s own guide to the circular states that sessions not used in a year cannot be added to the next.3 Revision 2, Circular 2024-0023, carries the benefit forward with coverage expansion.4

The rate changed twice in 2024. PhilHealth’s 2025 news release states that the package rate became PHP 6,350 per session effective October 9, 2024, that patients with chronic kidney disease stage 5 registered in the PDD can avail of up to 156 sessions a year, and that PhilHealth will therefore shoulder up to PHP 990,600 a year per patient.5 Press reports of the circular gave October 7 as the effective date; if a claim around those days matters, check the circular itself. The patient agreement form states that the PHP 6,350 rate includes the fees of the facility and the professional.6

A 2024 advisory lists the minimum standards of the package: an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent in the vial sizes of the Philippine National Formulary, intravenous iron sucrose as indicated, heparin or enoxaparin as needed, laboratory tests as needed (complete blood count, creatinine, hepatitis profile, alkaline phosphatase, potassium, phosphorus, calcium, iron studies and albumin), low-flux or high-flux dialyzers, and the use of machines, utilities and staff time. It says coverage is subject to the policy’s no-copay provision, that services depend on the patient’s status by the provider’s clinical assessment, and that they are not provided on demand.1 The Philippine Society of Nephrology (PSN) guideline asks the attending nephrologist to comply with the documentary requirements for the patient’s PhilHealth HD benefit.7

The causal chain from paper to dialysis chair is short. No PDD registration, no recognized sessions. No clear diagnosis of chronic kidney disease stage 5, no registration. No signed treatment record for each session, no claim. An error anywhere upstream becomes a family paying out of pocket, or a patient skipping sessions to save money.

Two integrity points deserve to be said plainly. A patient on dialysis for acute kidney injury that may recover is not a patient with chronic kidney disease stage 5, and labeling him so to unlock a benefit is a false record that also closes the door on recovery-focused follow-up (Module 9). And the benefit has no place in deciding how many sessions a patient needs: the prescription comes from residual function, volume, potassium, symptoms and adequacy, as Modules 22 and 23 explain. When a family asks for fewer sessions to save the tickets, the honest answer is that 156 sessions are enough for three a week all year, and that any change in frequency is a clinical decision the attending makes and monitors.

At your stageIntern: write the evidence of chronicity in your notes, with dates. Resident: start the PDD registration with the hospital’s PhilHealth or dialysis staff as soon as the attending documents stage 5, not on the day of discharge. Fellow: check that every prescription change in the chart has a clinical reason written beside it.
RememberRegister the true diagnosis early, document every session, and never let the ticket book write the prescription.
Lesson · part 2

Peritoneal dialysis and its Z benefit

Peritoneal dialysis is underused in the Philippines, and part of the reason is that the patient, the family and often the physician never hear it offered. A benefit exists. PhilHealth Circular 2024-0036 governs the Z benefits package for PD.8 PhilHealth’s January 2025 release describes an expanded package effective January 1, 2025: for adults it covers continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) only, at PHP 389,640 or PHP 510,140 a year depending on the daily PD solutions needed; for children it covers CAPD and automated peritoneal dialysis (APD) at higher ranges. Patients must have chronic kidney disease stage 5 and be registered in the PDD first, must meet membership eligibility, must follow their treatment plan including follow-up visits, and must not share, sell or distribute the PD solutions provided to them. The release listed 51 contracted PD providers nationwide.9 PhilHealth has also run a pilot of APD coverage for children under separate advisories; check the current status.

The mechanism behind PD’s value in the provinces is practical. PD runs at home, every day, without a machine for CAPD, without a three-times-weekly trip and without a dialysis chair. For a patient who lives two hours by bus from the nearest HD unit, or who wants to keep working, that changes life more than any laboratory number. It also needs a contracted provider for training, supplies and follow-up, a clean space at home, and someone able to do the exchanges.

There is no trial in this module’s sources showing that the PD benefit changes outcomes; what it changes is access. The decision between HD and PD rests on the clinical and personal factors in Module 27, and both should be offered where both are possible.

At your stageIntern: ask every new dialysis patient whether anyone has explained PD. Resident: know the nearest contracted PD provider to your hospital’s catchment. Fellow: offer PD with HD in every modality conversation where it is medically possible, and document that you did.
RememberA benefit no one mentions is a benefit no one uses. Offer PD where it is possible, and know who near the patient’s home can provide it.
Lesson · part 3

The kidney transplant Z benefit, Revision 1

The best treatment for many patients with kidney failure is a transplant, and cost is one of the reasons Filipino families never begin the evaluation. PhilHealth Circular 2024-0035 set the first Z benefits package for kidney transplantation.10 Circular 2026-0019, the Z Benefits Package for Kidney Transplantation (Revision 1), signed on October 26, 2026, amends it and covers both adults and children.11 The circular takes effect 15 days after publication in the Official Gazette or a newspaper of general circulation; the document itself does not state the publication date, so confirm the effective date with PhilHealth. Care after the transplant falls under separate PhilHealth benefits; check the current circulars.

Who and where. All patients with chronic kidney disease stage 5 who meet the clinical criteria are eligible, with a living donor, related or non-related, or a deceased donor, subject to PhilHealth’s membership eligibility rules. Selection follows the circular’s pre-authorization checklist and request form, and the benefit is available only in contracted health facilities. A multidisciplinary team (MDT) that includes the attending nephrologist, a transplant surgeon and, for living-donor procedures, a urologist evaluates donor and recipient and submits the treatment plan. Facilities must follow the ethical guidelines on living donation (Department of Health [Department of Health (DOH)] Administrative Order 2021-0059) and the laws on voluntary donation; deceased donation must be non-commercial, and a deceased-donor claim needs an organ retrieval report from an authorized organ procurement organization.11

Timing. For a living-donor transplant, the facility’s liaison submits the pre-authorization checklist and request form, the Member Empowerment form and the MDT plan before the benefit is used. For a deceased-donor transplant, pre-authorization is submitted within 2 working days after the transplant. An approved pre-authorization is valid for 180 calendar days. Contracted facilities assign a kidney transplantation (KT) registration number and submit a KT Registry form for every patient, and the facility files the claim within 60 days after discharge.11

What it covers. Pre-transplant evaluation; immunosuppressant therapy in the induction phase until one week after discharge from the transplant; the transplant surgery; donor management, donor nephrectomy, and organ retrieval and preservation. The 45-day annual benefit limit does not apply. There is no separate facility and professional fee: PhilHealth pays the facility, which distributes the professional fee. Drugs are reimbursed if they are in the latest Philippine National Drug Formulary (PNDF); otherwise they need a positive health technology assessment recommendation.11

What the family pays. There is no copayment in basic or ward accommodation, none for recipients who need special beds such as reverse isolation, and none for managing postoperative complications of donors and recipients. Copayment is allowed only for amenities, the choice of a physician within the MDT, specialist fees in non-basic accommodation, or services the package does not cover, and it must be agreed during the Member Empowerment form discussion. No balance billing beyond the package rate is allowed for covered services.11

If the graft fails. After the Z benefit, patients remain eligible for the HD or PD benefits if they return to dialysis, whether because of graft failure from rejection or infection, acute kidney injury or other complications.11 Tell families this early: fear of losing the dialysis benefit should not be the reason they decline a transplant evaluation.

You can sayYou should not say
“PhilHealth has a Z benefit for kidney transplantation, from a living or a deceased donor, for adults and children, at contracted centers.”“It is covered,” before the transplant center has checked eligibility and pre-authorization
“The package rate depends on the donor type, the induction medicine and the surgical method; the transplant center’s liaison will confirm the current rate.”A rate as if it were final, without confirming the effective date
“In ward accommodation there is no copayment, and the hospital may not bill beyond the package for covered services.”Promises about medicines after the induction phase; those fall under other benefits and the formulary
“If the kidney fails, you can return to the dialysis benefit.”“You will lose PhilHealth dialysis if you get a transplant”

The physician’s job is the same whatever the rate. Refer early to a transplant center when the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is falling toward kidney failure (Module 29), identify potential living donors and explain that donation must be voluntary and within the ethical rules, and keep the patient’s records, serologies and dialysis history complete so that the MDT evaluation and pre-authorization are not delayed.

At your stageIntern: record whether transplant has been discussed and whether a living donor has been named. Resident: refer to the transplant center and its PhilHealth liaison, and tell the family about the no-copayment and return-to-dialysis rules. Fellow: know your nearest contracted transplant center, the pre-authorization timing for living and deceased donors, and read the current circular before every benefit conversation.
RememberQuote a benefit only from the circular you have read, with its date, and send the family to the transplant center’s liaison to confirm the effective date and rate on the day.
Lesson · part 4

The transfer packet and the handoff

A dialysis patient who moves from a hospital to an outpatient unit, from one unit to another, or from one province to another carries risk in the move itself. Information gets lost at each transfer. A systematic review of communication at hospital discharge found that direct communication between hospital physicians and primary care physicians happened in only 3% to 20% of discharges, that a discharge summary was available at the first post-discharge visit in only 12% to 34%, and that summaries often lacked test results (missing from 33% to 63%), discharge medicines (2% to 40%), tests pending at discharge (65%) and follow-up plans (2% to 43%).12 Those studies were observational, mostly from North American settings, but every Filipino trainee who has received a patient with “no papers” will recognize them.

Structured handoffs help. In the multicenter study of a structured resident handoff program, medical errors fell from 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions and preventable adverse events from 4.7 to 3.3 per 100 admissions after implementation, without longer handoffs.13 The design was before and after, so it shows association rather than proof; the lesson that a standard structure reduces omissions carries over to a transfer letter.

Send the packet through a channel the patient has agreed to, and only to the people who need it; the Data Privacy Act treats health information as sensitive personal information.14 Give the patient and family a copy to carry, because the courier is often the only reliable delivery system across provinces. Then make a telephone call, physician to physician or unit to unit, before the first session. The call is where the receiving physician asks the question your letter did not answer.

Arithmetic illustration, not a target. Sessions remaining = 156 − sessions already claimed this calendar year.
A patient who has had 9 sessions in the hospital this year has 147 left for the rest of the calendar year, wherever he dialyzes. A patient who moves in December with 140 sessions used has 16 left, and the count resets on January 1.
Stop and callStop and call the attending nephrologist, per unit protocol, but always ask the attending nephrologist for guidance, when:
  • a family asks for fewer sessions or a shorter prescription to save money or sessions;
  • a patient is about to be discharged with no confirmed receiving unit or first session date;
  • a diagnosis of chronic kidney disease stage 5 is being written mainly to register a patient whose kidneys may recover;
  • the receiving unit cannot take the patient because of serology status or capacity.
At your stageIntern: draft the packet from the chart and list what you could not find. Resident: complete it, give the family a copy, and confirm the first session date at the receiving unit. Fellow: make the physician-to-physician call and check that the plan is safe for the first week.
RememberA transfer is complete when the receiving physician has the packet, the patient has a copy, someone has called, and the first session has a date.
On the floor

Real situations: problems and solutions

These are fictional but typical. For each one, cover the answer, decide what you would do, then compare. Where a step depends on local rules, your unit’s procedure and your supervisor’s direction come first.

The patient who arrives with no papers

What you see
A 46-year-old woman arrives at a unit in Tuguegarao City for her first session after discharge from a Manila hospital, carrying only a prescription slip and her PhilHealth ID.
What is likely going on
A transfer without a packet; PDD status, serologies and the prescription are unknown.
What to do
Call the sending hospital’s dialysis unit and attending now, get the serology dates and prescription before connecting her, and record what was missing.
Why it matters
Serology status decides her station; a missing prescription makes the first session a guess.

The acute kidney injury that might recover

What you see
A 35-year-old man on dialysis after leptospirosis in Pampanga has started passing more urine; the family asks for PDD registration so the sessions are covered.
What is likely going on
Acute kidney injury in recovery, not chronic kidney disease stage 5.
What to do
Explain that registration is for chronic stage 5 disease, ask the attending to plan dialysis weaning and follow-up, and refer the family to the billing office and social service for current admission coverage.
Why it matters
A false chronic label is a false record and can delay recognition of recovery.

The December move

What you see
A man moving from Cebu City to Bohol in mid-December has used 141 sessions this year.
What is likely going on
Only 15 sessions remain until the count resets on January 1.
What to do
Write the count in the packet, tell the receiving unit and the family, and make sure the plan for the last weeks of December is discussed before he travels.
Why it matters
Families are surprised by the year-end limit when nobody counted.

PD never mentioned

What you see
A schoolteacher in Masbate, two hours by boat from the nearest HD unit, starts HD in Legazpi City and is told she must move.
What is likely going on
A patient for whom home PD might fit better than relocation, and no one has raised it.
What to do
Ask the attending to assess her for PD, find the nearest contracted PD provider, and explain the PD Z benefit conditions to her and her family.
Why it matters
A benefit that nobody mentions is a benefit that nobody uses.
Field notes
  • Write the date and source of every old creatinine value you find, even if it is a photo on the patient’s phone.
  • Count PhilHealth sessions used this year at every transfer and write the number in the letter.
  • Give the family a printed copy of the transfer packet in a plastic folder; it survives rain and long trips.
  • When a benefit question has no clear answer, refer to the PhilHealth desk instead of guessing an amount.
  • Write a clinical reason beside every change in dialysis frequency.
Case review

Back to the case: Going home to Sorsogon

Papers before Saturday. The attending has documented chronic kidney disease stage 5 with evidence of chronicity: an old creatinine of 4.1 mg/dL, small echogenic kidneys and long-standing diabetes. Andrea makes sure that evidence is written in the chart with dates, starts the PDD registration now with the hospital’s PhilHealth or dialysis staff, and makes sure each of the nine sessions has a signed treatment record. The family signs the HD benefits agreement after it is explained in Tagalog. She counts the sessions claimed this year so the Sorsogon unit knows what remains.

Twice a week. The team thanks Jun for asking openly. The 156 sessions cover three sessions a week all year, so saving tickets is not a reason to cut sessions, and the PHP 6,350 package includes the facility and professional fees. Whether Mr. Dizon could do fewer sessions is a clinical question: he still passes about 500 mL of urine, but he arrived with hyperkalemia and fluid overload, and any change in frequency would be the attending’s decision with close monitoring of potassium, weight and symptoms. Transport cost is a real burden; the team refers the family to the hospital social service and records the concern. PD is also explained as an option for later, once his access and training can be arranged near home.

The packet and the call. The packet carries the diagnosis and evidence of chronicity, PDD status, sessions used, the attending’s current prescription, the catheter details and the plan for a fistula, dated hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV results, recent laboratory results and pending tests, the medicine list with changes, residual urine output, goals of care and the attending’s number. Mr. Dizon carries a copy. The fellow calls the Sorsogon unit’s nephrologist on Friday and confirms the first session for Monday, so no more than one interdialytic interval passes without dialysis.

Self-assessment

Check your understanding

Each visit draws 5 questions at random from this module’s 5-question bank. Choose the single best answer for each, then select Check answers; an explanation appears under the best answer. Select New set of questions for a different mix. A good target is 4 of 5 on two different sets. Nothing you select is sent anywhere; scoring happens in your browser.

Question 1 of 5An intern is writing the progress note of a patient newly started on maintenance hemodialysis. Which entry best supports both the next clinician and the PhilHealth registration?
PDD registration is for chronic kidney disease stage 5, and the next clinician needs the evidence that the disease is chronic. A bare label (the tempting distractor) tells neither of them why.
Question 2 of 5A resident explains the HD benefit to a newly registered patient. Which statement is accurate as of PhilHealth’s 2025 release?
PhilHealth states up to 156 sessions a year at PHP 6,350 per session for PDD-registered patients with chronic kidney disease stage 5, and its guide says unused sessions are not added to the next year. The carry-over option is the most tempting error.
Question 3 of 5A fellow reviews a request to reduce a patient’s HD from three to two sessions a week “so the benefit lasts.” What principle should guide the decision?
PhilHealth itself states that services follow clinical assessment, not demand, and 156 sessions equal three a week for 52 weeks. Following the request without assessment (a tempting distractor) ignores the patient’s safety.
Question 4 of 5A family whose father is on hemodialysis declines a transplant evaluation because they fear losing the dialysis benefit and being billed beyond PhilHealth. Under PhilHealth Circular 2026-0019, what can you accurately tell them?
The circular keeps HD and PD eligibility after graft failure and bars copayment in basic or ward accommodation and balance billing for covered services. Losing the dialysis benefit (the tempting distractor) is a common fear the circular contradicts; confirm the effective date with the liaison.
Question 5 of 5A patient on HD moves from Iloilo City to a unit in Roxas City. Which handoff is most likely to prevent a missed session and an unsafe first treatment?
Discharge information is often missing at the first visit, and structured handoffs are associated with fewer errors. Sending the whole chart to a general address (a tempting distractor) is neither structured nor a safe privacy practice.
