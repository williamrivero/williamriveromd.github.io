## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish diffusion, ultrafiltration and convection.
2. Describe the dialyzer membrane, opposing flows and dialysate, and recognize what needs pre-use verification.
3. Distinguish measured interdialytic gain, target weight, prescribed ultrafiltration volume and ultrafiltration rate.

## Opening case · Which number is the fluid removal goal?

It is Saturday, 06:40, and you are preparing for the first shift. You open the fictional record of Mr. Arnel dela Cruz, 59. His last measured post-dialysis weight was **64.8 kg**. Today his pre-dialysis weight is **67.1 kg**, and the chart lists a current target weight of **65.4 kg**. Nobody has yet confirmed the ultrafiltration goal for today’s session. A coworker, Mr. Jun Navarro, glances at the screen and says, “He gained 2.3 kg. Just enter that for fluid removal.”

- Which two weights show what changed between sessions?
- What is the difference between today’s pre-dialysis weight and the charted target weight?
- What must be verified before either number is used in setup?

## 1. How the dialyzer works

Think of a tea bag in a cup of hot water. The tea moves out through the paper into the water because there is more of it inside than outside, and the leaves stay in the bag because they are too large to pass. A dialyzer works on the same idea, with blood inside the bag and dialysate outside. The picture has a limit. A cup of tea soon reaches the same strength inside and out, and the movement stops. A dialyzer keeps the difference alive by running fresh fluid past the blood the whole time.

Blood moves through hollow fibers inside the dialyzer. Dialysate, also called dialysis fluid, flows around the outside of the fibers in a separate compartment. The fiber walls form a **semipermeable membrane**. In normal use, water and selected solutes cross it, while blood cells and most large proteins stay on the blood side. The two streams usually flow in opposite directions. This **countercurrent flow** keeps a concentration difference along the whole length of the fibers, and that improves exchange. [NIDDK · Hemodialysis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidney-failure/hemodialysis) · [UK Kidney Association · Membrane flux](https://guidelines.ukkidney.org/haemodialysis/rationale-for-clinical-practice-guidelines/membrane-flux-and-haemodiafiltration/)

| Process | What drives it | Dialysis example |
|---|---|---|
| **Diffusion** | A difference in solute concentration across the membrane. Net movement is from higher to lower concentration. | Urea normally moves from the blood into the dialysate. Under typical conditions, bicarbonate can move from the dialysate toward the blood. |
| **Ultrafiltration (UF)** | A pressure difference across the membrane. The machine controls net fluid removal according to the authorized treatment plan. | Water moves out of the blood compartment. The amount removed is tracked as a volume. |
| **Convection** | Moving water carries with it the dissolved substances that can cross the membrane. | Some solutes move with the water during ultrafiltration. Convection also adds to clearance in hemodiafiltration. |

**Worked example.** If the blood side holds more urea than the dialysate side, net urea diffusion is toward the dialysate. If that difference narrows while other conditions stay similar, net urea diffusion slows. Notice what does not change: the prescribed UF goal still describes the fluid volume the treatment is meant to remove. Diffusion and ultrafiltration are separate processes, and one does not set the other. [UK Kidney Association · Membrane flux and convective clearance](https://guidelines.ukkidney.org/haemodialysis/rationale-for-clinical-practice-guidelines/membrane-flux-and-haemodiafiltration/)

## 2. Dialysate and treatment verification

Dialysate is made from treated dialysis water and the prescribed concentrate products. Its electrolytes and buffer set up the gradients that drive exchange, so what is in it decides what moves. Sodium, potassium, calcium, and bicarbonate are examples of constituents that can matter to the prescription. Blood flow, dialysate flow, the membrane, and the treatment time actually delivered all affect how much is cleared. Verify the items assigned to you against the order and the unit procedure. [NIDDK · Hemodialysis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidney-failure/hemodialysis) · [CDC · Water used in dialysis](https://www.cdc.gov/dialysis-safety/hcp/recommendations-resources/water-use-in-dialysis.html)

**Scope in practice.** Your checks and your machine operation follow facility policy, the manufacturer’s instructions, and the responsibilities assigned to you. Confirm the patient record, the authorized prescription, the dialyzer and concentrate labels that apply, and the required machine or dialysate readiness checks. An unexpected conductivity result or a mismatch on a concentrate label must be reviewed before that setup is used. A normal conductivity reading cannot, by itself, confirm that the concentrate matches the patient’s order. Bring any unresolved result to the supervising nurse and the designated technical lead, as local procedure states. [Philippine DOH · Dialysis facility standards](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/50687) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis water monitoring guidelines](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/69036)

## 3. Different numbers answer different fluid questions

Several numbers in a chart are measured in kilograms or liters, and they look alike. Each answers a different question. Mix them up, and a wrong goal can reach the machine looking perfectly reasonable.

| Number | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Interdialytic weight gain (kg)** | Today’s measured pre-dialysis weight minus the previous measured post-dialysis weight. Confirm the dates, the units, and whether each value was taken before or after treatment. |
| **Target weight (kg)** | The post-treatment weight the clinical team aims for, based on its assessment of the patient’s fluid status. The team may revise it as the patient’s condition changes. |
| **Pre-to-target weight difference (kg)** | Today’s pre-dialysis weight minus the documented target weight. It describes the difference between two weights, nothing more. |
| **Prescribed UF volume (mL or L)** | The fluid removal goal authorized for this session. The clinical team may allow for fluids expected to be given or taken during the session and for what the patient is expected to tolerate. Check how your facility defines and records the machine goal and net removal. |
| **UF rate** | The volume removed per unit of time. A machine may display mL/h. A weight-based rate uses mL/kg/h, and it needs the weight basis named in the applicable protocol. |

**Case debrief.**

**Which two weights show what changed?** The previous post-dialysis weight and today’s pre-dialysis weight. The measured gain is **67.1 − 64.8 = 2.3 kg**.

**What is the pre-to-target difference?** **67.1 − 65.4 = 1.7 kg**. Mr. Navarro’s number and this one are both correct arithmetic. Neither is the fluid removal goal.

**What must be verified first?** The authorized goal for today. The case gives no confirmed goal, so hold the affected setup and ask the supervising nurse to verify the current order. A concise handoff: “Today’s pre-weight is 67.1 kg. The prior post-weight is 64.8 kg, and today’s charted target is 65.4 kg. The two differences are 2.3 kg and 1.7 kg. Please confirm the authorized UF goal before setup proceeds.” [UK Kidney Association · Fluid assessment and management](https://guidelines.ukkidney.org/haemodialysis/rationale-for-clinical-practice-guidelines/fluid-in-haemodialysis/)

**Rate practice.** On a separate mock record, an already authorized total UF volume of **1,600 mL** over **4 hours** gives a planned average of **400 mL/h**. This arithmetic describes the example only. If the treatment ends early, the clinical team decides what to do. Removing fluid too fast can contribute to low blood pressure, and symptoms such as new dizziness need prompt escalation under the unit’s procedure. [UK Kidney Association · Fluid assessment and management](https://guidelines.ukkidney.org/haemodialysis/rationale-for-clinical-practice-guidelines/fluid-in-haemodialysis/)

## 4. Urea clearance and adequacy

Urea is a convenient marker of how much cleaning a session achieved. It is easy to measure before and after, and the change tells the team how much was removed. **URR** is the percentage fall in blood urea nitrogen (BUN) from before to after a dialysis session. Use a matched pre and post pair, in the same units.

> **URR (%) = (pre-treatment BUN − post-treatment BUN) ÷ pre-treatment BUN × 100**  
> Mock calculation with pre-BUN **60 mg/dL** and post-BUN **18 mg/dL** gives **70%**.

**Kt/V** expresses urea clearance relative to the estimated space in the body through which urea is spread. **K** stands for effective urea clearance, **t** for treatment time, and **V** for the estimated urea distribution volume. Clinical formulas differ in how they estimate the delivered dose. URR and Kt/V measure urea removal or clearance, and the team reads them alongside other information, such as fluid status, symptoms, and the treatment schedule. [UK Kidney Association · Urea clearance and Kt/V](https://guidelines.ukkidney.org/haemodialysis/appendices/appendix%20a/) · [NIDDK · TiME study protocol on dialysis dose measures](https://repository.niddk.nih.gov/public/study_document/TiME_V1/Documents/TiME_Protocol_V2.20160607_and_Statistical_Analysis_Plan.pdf)

**Why the sampling record matters.** A result can be read correctly only if the samples are matched to the right patient and session and collected with the unit’s timing procedure. A sample taken at the wrong moment gives a real number that answers the wrong question. Within your assigned role, review the sample labels and times, the duration actually delivered, and any interruptions. If a sample is mislabeled or a result is unexpected, flag the discrepancy to the nurse through the approved process. [UK Kidney Association · Dialysis dose](https://guidelines.ukkidney.org/haemodialysis/rationale-for-clinical-practice-guidelines/dialysis-dose-in-thrice-weekly-dialysis-schedules/) · [NIDDK · Sampling and delivered dose in the ATN operations manual](https://repository.niddk.nih.gov/media/studies/atn/Operations_Manual_Ver_4.0.pdf)
