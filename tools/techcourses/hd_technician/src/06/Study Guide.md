# Module 06 · Machines, Dialyzers, and the Extracorporeal Circuit

![A hemodialysis machine stands beside a seated patient speaking with two people.](Opener.png)

**Time** — 6 hours classroom, 8 hours skills lab, 16 hours supervised placement  
**Curriculum alignment** — MEI Core Curriculum for the Dialysis Technician, 7th edition, Module 6  
**Before you start** — Review membrane transport in Module 4 and station turnover in Module 5.

## Learning objectives

1. Trace the blood and dialysate paths and identify the key components in each.
2. Explain what common safeguards monitor and recognize the limits of alarms.
3. Demonstrate assigned preparation and required pre-use checks on a training machine using the applicable instructions for use (IFU) and facility policies.
4. Hold equipment with an unresolved failure, notify the appropriate team members and document the facts.

## Opening case · Failed before the first treatment

It is Friday, 06:40, in the training unit. The first patient is due at 07:00. Machine HD-06 shows a failed required pre-use self-test, and the display names the test that failed. The outside of the machine and the disposables look intact. Your coworker, Mr. Ronnie Castillo, looks at the clock and says, “Just clear the message and run it again. We don’t have time.” Another machine in the unit may be available.

- Is HD-06 ready for use?
- Whom do you tell, and what will they need to know?
- If another machine is available, which preparation and verification steps still apply to it?

## 1. Trace the two circuits

Think of a house with two separate water systems: the clean water that comes in through the tap and the drain water that leaves through the sink. They run side by side through the same walls but never mix. A dialysis machine works with two paths in the same way, one for blood and one for dialysate, and they meet only across the thin wall of the dialyzer membrane. The picture has a limit. In a house, the two systems never exchange anything. In a dialyzer, exchange across that wall is the whole purpose.

**Extracorporeal** means outside the body. The blood path runs from the patient’s vascular access, through the blood tubing and the dialyzer, and back to the patient. A blood pump moves blood through the circuit. The line that draws blood out is often labeled **arterial**, and the line that returns it **venous**. These labels describe the direction of flow in the HD circuit, not the type of vessel. With a fistula or graft, both needles may be placed in the same vascular access. Connecting a patient is beyond the simulation in this module. [NIDDK · Hemodialysis](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidney-failure/hemodialysis) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis facility standards](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/50687)

**Dialysate** is made from treated water and concentrates. It flows through a separate compartment of the dialyzer, outside the hollow fibers that carry the blood. Used dialysate flows on to the machine’s effluent, or drain, pathway. The semipermeable membrane is where exchange takes place. In normal operation, blood and dialysate stay in their own compartments. The fluid pathways and protective components of a particular machine are shown in that model’s IFU. [NIDDK · Dialyzer cross section](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/news/media-library/17962) · [Fresenius Medical Care · Operator’s manuals](https://freseniusmedicalcare.com/en-us/support/product-support-documents/operators-manuals-hemodialysis-hd/)

## 2. Safeguards support direct observation

Think of the smoke detector in a kitchen. It is very good at one thing, smoke at the ceiling, and it says nothing about a gas leak at the floor. Each safeguard on a dialysis machine is like that. It measures one condition at one place in the circuit. Read the displayed message in its context, and do not assume that an alarm proves a single cause, or that silence proves safety. Depending on the machine’s design and the event, some alarms stop the blood pump, close a clamp, or divert the dialysate. Keep the patient, the access site, and the circuit under observation as the facility’s workflow assigns. [Fresenius Medical Care · Operator’s manuals](https://freseniusmedicalcare.com/en-us/support/product-support-documents/operators-manuals-hemodialysis-hd/) · [NNCC · CCHT examination specifications](https://www.nncc-exam.org/certification/ccht)

| Safeguard | What it monitors and what to remember |
|---|---|
| **Blood-side pressure monitors** | Sense pressure at their monitoring points. The patient’s access, kinks or restrictions in the tubing, flow, and other factors can affect the readings. A venous pressure alarm may miss some needle dislodgements, so keep the access and connections visible as directed. |
| **Air or level detector and venous clamp** | Watch for air or a low blood level at the designated point, depending on the design. Correct placement of the tubing or chamber matters. A working detector does not replace a correctly prepared circuit or your own visual checks. |
| **Blood leak detector** | Checks used dialysate for blood that has crossed the dialyzer membrane. It does a different job from watching for external bleeding or disconnected tubing. |
| **Conductivity and temperature monitors** | Monitor important properties of the prepared dialysate. An acceptable conductivity alone cannot confirm the identity of the concentrate, the level of each electrolyte, or the status of any other required check. |
| **UF and fluid balance monitoring** | Supports controlled fluid removal. The authorized prescription and the recorded settings still need to match before a session begins. |

**When an alarm sounds during treatment,** alert the dialysis nurse and follow the unit’s emergency and equipment procedures. Give the exact message and what you can see. Stay within your assigned role. Protective features and alarm limits stay in place while the problem is assessed. [Fresenius Medical Care · Operator’s manuals](https://freseniusmedicalcare.com/en-us/support/product-support-documents/operators-manuals-hemodialysis-hd/) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis facility standards](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/50687)

## 3. Verify readiness using the installed model’s IFU

Practice on an unconnected training machine with your preceptor. The sequence, the supplies, the checks, and the acceptance criteria depend on the machine, its software version, and local procedure. Use the prompts below alongside the approved checklist.

1. **Station and machine.** Verify the machine’s identity, service status, cleanliness, and any required disinfection or rinse status. Confirm through the unit’s process that the required utilities and validated dialysis water are available.
2. **Supplies.** Match the dialyzer, tubing, and concentrate labels against the authorized prescription and the equipment requirements. Check that each package is intact and in date. Use aseptic technique, as applicable, when you open or handle sterile components.
3. **Circuit.** Trace the designated paths. Verify the assigned placement of tubing, pump segments, monitors, detectors, and clamps. Demonstrate the facility-approved priming process in simulation, and verify that the circuit is ready by the model’s own criteria.
4. **Machine checks.** Confirm that the required self-tests completed successfully. Complete the assigned dialysate and equipment checks, including independent verification and residual-disinfectant checks where the applicable procedure requires them. Record the actual results, not “passed.”
5. **Handoff.** Before any patient is connected, confirm all assigned checks and any unresolved issues with the supervising nurse. Ask who has the authority to clear support equipment after a fault or a service intervention.

A successful built-in test confirms only the functions that test covers. It does not confirm the patient’s identity, does not match the disposables to the order, and does not replace any other required check. [Fresenius Medical Care · Operator’s manuals](https://freseniusmedicalcare.com/en-us/support/product-support-documents/operators-manuals-hemodialysis-hd/) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis water monitoring](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/69036)

## 4. Hold, escalate and document an unresolved failure

**Case debrief.**

**Is HD-06 ready for use?** No. A failed required test means the machine has not shown that it is safe. Keep it unavailable for patient use and apply the facility’s hold or out-of-service process. Clearing the display message does not make the machine ready; it only hides the evidence. Perform only the operator checks you are authorized to do under the applicable procedure, and use the facility’s formal clearance process once the problem is resolved.

**Whom do you tell, and what do they need?** The supervising or charge nurse, and the designated equipment or biomedical team. Give them the machine identifier, the time, the name of the test, the exact message, whether a patient was connected, and what you did. Example handoff: “Machine HD-06 failed its required pre-use test at 06:40. The exact display message is logged. No patient was connected. I have placed it on hold and contacted the equipment lead. Please confirm the treatment assignment.”

**What applies to a replacement machine?** Everything. If a replacement is assigned, verify its identity and complete that machine’s own required preparation, checks, and documentation before connection. Mr. Castillo’s worry about time is real, and the nurse can rearrange the schedule. Nobody can undo a treatment started on an unsafe machine.
