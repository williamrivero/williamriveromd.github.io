# Module 10 · Complications and Emergency Response

![A dialysis team reviews an emergency drill scenario at an unoccupied treatment station.](Opener.png)

**Time** — 6 hours classroom, 6 hours skills lab, 16 hours supervised placement  
**Curriculum alignment** — MEI Core Curriculum for the Dialysis Technician, 7th edition, Modules 10 and 12  
**Before you start** — Review equipment alarms, water exceptions, vascular access and session monitoring in Modules 6–9.

## Learning objectives

1. Recognize the common complications of dialysis, and pick out the findings that need emergency escalation at once.
2. Call for help and give a short report of the patient’s condition, the timing, and what you see at the access and the equipment.
3. Carry out assigned support tasks during a simulation, then record and debrief the event.
4. Recognize a utility or facility disruption, and follow the pathway of hold, communication, and authorized release.

## Opening case · Sudden chest pain at the station

It is a fictional Thursday morning. Mr. Danilo Cruz, 58, is in the second hour of his session at Station 4. He turns to you and says, “My chest hurts and I can’t catch my breath.” The symptoms began moments ago. He can still speak, but he looks distressed. No machine alarm has sounded. Two team members are in the treatment area.

- What do you say and do in the first seconds?
- What will you tell the nurse who responds?
- Which tasks can you take on if assigned, and who leads the clinical response?

## 1. Recognize changes that matter

A warning light on a jeepney’s dashboard can mean a loose wire or a failing engine. The light is the same, and the driver cannot tell which by looking at it. The driver’s job is to pull over and get the mechanic. A patient’s symptoms work in a similar way. The picture has a limit. A parked jeepney can wait for the mechanic. A patient sometimes cannot, so when an emergency is apparent, you call for help before you gather anything else.

Different complications can produce the same symptoms. Use the symptoms, the timing, any change you see in the circuit, and the measurements you have to make a factual report. The clinical team works out the cause and directs treatment. Follow the unit’s procedure for activating an emergency response. When an emergency is apparent, call for help before waiting for more readings or charting. [SA Health · Clinical guideline for hemodialysis complications](https://www.sahealth.sa.gov.au/wps/wcm/connect/32dff291-573a-4d24-b3b7-afb5b47c4c00/Management%2Bof%2BComplications%2Bduring%2BHaemodialysis%2BCG%2BV1.pdf?MOD=AJPERES) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis facility standards](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/50687)

| Finding or suspected complication | What to notice and report |
|---|---|
| **Falling blood pressure or possible intradialytic hypotension** | Report promptly any new dizziness, nausea, sweating, weakness, or change in alertness, with any blood pressure trend you have. Escalate at once for severe symptoms, collapse, or a rapid decline. |
| **Muscle cramps** | Report when they began, where they are, how severe they are, any other symptoms, and the readings you have. The nurse reviews the treatment and decides the response. |
| **Chest pain or breathlessness** | Call for help at once. Give the time the symptoms began and how the patient is now. A silent machine and an earlier normal reading do not settle the concern. |
| **Fever, chills or suspected infection** | Tell the nurse promptly. Report the timing, any temperature reading, symptoms at the access, and whether other patients have the same complaints. Use the emergency pathway if the patient is very ill or getting worse. |
| **Access hemorrhage, disconnected blood line or venous needle dislodgement** | Treat visible dislodgement or heavy bleeding as an emergency, now. Say where it is and what you see. An alarm may miss some dislodgements. |
| **Possible hemolysis or another circuit or dialysate emergency** | Alert the team at once for a worrying combination, such as sudden chest or back pain, breathlessness, nausea, or a new and unusual look to the blood line. Report any shared water alert, or the same symptoms at more than one station. |
| **Possible air embolism or other sudden cardiopulmonary event** | Treat sudden severe breathlessness, chest pain, a change in consciousness, or a visible air or line concern as an emergency. Report exactly what you saw in the patient and in the circuit. |
| **Possible severe allergic reaction** | Sudden wheeze, trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, hives over much of the body, or collapse needs escalation at once. Report the timing and any recent exposure you know about. |

What the machine does, and what is decided about the blood circuit, differ by event and by equipment. The authorized team follows the facility emergency procedure and the machine’s instructions for use. Carry out only the actions assigned to you and for which you are trained. [UK Kidney Association · Hemodialysis clinical practice guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6798406/) · [Fresenius Medical Care · Model-specific operator manuals](https://freseniusmedicalcare.com/en-us/support/product-support-documents/operators-manuals-hemodialysis-hd/)

## 2. First response and team roles

![Role card showing how to summon help, give the station and patient status, support the assigned team role, and hand off for documentation.](Teaching%20Diagram.png)

*Figure 1. Emergency role card. The sequence below provides a text equivalent. Learn your unit’s activation method and assigned role before each placement.*

1. **Call for help at once for serious signs.** Say where you are, what you saw, and that help is needed now. Use the unit’s emergency call process, and follow your assignment for calling emergency medical services.
2. **Give a short handoff.** Give the patient’s location, symptoms, time of onset, whether the patient is connected, and any findings you already have. Mention any visible change at the access or circuit, and quote the machine or water messages if you know them. Collecting measurements must never delay the emergency call.
3. **Stay available for your assigned task.** The team lead directs assessment, changes to treatment, decisions about the circuit, use of emergency equipment, and coordination with outside responders. Within your current training and assignment, you may call for help, bring equipment, guide responders, pass on observations, or assist in resuscitation. If cardiac arrest is suspected, activate the response and follow your current BLS training and the unit protocol.
4. **Close the loop.** Repeat back the task you were given, and report when it is done or why it cannot be done. This lets the team lead keep track of what has happened and what still needs action. [AHRQ · TeamSTEPPS communication tools](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/resources/modules/index.html) · [AHA · Adult BLS algorithm for healthcare professionals](https://cpr.heart.org/-/media/CPR-Files/CPR-Guidelines-Files/2025-Algorithms/Algorithm-BLS-Adult-Healthcare-250701.pdf)

**Case debrief.** *The first seconds.* Call for clinical help to the station at once, and activate the unit response as required. Do not wait for an alarm or a blood pressure reading first. A silent machine tells you nothing about Mr. Cruz’s heart or lungs.

*What to tell the nurse.* A short report is enough: “At Station 4, Mr. Cruz suddenly reports chest pain and difficulty breathing. The symptoms started just now. He is connected and speaking but looks distressed. I haven’t seen an alarm. Please come now.” Notice what it contains: where, who, what, when, connection status, and a clear request.

*Who leads.* The team lead directs the response. Follow the instructions you are given and pass on any change you see. The clinical team determines the cause and the treatment.

## 3. Document and debrief

During the response, record only what you have been assigned to record. Immediate actions come first. Write down the symptoms you observed, the time they began or were found, the patient’s condition and connection status, any measurements with their times, machine messages, notifications, what you did within your role, and the next handoff. Keep equipment information, logs, or supplies as the unit directs. Follow the facility process for incident reporting and equipment review. [Philippine DOH · Dialysis facility standards](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/50687)

**Example record.** “10:24 · At Station 4, patient said, ‘My chest hurts and I can’t catch my breath.’ Patient connected and speaking. No alarm was observed at that time. Called emergency response and notified charge nurse at 10:24. Remained available for assigned support. At 10:25, directed responders to Station 4.”

A debrief looks back at how the event was noticed, how quickly help was called, whether everyone understood their role, and what would make the next response better. Record one action, the team member who owns it, and how the team will check that it was done. Use approved de-identified data in training.

## 4. Respond to utility or facility disruptions

A patient emergency usually involves one station. A shared failure can involve many stations at the same moment, because they share the same water, power, and building. Report promptly when several machines alarm together, a water check fails, power is interrupted, or an evacuation instruction is given. Learn the incident lead, the contact numbers, the contingency plans, and who has release authority at your site. [CDC · Water use in dialysis](https://www.cdc.gov/dialysis-safety/hcp/recommendations-resources/water-use-in-dialysis.html) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis water monitoring](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/69036)

| Disruption | What the technician should support |
|---|---|
| **Water outage, water safety alert or suspected contamination** | Hold the affected new starts, and alert the charge nurse and the water-system lead. Report whether any patients are already connected and which stations may be affected. Connected patients need immediate coordination through the unit’s clinical contingency process. |
| **Power failure or unstable backup power** | Report the areas affected and what you see on the machines. Hold the affected new starts, and follow the site emergency plan under the incident lead. Backup support and alarms differ by equipment and by facility. |
| **Fire, earthquake, flood or evacuation** | Use the facility alert and evacuation procedure, name the affected area, and guide responders as directed. The incident lead decides the order in which patients are moved, how they are relocated, and where they are transferred. |
| **Proposed return to service** | Confirm that the designated leads have cleared the equipment, utilities, and patient assignments under unit policy. A screen that is back on, or a utility that has returned, does not by itself mean the unit is ready. Record the authorized release and who gave it. |

**Tabletop variation.** One patient is connected. Water alarms appear on the neighboring machines, and the next shift is getting ready to start. Give the charge nurse and the designated technical lead the location, the number of connected patients if you know it, the exact alert, and what is on hold. Then name who directs care for the connected patients and who records the clearance of the water and equipment. [CDC · Emergency water supply planning guide](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/media/pdfs/2024/07/emergency-water-supply-planning-guide-2019-508.pdf)
