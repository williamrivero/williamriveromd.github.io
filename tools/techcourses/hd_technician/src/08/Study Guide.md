# Module 08 · Vascular Access Recognition and Protection

![A patient rests an arm on a cushion while speaking with a dialysis nurse.](Opener.png)

**Time** — 6 hours classroom, 6 hours skills lab, 14 hours supervised placement  
**Curriculum alignment** — MEI Core Curriculum for the Dialysis Technician, 7th edition, Module 7  
**Before you start** — Review infection prevention in Module 5 and the blood circuit in Module 6.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish an arteriovenous (AV) fistula, an AV graft and a central venous catheter (CVC).
2. Explain everyday measures that protect the access and reduce infection risk.
3. Recognize changes in flow, infection indicators, bleeding and other warning findings.
4. Escalate and report objective findings promptly and perform observations only as authorized.

## Opening case · The usual vibration is gone

Before a scheduled session, Ms. Lim says, “My fistula normally buzzes when I check it. I couldn’t feel it this morning.” She last noticed her usual vibration the evening before. The treatment has not started. There is no visible bleeding, and the patient asks if the team can proceed because she can only stay for her usual appointment time.

- What is the safest next action? Who needs to know?
- Which facts should be in your handoff?
- Does the absence of visible bleeding establish readiness?

## 1. Identify the access type

Vascular access connects the patient’s circulation to the extracorporeal blood circuit for hemodialysis. The clinical team chooses the access based on the patient’s kidney care plan, anatomy, expected treatment course and preferences. The access type is documented in the chart. Confirm the assigned access and refer uncertainties to the nurse. [KDOQI · Vascular access guideline, 2019 update](https://www.kidney.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/2019-kdoqi-guideline-vascular-access.pdf) · [NIDDK · Hemodialysis and vascular access](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidney-failure/hemodialysis)

![Three labeled schematics distinguish a direct artery-to-vein fistula, a graft joining an artery and vein, and a catheter in a central vein.](Teaching%20Diagram.png)

*Figure 1. Access types, simplified for recognition. The table provides a text equivalent.*

| Access | What it is | What to keep in mind |
|---|---|---|
| **AV fistula** | A surgically created direct connection between an artery and a vein, commonly in an arm. Increased flow helps the vein develop for dialysis use. | The team evaluates readiness. A usual vibration may be felt from blood flow. A change from the patient’s baseline needs review. |
| **AV graft** | An implanted conduit connects an artery and vein, creating a path for dialysis access. | A graft has a different structure from a fistula. It can develop narrowing, clotting or infection. |
| **CVC** | A dialysis catheter lies in a central vein, usually with an external segment and separate lumens for blood withdrawal and return. | Protect the dressing and report damage or displacement. Catheters generally carry greater bloodstream infection risk than fistulas or grafts. |

A **thrill** is a vibration caused by blood flow that can be felt over an AV access. A **bruit** is the sound of flow heard with a stethoscope. The clinical team interprets the character of each sign and any change from baseline. A trainee may observe the nurse’s assessment or practice recognizing simulated findings on a model. Perform observations only if authorized and supervised. [KDOQI · Clinical indicators of access dysfunction](https://www.kidney.org/sites/default/files/vait-10_post-operative_av_access_monitoring_and_management.pdf) · [CDC · Infections and patients on dialysis](https://www.cdc.gov/dialysis-safety/about/index.html)

## 2. Protect the access during routine care

- **AV fistula or graft.** Check the documented access side before taking blood pressure or arranging routine blood collection. Protect that limb from blood pressure cuffs, routine venipuncture and constriction under the access care plan. Help the patient avoid pressure from tight items, equipment or positioning. Promptly relay reports of a changed vibration, redness, swelling or bleeding.
- **CVC.** Keep the dressing and external catheter protected from moisture, pulling and damage. Promptly report a wet, loose or soiled dressing, a missing cap or a change in external catheter position. Refer catheter handling and dressing care to qualified staff.
- **All access types.** Follow hand hygiene and the facility’s infection prevention policy. Reinforce approved patient education, including whom to contact for changes away from the unit. Keep the access and connections visible during treatment as directed and report any safety concern even if the machine is quiet.

Patients may receive individualized instructions for daily access checks. Use approved materials and teach-back to reinforce their plan. [NIDDK · Caring for vascular access](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidney-failure/hemodialysis) · [CDC · Preventing dialysis bloodstream infections](https://www.cdc.gov/dialysis-safety/hcp/clinical-safety/)

## 3. Recognize a change and escalate it

| Finding | What it could mean | Technician response |
|---|---|---|
| Usual thrill newly absent or markedly changed | A possible problem with access flow, such as narrowing or clotting, requires clinical assessment. | Keep the affected access out of use and notify the nurse promptly. Report when the usual finding was last noticed. |
| New swelling of the access limb or prolonged bleeding after treatment | A possible access complication. The clinical team considers the pattern and other findings. | Notify the nurse and describe the side, location, timing, progression and associated symptoms. Escalate ongoing heavy bleeding immediately. |
| New redness, warmth, tenderness, swelling or drainage at the access | Possible infection or another local complication. Fever or chills can accompany infection. | Alert the nurse promptly. Summon help immediately if symptoms or vital signs suggest clinical deterioration under the unit’s protocol. |
| New pain, swelling or bruising near a needle during dialysis | A possible infiltration or other access problem warrants immediate nurse assessment. | Alert the nurse promptly, keep the area visible and follow assigned instructions. |
| A newly cold, pale, painful, numb or weak hand on the access side | A possible circulation problem needs prompt clinical review. | Alert the nurse and report onset and symptoms. Activate the unit emergency process if directed or required by protocol. |
| Active heavy bleeding, visible dislodgement or an open blood connection during treatment | An immediate safety emergency. An alarm may fail to identify some dislodgements. | Summon immediate help and follow the unit’s emergency procedure within your authorized role. |

Report what you see, what the patient says and how it differs from baseline. The clinical team assesses causes and decides whether an access can be used. Initial education covers recognition and communication. Patient access procedures require specific authorization and validated competency. [KDOQI · Vascular access guideline](https://www.kidney.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/2019-kdoqi-guideline-vascular-access.pdf) · [PSN · Hemodialysis clinic guidelines](https://psn.org.ph/2024/03/25/hd-guidelines-3rd-edition-2024/) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis facility standards](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/50687)

## 4. Work through the case

**Case debrief.** Pause use of the affected access and notify the supervising nurse before treatment. The patient reports a new change from her usual baseline. The absence of visible bleeding leaves the flow concern unresolved. The nurse and clinical team assess the access and confirm the next step.

**Example handoff.** “Before treatment, Ms. Lim reported that she could no longer feel her usual fistula vibration this morning. She last noticed it yesterday evening. I saw no external bleeding. Treatment has not started, and I have kept the access out of use. Please assess it and advise on the plan.” If you did not perform an authorized access observation, distinguish the patient’s report from anything you personally observed. Document the time, relevant facts, who was notified and the confirmed instructions.

**Teach-back prompt.** “If the usual vibration changes when you check it at home, how will you contact the team?” If the answer is unclear, restate the approved contact instructions and ask again. [AHRQ · Teach-back](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html)
