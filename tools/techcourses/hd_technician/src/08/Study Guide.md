# Module 08 · Vascular Access Recognition and Protection

![A patient rests an arm on a cushion while speaking with a dialysis nurse.](Opener.png)

**Time** — 6 hours classroom, 6 hours skills lab, 14 hours supervised placement  
**Curriculum alignment** — MEI Core Curriculum for the Dialysis Technician, 7th edition, Module 7  
**Before you start** — Review infection prevention in Module 5 and the blood circuit in Module 6.

## Learning objectives

1. Tell an arteriovenous (AV) fistula, an AV graft, and a central venous catheter (CVC) apart.
2. Explain the everyday measures that protect an access and lower the risk of infection.
3. Recognize a change in flow, signs of infection, bleeding, and other warning findings.
4. Escalate and report what you observe promptly, and make observations only as you are authorized.

## Opening case · The usual vibration is gone

It is Wednesday morning. Ms. Carmela Lim, 52, has just arrived for her scheduled session and is waiting beside the scale. Before you begin anything, she tells you, “My fistula normally buzzes when I check it. I couldn’t feel it this morning.” The last time she noticed her usual vibration was the evening before. Treatment has not started. You see no bleeding. She asks whether the team can go ahead anyway, because she can stay only for her usual appointment time.

- What is the safest next step, and who needs to know now?
- Does the absence of visible bleeding mean the access is ready to use?
- Which facts belong in your handoff?

## 1. Identify the access type

Picture a garden hose fitted straight onto a strong faucet. Lay your hand on the hose and you feel the water rushing through it. If the hose kinks or something blocks it, the feel changes before you see any sign on the outside. A fistula or graft behaves in a similar way. The picture has a limit. A hose is the same every day, and an access is not. What matters is a change from this patient’s own usual, and it is the clinical team that judges what the change means.

Vascular access joins the patient’s circulation to the blood circuit outside the body for hemodialysis. The clinical team chooses the access from the patient’s kidney care plan, anatomy, expected course of treatment, and preferences. The access type is written in the chart. Confirm the assigned access, and take any uncertainty to the nurse. [KDOQI · Vascular access guideline, 2019 update](https://www.kidney.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/2019-kdoqi-guideline-vascular-access.pdf) · [NIDDK · Hemodialysis and vascular access](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidney-failure/hemodialysis)

![Three labeled schematics distinguish a direct artery-to-vein fistula, a graft joining an artery and vein, and a catheter in a central vein.](Teaching%20Diagram.png)

*Figure 1. Access types, simplified for recognition. The table provides a text equivalent.*

| Access | What it is | What to keep in mind |
|---|---|---|
| **AV fistula** | A surgeon joins an artery directly to a vein, usually in an arm. The extra flow makes the vein grow until it can be used for dialysis. | The team decides when it is ready. You may feel a usual vibration from the flow. Any change from the patient’s baseline needs review. |
| **AV graft** | An implanted tube joins an artery and a vein and gives a path for dialysis access. | A graft is built differently from a fistula. It can narrow, clot, or become infected. |
| **CVC** | A dialysis catheter sits in a central vein. Usually part of it lies outside the body, with separate channels for drawing blood out and returning it. | Protect the dressing, and report any damage or movement. Catheters generally carry a higher risk of bloodstream infection than fistulas or grafts. |

A **thrill** is the vibration of blood flow that can be felt over an AV access. A **bruit** is the sound of that flow heard through a stethoscope. The clinical team interprets the character of each sign and any change from the patient’s baseline. As a trainee, you may watch the nurse’s assessment or practice recognizing simulated findings on a model. Make observations only when you are authorized and supervised. [KDOQI · Clinical indicators of access dysfunction](https://www.kidney.org/sites/default/files/vait-10_post-operative_av_access_monitoring_and_management.pdf) · [CDC · Infections and patients on dialysis](https://www.cdc.gov/dialysis-safety/about/index.html)

## 2. Protect the access during routine care

An access stays open while blood keeps moving through it, and stays clean while its skin stays intact. Most protective habits follow from those two points.

- **AV fistula or graft.** Check which side the access is on in the record before anyone takes a blood pressure or plans a routine blood draw. Under the access care plan, keep that limb free of blood pressure cuffs, routine needle sticks, and anything that squeezes it. Help the patient avoid pressure from tight items, equipment, or the way they sit or lie. Pass on at once any report of a changed vibration, redness, swelling, or bleeding.
- **CVC.** Keep the dressing and the outside part of the catheter safe from moisture, pulling, and damage. Report promptly a wet, loose, or soiled dressing, a missing cap, or a change in where the catheter sits. Catheter handling and dressing care belong to qualified staff.
- **All access types.** Follow hand hygiene and the facility’s infection prevention policy. Reinforce the approved patient teaching, including whom to call about a change at home. Keep the access and connections in view during treatment as directed, and report any safety concern even when the machine is silent.

Patients may be given their own instructions for checking the access every day. Use the approved materials, and use teach-back to confirm that the patient understands their plan. [NIDDK · Caring for vascular access](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidney-failure/hemodialysis) · [CDC · Preventing dialysis bloodstream infections](https://www.cdc.gov/dialysis-safety/hcp/clinical-safety/)

## 3. Recognize a change and escalate it

Your work is not to name the problem. It is to notice the change, describe it exactly, and get it to the nurse before the access is used.

| Finding | What it could mean | Technician response |
|---|---|---|
| Usual thrill newly absent or markedly changed | A possible problem with flow through the access, such as narrowing or clotting. It needs clinical assessment. | Keep the access out of use and tell the nurse promptly. Report when the usual finding was last noticed. |
| New swelling of the access limb or prolonged bleeding after treatment | A possible access complication. The clinical team weighs the pattern with the other findings. | Tell the nurse the side, location, timing, progression, and any symptoms that came with it. Escalate ongoing heavy bleeding at once. |
| New redness, warmth, tenderness, swelling or drainage at the access | Possible infection or another local complication. Fever or chills can come with infection. | Alert the nurse promptly. Call for help at once if symptoms or vital signs suggest the patient is getting worse, as the unit’s protocol directs. |
| New pain, swelling or bruising near a needle during dialysis | A possible infiltration or another access problem, which needs the nurse’s assessment now. | Alert the nurse promptly, keep the area in view, and follow the instructions you are given. |
| A newly cold, pale, painful, numb or weak hand on the access side | A possible problem with blood supply to the hand, which needs prompt clinical review. | Alert the nurse and report when it began and what the patient feels. Activate the unit emergency process if you are directed to or if protocol requires it. |
| Active heavy bleeding, visible dislodgement or an open blood connection during treatment | An immediate emergency. The machine may not alarm for some dislodgements. | Call for help at once and follow the unit’s emergency procedure within your authorized role. |

Report what you saw, what the patient said, and how both differ from baseline. The clinical team works out the cause and decides whether the access can be used. This module teaches recognition and communication. Procedures on a patient’s access need specific authorization and validated competency. [KDOQI · Vascular access guideline](https://www.kidney.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/2019-kdoqi-guideline-vascular-access.pdf) · [PSN · Hemodialysis clinic guidelines](https://psn.org.ph/2024/03/25/hd-guidelines-3rd-edition-2024/) · [Philippine DOH · Dialysis facility standards](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/10/50687)

## 4. Work through the case

**Case debrief.** *The safest next step.* Keep the access out of use and tell the supervising nurse before any treatment begins. Ms. Lim is describing a new change from her own baseline, and that is a finding in itself.

*Does no bleeding mean ready?* No. The absence of visible bleeding answers a different question and leaves the concern about flow open. The nurse and the clinical team assess the access and decide the next step. Her wish to finish within her usual time is real, and you can tell her that you have asked the nurse to see her now.

**Example handoff.** “Before treatment, Ms. Lim told me she could no longer feel her usual fistula vibration this morning. She last noticed it yesterday evening. I saw no external bleeding. Treatment has not started, and I have kept the access out of use. Please assess it and advise on the plan.” If you did not make an authorized access observation yourself, keep what the patient reported separate from anything you observed. Record the time, the relevant facts, whom you told, and the instructions you received.

**Teach-back prompt.** “If the usual vibration changes when you check it at home, how will you contact the team?” If her answer is unclear, repeat the approved contact instructions in plain words and ask again. [AHRQ · Teach-back](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html)
