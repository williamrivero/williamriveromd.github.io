# 05. Infection prevention and surveillance

**Core** · 45 minutes study · 45 minutes supervised practice · Source review date: November 24, 2026
**Figures:** FIG-05 (Understand) · FIG-05B (Apply) · FIG-05C (Appreciate) · **Calculator:** CALC-03 Infection surveillance rates

> **Why this matters.** Hemodialysis deliberately breaks the skin barrier two or three times a week, connects the bloodstream to an external circuit and brings many patients into one room with shared surfaces. Infection is the second leading cause of death in dialysis patients after cardiovascular disease. Most dialysis infections are preventable, and prevention is mostly a management system: supplies in the right place, audits that happen, results that someone reads.

## Learning objectives

1. Trace the pathways by which bacteria and bloodborne viruses reach dialysis patients.
2. Implement and audit the CDC Core Interventions for bloodstream infection (BSI) prevention.
3. Calculate BSI rates per 100 patient-months and hand-hygiene compliance, and interpret a change.
4. Organize serologic screening, vaccination and isolation so that results are acted on, not just filed.

## The mechanism: how infections reach the patient

**Bacteria through the access.** Skin organisms, especially *Staphylococcus aureus*, enter at the needle site or the catheter hub. A central venous catheter (CVC) is a plastic highway into the bloodstream: its hub is touched at every connection, and biofilm forms on its inner surface within days. That is why catheter-dependent patients have far higher BSI rates than patients with an arteriovenous fistula.

**Bloodborne viruses through the environment.** Hepatitis B virus (HBV) survives on surfaces for at least seven days and is transmitted by invisible amounts of blood. Contaminated gloves, shared multidose vials, clamps, tourniquets and machine surfaces can carry it from one station to the next. Hepatitis C virus (HCV) spreads the same way, though less efficiently.

**The lesson from outbreaks.** CDC investigations of HBV outbreaks in five US dialysis centers in 1994 found the same failures again and again: infected patients not identified or not isolated, staff and supplies shared between infected and susceptible patients, monthly screening results that nobody reviewed, and low vaccination rates (*MMWR* 1996). In one center, a shared heparin vial was the probable route. None of those failures was a lack of medical knowledge. Every one was a system failure a manager can prevent.

## What the evidence shows

- **The Core Interventions work.** In the CDC Dialysis BSI Prevention Collaborative, 17 outpatient facilities that implemented the bundle saw overall BSIs fall by 32% and access-related BSIs fall by 54% (Patel et al., *AJKD* 2013), and the fall in access-related BSIs persisted for up to four years, although overall BSI rates drifted back toward baseline (Yi et al., *Infection Control and Hospital Epidemiology* 2016). Gains fade when audits and feedback stop. This was a before-and-after design without a control group, so it **supports** rather than proves benefit. National US surveillance then showed the pooled BSI standardized infection ratio falling by 40% between 2014 and 2019 as the practices spread (CDC NHSN).
- **Isolation of HBV-positive patients is established practice.** Since CDC recommendations were introduced in 1977, HBV incidence among US dialysis patients and staff fell sharply (CDC, *MMWR Recommendations and Reports* 2001). Current standards call for HBsAg-positive patients to dialyze in a separate room or area with dedicated machines, equipment, supplies and staff.
- **HCV is managed differently.** KDIGO's guideline on HCV in CKD (2018) emphasizes rigorous standard precautions and regular audits rather than routine isolation or dedicated machines for HCV. Your unit policy, the PSN guidelines and DOH requirements decide local practice. Know which one your unit follows and why.

## The CDC Core Interventions (management view)

| Intervention | What you, as manager, make happen |
|---|---|
| Monthly BSI surveillance with feedback | A named person counts events and reports the rate at the staff meeting |
| Hand-hygiene observations with feedback | Monthly audits, results posted by shift |
| Catheter and vascular access care observations | Quarterly observation of connection, disconnection and dressing change |
| Staff competency in infection control | Training at hire and annually; competency on the matrix (Module 03) |
| Patient education | Access care, hand hygiene, signs of infection |
| Catheter reduction | Track catheter prevalence; push referrals for fistula creation |
| Chlorhexidine skin antisepsis | Stock and use as the standard for catheter care |
| Catheter hub disinfection | Standardized scrub technique with the approved agent |
| Antimicrobial ointment at the catheter exit site | Where policy and product availability support it |

## Calculator CALC-03: infection surveillance rates

**Inputs:** BSI events in the month (positive blood cultures meeting the surveillance definition), patient-months, hand-hygiene opportunities observed and opportunities with correct hand hygiene.

**Formulas**

- Patient-months: following the NHSN convention, the number of maintenance HD patients treated in the unit on the first two working days of the month
- BSI rate = BSI events ÷ patient-months × 100 (reported per 100 patient-months)
- Access-related BSI rate: same formula using only access-related events
- Hand-hygiene compliance (%) = compliant opportunities ÷ observed opportunities × 100

**Worked example.** Eighty patients counted, two BSIs, one access-related: overall rate 2.5 per 100 patient-months; access-related rate 1.25. With 120 observed opportunities and 96 compliant, hand hygiene is 80%.

**Interpretation.** A single month means little in a small unit; one event can double the rate. Plot the monthly rate on a run chart (Module 09) and watch the trend, and break rates down by access type. Most of the excess will come from catheter patients.

## Serology, vaccination and isolation: the result must trigger an action

1. **Screening schedule.** Follow the Medical Director-approved schedule for HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HCV (and other tests per policy) at admission and at set intervals.
2. **Result review.** Name the reviewer and set a deadline (for example, within 48 hours of receipt). The 1994 outbreaks show that unread results protect no one.
3. **Action.** A new positive result triggers isolation decisions, notification of the Medical Director and contact review the same day.
4. **Vaccination.** Track HBV vaccination series and anti-HBs response. Non-responders need a documented plan.
5. **Transient patients.** Visiting patients need current serology before their first session.

## Field case: the unread result

**Situation.** A patient's monthly HBsAg comes back positive. It was previously negative. The report sat in an inbox for nine days while the patient dialyzed at a general station on a shared machine.

**What went wrong.** No named reviewer, no deadline, no escalation.

**What a good manager does.** Triggers isolation immediately with the Medical Director, starts a contact review of patients who shared the shift and machine, arranges repeat testing of susceptible contacts per the physician's plan, reports as required, and then fixes the system: a named reviewer, a 48-hour rule and a log showing date received, date reviewed and action. The CAPA from Module 02 documents it all.

## Common failure modes

- Audits done but never fed back to staff.
- Hand-rub dispensers empty, placed far from stations or not at the point of care.
- Clean and dirty supply areas next to each other.
- Multidose vials carried between stations.
- Catheter prevalence accepted as "just how our patients are."
